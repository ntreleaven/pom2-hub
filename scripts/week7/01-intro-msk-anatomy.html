Week 7 — Introduction to MSK Anatomy

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objectives

This module is a reference review before we get into the MSK block proper. By the end I should be able to:
- Use correct anatomical terminology for the musculoskeletal system — anatomical position, supine vs. prone, and regional terms like axilla, cubital fossa, popliteal fossa, and inguinal region.
- Describe the terms of movement: flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, circumduction, protraction/retraction, elevation/depression, pronation/supination, and dorsiflexion/plantarflexion.
- Walk through the somatic nervous system pathway — the generic spinal nerve, the reflex arc, and how dermatomes and myotomes arise developmentally.
- Describe the four types of muscle contraction (isometric, and the two isotonic types — concentric and eccentric) and open vs. closed kinetic chains.
- Describe synovial joint characteristics and the "laws of joints," including Hilton's Law.
- Distinguish the axial from the appendicular skeleton and know the general bony terminology (process, tuberosity, condyle, foramen, fossa, etc.).

High-Yield Content

Why this module exists: most of this is review from first year, but it's the shared vocabulary for everything else in the MSK block — back pain, shoulder pain, radiology — so it's worth being fluent in it rather than just recognizing it.

Anatomical position and movement. Anatomical position is standing, palms forward. Supine is face-up, prone is face-down. The movement vocabulary is mostly intuitive once you've seen it once: protraction/retraction are the scapula sliding forward/back, elevation/depression are up/down, and supination/pronation apply to the forearm (and also get reused for the foot — "supination" and "pronation" at the ankle refer to complex tri-planar motions, distinct from forearm supination/pronation, which is simple rotation).

The somatic nervous system, from first principles. Every spinal nerve carries two general fibre types: general sensory (pain, proprioception, temperature, pressure, touch — travelling from the body wall to the CNS via the dorsal root, with the cell body sitting in the dorsal root ganglion) and general motor (travelling out via the ventral root to skeletal muscle). That's the whole reflex arc in miniature: sensory neuron in via the dorsal root → synapse → motor neuron out via the ventral root.

Where dermatomes and myotomes come from. In development, paired blocks of paraxial mesoderm called somites form alongside the neural tube. Each somite's dermomyotome splits into a dermatome (skin) and myotome (muscle) division, and each somite is supplied by one spinal nerve segment. The twist is that myotomes elongate and fuse to form actual muscles, so by the time you're looking at an adult muscle, its innervation usually spans more than one spinal level — which is exactly why a single nerve root lesion rarely wipes out a whole muscle, it just weakens it.

Muscle contraction types, cleanly separated. Isometric \= tension develops but muscle length doesn't change. Isotonic \= the muscle does change length, and splits into concentric (shortening while contracting — the "lifting" phase) and eccentric (lengthening while contracting — the "lowering" phase, and the one that produces more delayed-onset muscle soreness). Kinetic chains: open means the distal segment is free to move (like a seated leg extension), closed means the distal segment is fixed against a surface (like a squat) — closed-chain exercises are generally considered to load joints more predictably because the forces are more constrained.

Synovial joints. A synovial joint has a fibrous joint capsule lined by synovial membrane, which secretes synovial fluid, with hyaline cartilage capping the bone ends. Clinically important: the cartilage and synovial membrane are poorly innervated, so joint pain is actually coming from the capsule, ligaments, or subchondral bone — not from the cartilage itself. The six classic types to be able to name and give an example of: hinge (elbow), pivot (radioulnar joint), saddle (first CMC), condyloid (MCP joints), plane/gliding (AC joint), and ball-and-socket (glenohumeral, hip). Hilton's Law is worth knowing cold because it comes up again in the shoulder module: the nerve that innervates a muscle acting on a joint also supplies the sensory innervation to that joint and the skin overlying it. Joint stability in general comes down to three things — the shape of the articulating surfaces, the ligaments, and the muscles crossing the joint.

The skeleton, divided. Axial skeleton \= skull, vertebral column, sternum, ribs — the central support. Appendicular skeleton \= the limbs and the girdles that attach them (pectoral girdle: clavicle + scapula; pelvic girdle: the pelvic bones). Worth being able to rattle off the long bones of each limb without hesitating: humerus → radius/ulna → carpals/metacarpals/phalanges for the upper limb, and femur → tibia/fibula → tarsals/metatarsals/phalanges for the lower limb, with the patella as a sesamoid bone sitting in the quadriceps tendon.

Bony terminology, grouped by what it describes (this is the part that's easy to blank on under pressure, so group it conceptually rather than memorizing a flat list):
- Joint surfaces: head (rounded end tapering to a neck), facet (small flat articulating surface), condyle (rounded projection at a distal end).
- Soft-tissue attachment points: process (any bony prominence), tuberosity (large rounded projection), tubercle (small rounded projection), trochanter (large irregular process, femur-specific), epicondyle (raised area around a condyle), crest (a prominent ridge), spine (a pointed ridge).
- Depressions: fossa (a basin-like depression, often an articulation site), groove and sulcus (furrows that house a structure), notch (an edge indentation).
- Openings: foramen (round/oval opening through bone), fissure (narrow slit-like opening), meatus (a canal-like passage).

The practical reason to actually hold onto this vocabulary rather than skimming it: it's exactly the language that's going to show up again, unglossed, in the back-pain and shoulder modules this week — "intervertebral foramen," "superior articular facet," "greater tuberosity" — so getting fluent here saves re-looking-things-up for the rest of the block.
