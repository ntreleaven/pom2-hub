This module's clinical piece is proximal biceps tendinopathy. The other shoulder conditions are in the Muscles of the Shoulder script.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: the arm is the simplest region of the limb, with two compartments, two nerves and four muscles. That makes it a good place to practise the habit that makes anatomy clinically useful: which joints does this muscle cross? That decides what it can do, and the long head of biceps crossing the shoulder is exactly why it gets dragged into shoulder pain.

Objective 1: how the arm is organized

Under the skin is the brachial fascia. Medial and lateral intermuscular septa run from it to the humerus and split the arm into two compartments, each with one job and one nerve:

  • Anterior compartment: flexes the shoulder and elbow and supinates the forearm. Nerve: musculocutaneous.
  • Posterior compartment: extends the shoulder and elbow. Nerve: radial.

Anterior compartment: three muscles, all musculocutaneous

Biceps brachii.

  • Origin: long head from the supraglenoid tubercle, running through the intertubercular groove. Short head from the coracoid process.
  • Insertion: radial tuberosity, plus the bicipital aponeurosis into the forearm fascia.
  • Crosses the shoulder and the elbow.
  • Function: strong supinator (its signature action), flexes the forearm, weak shoulder flexor, and helps resist shoulder dislocation.

Coracobrachialis.

  • Origin: coracoid process. Insertion: middle third of the medial humerus.
  • Crosses only the shoulder: it flexes and adducts the arm and resists dislocation.
  • The musculocutaneous nerve pierces it, which makes it a handy landmark in the lab.

Brachialis.

  • Origin: distal half of the anterior humerus. Insertion: coronoid process of the ulna.
  • Crosses only the elbow. It is the primary flexor of the forearm, more so than biceps.

Posterior compartment: one muscle, radial nerve

Triceps brachii has three heads:

  • Long head: infraglenoid tubercle of the scapula. It crosses the shoulder too, so it extends the shoulder and resists dislocation.
  • Lateral head: proximal third of the posterior humerus.
  • Medial head: distal third of the posterior humerus.

All three insert via a common tendon on the olecranon. Triceps is the primary elbow extensor. The radial nerve and profunda brachii run in the radial groove between the lateral and medial head origins.

Easy pair to confuse: biceps long head = supraglenoid, triceps long head = infraglenoid. Both cross the shoulder.

Objective 2: proximal biceps tendinopathy

Terminology: "tendinopathy", not "tendinitis". It covers a spectrum, from overuse and degeneration (tendinosis) to acute inflammation to partial or complete tears.

Who: anyone.

  • Younger: athletes, especially overhead throwers, and people with inflammatory conditions.
  • Older: overuse and degeneration are very common.

The anatomic basis ("location, location, location"). The long head is vulnerable because:

  • it lives right next to the rotator cuff tendons, so it shares their wear and impingement
  • it weaves through the tight intertubercular groove
  • it's susceptible to impingement
  • it originates on the supraglenoid tubercle and the superior glenoid labrum, so labral problems become biceps problems
  • the proximal tendon is an area of relative hypovascularity, so it heals poorly

Clinical presentation:

  • History: acute onset (tear or inflammation) or chronic onset (degenerative, overuse). Anterior shoulder pain.
  • Physical: tender to palpation in the intertubercular groove. Pain is worse with shoulder flexion and elbow flexion, especially with the forearm supinated (loading exactly what biceps does). A frank tear gives weakness and a visible defect from muscle retraction (the "Popeye" bulge).

Diagnosis: clinical examination is often the most helpful step. Differential:

  • Rotator cuff tendinopathy
  • Subacromial bursitis
  • Labral tear (SLAP lesion): a tear of the superior labrum, front to back
  • Shoulder (glenohumeral) or AC joint arthritis
  • Cervical radiculopathy
  • Referred pain: facetogenic, or visceral pain presenting in the shoulder

Investigations: ultrasound if unclear, which also lets you assess the rotator cuff tendons and the subacromial bursa at the same time. MR arthrogram if you suspect a SLAP lesion.

Management:

  • Education: prognosis depends on the type of injury. The vast majority are chronic and improve with conservative management.
  • Non-pharmacologic: physiotherapy is the mainstay. You can use modalities for acute pain, but active therapy is necessary. Add relative rest from overhead activity.
  • Pharmacologic: analgesia as needed.
  • Interventional: surgery for refractory cases.

Bottom line: in the anterior compartment, biceps supinates, brachialis is the prime elbow flexor, and coracobrachialis only crosses the shoulder (and the musculocutaneous nerve pierces it). The posterior compartment is triceps on the radial nerve. Biceps tendinopathy is anterior shoulder pain, tenderness in the groove, and pain on resisted flexion with supination. Examine first, get an ultrasound if unclear and an MR arthrogram for a suspected SLAP lesion, and treat with active physiotherapy.
