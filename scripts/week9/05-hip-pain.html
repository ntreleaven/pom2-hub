High-Yield Content

Why this matters: "my hip hurts" can mean three different places, and each points to different structures. The first move in every hip pain visit is to clarify where they mean.

First: what does the patient mean by "hip"?

  • "True" (intra-articular) hip pain is usually anterior, in the groin.
  • Patients often point to the lateral buttock or greater trochanter when they say "hip", and sometimes to the buttock itself.
  • So sort the pain into anterior (groin), lateral, and posterior (buttock).

Anterior / groin pain: usually intra-articular

Differential: arthritis (OA, RA, septic), labral tear, avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis), iliopsoas tendinopathy.

Hip osteoarthritis (more detail comes in the rheumatology week):

  • A degenerative "wear and tear" joint disease. Primary OA is idiopathic and seen in older adults. Secondary OA is post-traumatic and seen in younger people.
  • Presentation:
    • anterior hip or groin pain, often the "C sign" (the hand cupped over the lateral hip and groin)
    • gradual or sudden onset, worse with prolonged activity, night pain
    • morning stiffness under 30 minutes, and stiffness after prolonged sitting (car rides, desk jobs)
    • crepitus, locking or sticking
    • reduced ROM (getting in and out of the car, bending, abduction/ER/IR)
    • an antalgic gait
  • Exam:
    • Look for a leg length discrepancy.
    • Move: internal rotation is lost first.
    • Special tests: FABER, Stinchfield, FADDIR.
  • Investigations: it's a clinical diagnosis.
    • X-ray confirms it and can track progression, but radiographic changes don't always match symptoms.
    • CT/MRI isn't needed to diagnose; it's only for secondary causes or surgical planning.
    • The slide's knowledge check: a 65-year-old with 2 years of groin pain asks for an MRI. The answer is start with an X-ray. MRI adds nothing, CT adds radiation, and ultrasound is poor for a deep joint.
  • Treatment:
    • Non-pharmacologic: education, exercise and physiotherapy, functional supports (work modifications, a sit-stand desk), weight management, Tai Chi, a cane.
    • Pharmacologic: oral NSAIDs, acetaminophen, duloxetine, and image-guided intra-articular injection (steroid vs HA vs PRP).
    • Then surgery (replacement) for end-stage disease.

Labral tear:

  • Seen at all ages, commonly in active females.
  • Vague groin pain with clicking, catching, snapping or locking. FABER/FADIR are positive.
  • X-ray to exclude other problems. MR arthrogram is the imaging study of choice.
  • Treat with the general hip pain approach. Surgery is arthroscopic labral debridement or repair.

Osteonecrosis (avascular necrosis):

  • Disruption of blood flow to bone, commonly the femoral head.
  • Causes: trauma, steroids (oral or IM), alcohol, lupus, sickle cell disease, radiation.

Lateral hip pain

Greater trochanteric (GT) bursitis:

  • A very common cause of lateral hip pain, female > male. Chronic friction of the IT band over the greater trochanter irritates the bursa.
  • Clinical diagnosis:
    • pain over the GT with point tenderness (highly sensitive)
    • worse with weight bearing, can't lie on that side
    • may radiate to the buttock or down the thigh to the knee
    • no swelling, redness or skin changes
  • Imaging is rarely needed. Ask about a recent increase in activity. Ober's test is positive.
  • Treatment: physio (stretch the ITB, strengthen the hip abductors), PRICE, NSAIDs, corticosteroid injection.

GT bursitis vs greater trochanteric pain syndrome (GTPS):

  • Friction bursitis is only half the story. Often there is chronic tendinopathy of gluteus medius and minimus, which insert on the GT, with flares of bursitis or tendinitis on top.
  • GTPS looks like GT bursitis but is more chronic and recalcitrant.
  • The single leg stance test is painful, because standing on one leg loads the gluteal abductors.
  • Onset may be insidious, or a sudden flare after unaccustomed exercise, a fall, prolonged standing or sporting overuse.
  • Imaging: X-ray, perhaps US or MRI.
  • Treatment: physio (stretch the ITB, strengthen the abductors), load management, weight management, NSAIDs and injections, adjuncts like shockwave or ultrasound (weak evidence), perhaps surgery.

GTPS vs hip OA: "Can you put your socks on?"

  • Hip OA can also present laterally, and the two are often confused.
  • Putting socks on while seated needs hip flexion and external rotation, basically a FABER. That hurts in OA but is usually fine in GTPS, where hip ROM is relatively pain-free.
  • Putting socks on while standing is a single leg stance, which loads the glutes, so it hurts in GTPS.
  • The slide case: a 71-year-old with lateral hip pain and no trouble with socks while seated. The answer is GTPS.

Posterior hip (buttock) pain

Piriformis syndrome:

  • A muscle strain or tendinopathy with pain in the buttock. It can also impinge the sciatic nerve, giving radicular symptoms down the leg.
  • Causes: trauma to the buttock, prolonged sitting, anatomic anomalies (the sciatic nerve passing through piriformis), perhaps hypertrophy.
  • Clinical: buttock pain worse getting out of bed, with prolonged sitting and with hip movement. Tender over piriformis.
  • Tests (these stretch or load piriformis):
    • Pace: resisted abduction and ER
    • Beatty: side-lying, lift the flexed knee
    • FAIR: flexion, adduction, IR
    • Freiberg: forceful IR of the extended thigh
  • EMG if nerve involvement is suspected.
  • Treatment: physio (stretch piriformis, ROM, deep massage), NSAIDs, US-guided steroid injection, perhaps surgery.

Snapping hip syndrome

  • Very common. If it isn't painful, don't worry about it.
  • If it is painful, think about the structures in that area:
    • deep: labral tear, loose body
    • anterior: iliopsoas over a bony prominence
    • lateral: ITB or gluteus maximus over the GT
    • posterior: hamstrings over the ischial tuberosity
  • Treat the structure: muscle problems are stretched and strengthened; bone and ligament problems are strengthened, perhaps injected, perhaps operated on.
  • The slide says not to memorize the list; it's for understanding.

Bottom line:

  • First clarify where "hip" means. Groin pain is intra-articular until proven otherwise.
  • Hip OA: groin pain, C sign, IR lost first, positive FABER. It's a clinical diagnosis confirmed by X-ray, not MRI. Treat with exercise, weight loss and a cane before injections or replacement.
  • Labral tear: clicking and locking; the MR arthrogram is the test of choice.
  • AVN causes: steroids, alcohol, trauma, lupus, sickle cell.
  • Lateral pain: GT bursitis or GTPS (point tenderness, can't lie on that side, gluteal tendinopathy). Seated socks are easy in GTPS and hard in OA.
  • Buttock pain with sciatica: think piriformis.
