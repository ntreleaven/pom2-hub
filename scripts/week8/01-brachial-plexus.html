Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the upper extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of arm pathology.
  2. Identify the components of the brachial plexus and its anatomic relationships to surrounding structures.
  3. Determine the sensory and motor loss anticipated with injuries to specific sites of the plexus (the roots and the terminal branches).

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: every nerve question in the upper limb this block comes back to the plexus. The clinical skill isn't reciting the diagram. It's working backwards: a patient has lost certain muscles and a patch of skin, so where is the lesion? A root (one spinal level) or a terminal branch (one peripheral nerve)? You can only answer that if you can draw the plexus from memory, which is what Dr. Martin asks you to practise.

Objective 2: the components, in order

The brachial plexus ("arm braid") is a network formed by the ventral rami of C5–T1, from the cervical enlargement of the spinal cord. It begins in the neck and ends in the axilla as terminal branches. The five parts, in order, are roots → trunks → divisions → cords → branches: "Really Thirsty? Drink Cold Beer."

One trap: the "roots" of the plexus are ventral rami, not the dorsal and ventral roots of a spinal nerve. Each one is already a mixed nerve, motor and sensory.

  • Roots (C5, C6, C7, C8, T1) give two branches:
    • Dorsal scapular nerve (C5) pierces the middle scalene and runs back to the rhomboids.
    • Long thoracic nerve (C5, C6, C7) supplies serratus anterior, running along its superficial surface.
  • Trunks: superior = C5 + C6, middle = C7 alone, inferior = C8 + T1. Only the superior trunk gives a branch: the suprascapular nerve (C5, C6) to supraspinatus and infraspinatus. The middle and inferior trunks give no nerves.
  • Divisions: each trunk splits into an anterior and a posterior division, six in all. No nerves come off the divisions. Anterior divisions supply the flexor (anterior) compartments. Posterior divisions supply the extensor (posterior) compartments. This is the organizing principle of the whole limb.
  • Cords are named for where they sit around the axillary artery, which is the key anatomic relationship:
    • Lateral cord = anterior divisions of the superior + middle trunks (C5–C7).
    • Medial cord = the anterior division of the inferior trunk, carrying on (C8, T1).
    • Posterior cord = all three posterior divisions (C5–T1).

Cord branches (before the terminal five):

  • Lateral cord → lateral pectoral nerve (C5–C7) to pectoralis major and minor. It travels superomedial to pectoralis minor.
  • Medial cord → medial pectoral nerve (C8, T1), which pierces pectoralis minor on its way to pectoralis major. The medial cord also gives the medial cutaneous nerve of the arm (T1) and the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (C8, T1). Both are sensory only, and both run right beside the ulnar nerve.
  • Posterior cord → upper subscapular (C5, C6) to subscapularis · thoracodorsal ("middle subscapular") (C6, C7, C8) to latissimus dorsi · lower subscapular (C5, C6) to subscapularis and teres major.

The five terminal branches: "make an M"

The lateral and medial cords form a letter M. The musculocutaneous nerve comes off the lateral cord. The ulnar comes off the medial cord. The median is the middle of the M, taking a lateral root and a medial root, one from each cord. The posterior cord ends as the axillary and radial nerves.

Root contributions, with the slide mnemonic "3 Musketeers Assassinate: 5 Mice, 5 Rats, and 2 Unicorns":

  • Musculocutaneous: 3 roots (C5, C6, C7)
  • Axillary: 2 (C5, C6)
  • Median: 5 (C5–T1)
  • Radial: 5 (C5–T1), because the posterior cord gets all three posterior divisions
  • Ulnar: 2 (C8, T1). The neurovascular modules list it as C7, C8, T1, so check which the exam uses.

To draw the plexus: "3 Wise (Y) Men (M) go bowling. One got a strike (X); the other got a spare (\)."

Objective 3: predicting motor and sensory loss

For each terminal branch, know what it moves and what skin it feels:

  • Musculocutaneous: motor to the anterior arm (coracobrachialis, which it pierces, then biceps and brachialis). It ends as the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. Lesion: weak elbow flexion and supination, numb lateral forearm.
  • Axillary: motor to deltoid + teres minor, sensory to the skin over the shoulder. Lesion: weak abduction past the first few degrees, numb "regimental badge" patch.
  • Radial: motor to the extensors of the posterior arm and forearm, sensory along the back of the limb. Lesion: weak elbow, wrist and finger extension ("wrist drop").
  • Median: motor to most forearm flexors and five hand muscles, sensory to part of the hand. It supplies nothing in the arm.
  • Ulnar: motor to 1½ anterior forearm muscles and most of the hand muscles, sensory to the medial hand. It also supplies nothing in the arm.

Root-level contrast: a long thoracic lesion (the nerve comes straight off the roots) gives a winged scapula, because serratus anterior can no longer hold the medial border of the scapula against the chest wall when the patient pushes. A plexus-level injury knocks out a pattern that crosses several peripheral nerves but follows root levels. A terminal-branch injury knocks out exactly one nerve's muscles and skin.

Objective 1: relationships that set up the rest of the week

The arm course of each nerve explains why fractures injure specific nerves (Lecture 04):

  • Median runs lateral to the brachial artery, crosses to its medial side mid-arm, then passes deep to the bicipital aponeurosis in the cubital fossa, beneath the median cubital vein.
  • Ulnar runs medially, then at about mid-shaft pierces the medial intermuscular septum into the posterior compartment, heading behind the medial epicondyle.
  • Radial enters the arm behind the brachial artery and in front of the long head of triceps, then spirals in the radial groove with the profunda brachii artery. It supplies triceps, pierces the lateral intermuscular septum, and reaches the forearm between brachialis and brachioradialis.

Skin of the upper limb (preview): posterior arm and forearm = radial (posterior cutaneous nerves) · lateral forearm = musculocutaneous (lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm) · medial arm = medial cutaneous nerve of the arm (medial cord) · medial forearm = medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (medial cord) · over the deltoid = axillary.

Bottom line: know the trunk roots (C5–6 / C7 / C8–T1). Remember that divisions have no branches, that anterior divisions mean flexors and posterior divisions mean extensors, and that cords are named around the axillary artery. Know the M, the root counts (3-2-5-5-2), and that median and ulnar do nothing in the arm. Then practise drawing it until you can do it in under a minute.
