High-Yield Content

Why this matters: all inflammatory arthritides are clinical diagnoses. History and exam build the differential, and tests only support it. Don't reflexively order ANA and RF on every sore joint.

Classifying arthritis

  • Type: inflammatory (autoimmune, infectious, crystalline) or non-inflammatory (primary OA, trauma, avascular necrosis, neuropathic arthropathy, recurrent hemarthrosis, hemochromatosis)
  • Duration: acute 6 weeks or less; chronic over 6 weeks
  • Number: mono 1 joint, oligo 2–3, poly 4 or more

Synovitis means increased fluid (swelling), warmth, pain and stiffness, with redness sometimes (infectious or crystalline).

Differential by duration

Acute (under 6 weeks) Chronic (over 6 weeks)
Polyarticular gout Rheumatoid arthritis
Infectious and post-infectious, including viral Connective tissue disease (SLE, myositis, Sjögren's, scleroderma, MCTD)
Rheumatic fever Reactive arthritis
Drug reactions (serum sickness) Seronegative spondyloarthropathy (psoriatic, enteropathic)
Early RA, CTD, vasculitis or seronegative arthritis Palindromic rheumatism, systemic vasculitis, polyarticular JIA, adult-onset Still's, sarcoidosis, drug reactions

Many acute autoimmune polyarthritides become chronic.

Viral arthritis: during or weeks after infection. Hepatitis A, B, C; HIV, CMV, EBV; parvovirus; chikungunya; influenza; COVID-19. Self-limited, supportive care, NSAIDs and short prednisone if severe.

Temporal pattern

  • Onset: acute (hours to days) in crystal and infectious arthritis; insidious (weeks to months) in RA, CTD, degenerative disease
  • Course: episodic (crystal); migratory (palindromic rheumatism, rheumatic fever, early gonococcal); progressive and additive (RA, CTD, degenerative)

Joint distribution

Pattern Think of
Symmetric, small and large RA (also SLE, CTD)
Asymmetric, small and large Psoriatic arthritis (also gout, reactive)
Axial, with peripheral large joints Axial SpA / ankylosing spondylitis

Joint predilections:

  • Axial: AS
  • First CMC: OA
  • First MTP: gout, OA
  • DIPs: OA, psoriatic; spared in RA and other CTDs
  • MCPs: RA, CPPD
  • Wrists, shoulders, elbows, ankles: RA, CPPD, psoriatic

Other findings: tenosynovitis, dactylitis (sausage digit), enthesitis, bursitis, nodules, Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes, swan-neck, boutonnière, ulnar deviation, MCP subluxation.

Investigations

Routine labs:

  • CBC: low Hgb (chronic disease), high platelets (inflammation), low platelets or WBC (autoimmune cytopenia)
  • Creatinine: glomerulonephritis or vasculitis; drug clearance
  • Liver enzymes: drug clearance; autoimmune hepatitis
  • CRP most used; ESR not routine and nonspecific

Special tests: uric acid for gout; viral serologies; HLA-B27 for AS; for CPPD, look for hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism.

RF: usually IgM against the Fc portion of IgG. Found in 70–80% of RA. Also in 5–20% of healthy people, Sjögren's, SLE, myositis, MCTD, hepatitis B and C, TB, syphilis, endocarditis, chronic lung disease, lymphoma. Neither sensitive nor specific for RA.

Anti-CCP: 95% specific for RA, can predate arthritis by years.

RF and anti-CCP: order only for initial diagnosis when RA is suspected. Don't use them to monitor; titres don't predict flares or response. Seropositive means more aggressive disease.

ANA: present in up to 30% of normal people. Positive doesn't rule in CTD; negative typically rules it out.

Management, briefly

Drugs: analgesia (acetaminophen, NSAIDs); corticosteroids (prednisone, IM, injection); DMARDs: csDMARD (methotrexate), tsDMARD (tofacitinib), bDMARD (adalimumab). Gout: colchicine, NSAIDs, steroids, allopurinol, febuxostat. Pseudogout: colchicine, NSAIDs, steroids.

Non-drug: gentle physiotherapy, strengthening, compresses, rest.

⚠ DMARD toxicity and biologics (not on these slides)

Standard teaching to check against the course: methotrexate (liver toxicity, cytopenias, teratogenicity, with folic acid); hydroxychloroquine (retinal toxicity); sulfasalazine (cytopenias, GI upset); leflunomide (liver toxicity, teratogenicity). Conventional DMARDs broadly modulate the immune system; biologics target one cytokine or cell, are used when conventional therapy isn't enough, and raise infection risk. You won't be asked to choose between specific biologics.

Take-home: narrow the differential by demographics, joint pattern, serology, fluid, risk factors and extra-articular features.
