High-Yield Content

Why this matters: an infected joint or bone is destroyed by delay, and antibiotics alone rarely fix it. The theme of this module is source control plus antibiotics.

Differential for an acutely swollen joint

Acute monoarthritis:

  • Infectious: bacterial, viral, mycobacterial, fungal
  • Crystal: gout, CPPD
  • Inflammatory: reactive arthritis, RA, sarcoid
  • Trauma
  • Blood: hemarthrosis
  • Malignant (less common)

Red flags for infection: fever, pain, and a significant reduction in range of motion.

History: onset (OPQRST), which joints, associated fever or rash, exposures (IVDU, sexual contact, travel), past interventions, and risk factors (immune compromise, an inflammatory joint condition such as RA).

Exam: vital signs; effusion, erythema, prior scar, rash; warmth and subtle effusion compared with the other side; ROM and function limited by pain.

Septic arthritis

How it happens: hematogenous seeding or direct inoculation. Risk factors: immune compromise, type 2 diabetes, alcohol, age over 80, IVDU, RA.

Pathophysiology: the synovium acts as a "petri dish". Inflammation leads to joint damage.

Investigations:

  • CBC, electrolytes, creatinine, CRP
  • Blood cultures
  • Joint aspirate: cell count, microscopy, Gram stain
  • Imaging if needed
  • Tests guided by the differential: coagulopathy, HIV, travel history

Reading the aspirate: these slides flag WBC counts above 20,000 and above 50,000. Gout typically sits at 20,000–50,000 with MSU crystals. Septic arthritis has a positive Gram stain or culture. Older patients with either may or may not be febrile, so fever does not separate them.

Pathogens:

  • Adults: S. aureus (MSSA and MRSA), streptococci (groups A, C, G), and less often Gram-negative bacilli
  • Children: S. aureus, streptococci

Treatment has two arms:

  • Surgical: joint decompression, irrigation (removes organisms and proteolytic and lysosomal enzymes), and debridement of necrotic tissue. The goal is to preserve the synovium as an immune-competent barrier.
  • Medical: systemic antibiotics. Guidelines quote 2–4 weeks; in practice S. aureus gets 4–6 weeks. The OVIVA trial (oral vs IV for bone and joint infection) is the reference on the slides.
  • Empiric: cefazolin (Ancef) 2 g IV q8h, or vancomycin 15 mg/kg plus ceftriaxone 2 g IV q24h.

Other infectious causes:

Infectious polyarthritis: bacterial, think infective endocarditis. Viral: parvovirus B19 (associated with anemia), chikungunya, Zika, dengue, hepatitis B and C, HIV.

Reactive arthritis: arthritis days to weeks after an infection, mono- or oligoarticular, with dactylitis and enthesitis, linked to HLA-B27. GI triggers: Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, C. difficile. GU trigger: Chlamydia trachomatis.

Prosthetic joint infection: best managed by orthopedics and ID together. Early vs late. Less virulent organisms (coagulase-negative staph, Propionibacterium acnes), so keep a high index of suspicion.

Osteomyelitis and discitis

Routes:

  • Hematogenous: vertebral osteomyelitis and most pediatric osteomyelitis
  • Non-hematogenous (contiguous): direct inoculation by trauma, post-surgical, diabetic foot infection

Why it is hard to cure: bacteria adhere and form biofilm, and survive inside osteoblasts. Acute disease brings edema, vascular congestion and small vessel thrombosis. Chronic disease leaves necrotic bone, which drives treatment failure.

Adults: diabetic foot, native vertebral osteomyelitis (people who inject drugs, middle-aged men), Pott's disease (TB), trauma or surgery, sacral wounds.

Children: hematogenous, usually under 12, in the vascular metaphysis. Organisms: S. aureus always, Salmonella in sickle cell disease, Kingella and other Gram-negatives.

Imaging:

  • X-ray and CT are not sensitive enough
  • MRI is the most sensitive, and findings persist despite resolution
  • Choosing Wisely: don't routinely repeat imaging in a patient improving on adequate antibiotics
  • Tagged WBC scan takes 2 days

Treatment questions: vascular supply, chronicity, need for debridement, patient engagement. Antibiotics alone will not save you.

Vertebral osteomyelitis / spondylodiscitis case: a 55-year-old man with 5 days of severe back pain, afebrile, with a normal neurologic exam. Next steps: blood cultures and MRI spine, and consider a spinal biopsy. MRI shows L3–L4 marrow edema with endplate irregularity, which is spondylodiscitis. Look for paravertebral and epidural abscess. Think of TB, Brucella (epidemiologic history) and M. bovis (BCG for bladder cancer). Treat for 6 weeks, with IV vancomycin plus ceftriaxone and neurosurgery if there is cord compression.

Sacral ulcer: ask about chronicity, offloading and debridement; involve plastics.

Hand infections

The key move is to separate cellulitis from abscess.

Cellulitis Abscess / deep infection
Location Superficial, wrist to MCP, dorsal, irregular margins Fingertip, around joints, palmar surface
Treatment Rest, elevation, antibiotics Incision and drainage, wound care, antibiotics

Rest and elevation for both.

  • Cat bite: PIP and DIP joints, mixed infection, Pasteurella multocida. Broad-spectrum antibiotics; I&D if it progresses.
  • Human "bite": young men, fights, MCP joint, mixed flora, Eikenella corrodens. Broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Paronychia: nail fold infection, F>M. S. aureus; mixed organisms in diabetes; fungus if immunocompromised.
  • Felon: pulp space infection.
  • Suppurative tenosynovitis: Kanavel's four signs: swelling of the entire finger, flexed posture, tenderness along the tendon sheath, intense pain on extension.

Diabetic foot infection

Assess: is it truly infected? How chronic? Any systemic symptoms? Why did it happen: new shoes, injury, device not worn, glycemic control, vascular disease, Charcot foot, prior ulcer?

Exam: location, blood supply, smell, and probe to bone.

Severity:

  • Mild: likely staph or strep
  • Moderate: deeper than skin, over 2 cm
  • Severe: moderate plus systemic features (SIRS)

Investigations: CBC, electrolytes, creatinine, CRP. Swab only if it looks infected and you can get a good specimen. A wound isn't necessarily infected, and any wound you swab will grow bacteria. X-ray first, MRI is most sensitive, tagged WBC scan takes 2 days.

Antibiotics:

  • Monomicrobial (staph/strep): cefazolin, or oral cephalexin, doxycycline or amoxicillin-clavulanate
  • Polymicrobial (chronic ulcer, Gram-negatives, possibly Pseudomonas): amoxicillin-clavulanate; if severe, IV ceftriaxone plus metronidazole
  • Pseudomonas on swab: piperacillin-tazobactam

Severe infection: exclude necrotizing fasciitis, draw blood cultures, get a deep swab, ask whether ortho or vascular is needed, start piperacillin-tazobactam (add vancomycin for MRSA history or risk).

Non-antibiotic pillars: revascularization, debridement, offloading, patient engagement.

Take-home: clinical assessment is essential, and what makes these infections better is not antibiotics alone.
