High-Yield Content

Why this matters: back pain is common, but a young patient whose back pain improves with activity may have a disease that fuses the spine. Inflammatory back pain is the pattern to recognize.

The spondyloarthritis family

SpA means inflammatory changes in the spine and spinal joints, but it can present with mainly peripheral arthritis and no spinal symptoms. It is called "seronegative" because rheumatoid factor is absent.

Family members: ankylosing spondylitis at the centre, with psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, arthritis with ulcerative colitis or Crohn's, acute anterior uveitis, "juvenile SpA", and undifferentiated SpA.

Ankylosing spondylitis

The prototypical SpA. An autoimmune attack on spinal joints that classically begins in the sacroiliac joints and slowly ascends. Inflammation triggers new bone, which fuses the spine into a rigid "bamboo spine".

Epidemiology and genetics:

  • HLA-B27 in over 90% of white AS patients, 50–80% of non-white patients, and 8% of the general Caucasian population
  • HLA-B27 raises relative risk 50–100×
  • Twin concordance: 60% monozygotic; 20–25% in HLA-B27+ dizygotic
  • Prevalence 0.1–1.4%

Sacroiliitis and imaging

  • Usually bilateral and symmetric
  • Starts in the synovial lower two-thirds of the joint
  • Earliest change: erosion on the iliac side, where cartilage is thinner
  • "Pseudowidening", then sclerosis, then complete fusion

Non-radiographic axial SpA (nr-axSpA): axial SpA with no diagnostic SI changes on X-ray but diagnostic MRI findings.

Enthesitis

The enthesis is where ligament, tendon or capsule inserts into bone. Enthesitis produces new bone or fibrosis. Common sites: SI joints, intervertebral discs, manubriosternal joints, symphysis pubis, iliac crests, trochanters, patellae, clavicles, calcanei (Achilles, plantar fascia).

"ANKSPOND": more than back pain

  • Aortic insufficiency, ascending aortitis, conduction abnormalities, pericarditis
  • Neurologic: atlantoaxial subluxation, cauda equina
  • Kidney: amyloidosis, chronic prostatitis
  • Spine: cervical fracture, stenosis, osteoporosis
  • Pulmonary: upper lobe fibrosis, restrictive changes
  • Ocular: anterior uveitis in 25–30%
  • Nephropathy (IgA)
  • Discitis or spondylodiscitis

Also microscopic colitis in 30–60%. AS patients can have a lower-limb oligoarthritis, sometimes before any back complaint.

Psoriatic arthritis

A heterogeneous arthritis, peripheral, axial or both. Psoriasis comes first in 67%; arthritis first or simultaneous in 33%. Look for hidden psoriasis: umbilicus, scalp, behind ears, perianal, nails.

Numbers: psoriasis 1.2%; PsA in 5–7% of psoriasis (as on the slides); first-degree relatives 50× more likely. HLA-B38, B39, DR4, B27.

Patterns:

  • Asymmetric oligoarthritis, over 50%
  • Axial, 20–40%
  • Rheumatoid-like polyarthritis, 15–25%
  • Predominantly DIP, 5–10%
  • Arthritis mutilans, 5%

Axial: asymmetric sacroiliitis, with large non-marginal "jug-handle" syndesmophytes.

Extra-articular:

  • Nail changes in 80% of PsA vs 30% of psoriasis alone: pitting, ridging, onycholysis, hyperkeratosis, oil spots, splinter hemorrhages
  • Conjunctivitis 20%, uveitis 7% (more with axial disease)
  • Dactylitis (sausage digit)

Reactive arthritis

Inflammatory arthritis in response to an infection elsewhere, not an infection of the joint. Avoid the eponym "Reiter's".

Classic triad:

  • Can't see: conjunctivitis or uveitis
  • Can't pee: urethritis or cervicitis
  • Can't climb a tree: large joint oligoarthritis, sacroiliitis, enthesitis

Skin: circinate balanitis, keratoderma blennorrhagicum.

Facts:

  • RF negative, HLA-B27 linked
  • GI: Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter. GU: Chlamydia trachomatis and other STIs
  • Most common in men aged 20–40
  • Starts 1–3 weeks after infection
  • Self-limiting, chronic or progressive; chronic in 15–30%
  • Treat based on symptoms

RA vs SpA

From this week's Polyarthritis slides: RA is symmetric, small and large joints, MCPs, DIPs spared, RF and anti-CCP positive. SpA is axial or asymmetric, with enthesitis, dactylitis and uveitis, RF negative, often HLA-B27 positive.

Treatment: axial SpA

  • Physiotherapy for all: posture, chest expansion, fewer deformities
  • NSAIDs: for mild to moderate symptoms, potentially disease modifying; indomethacin seems to work best; watch for GI disease
  • csDMARDs don't work for spinal disease, only predominantly peripheral symptoms
  • TNF inhibitors: very effective, likely disease modifying early
  • IL-17 inhibitors: very effective; time will tell on disease modification

Treatment: psoriatic arthritis

  • csDMARDs: often used, limited evidence against radiographic progression. Methotrexate treats skin and joints; sulfasalazine and leflunomide also used. Hydroxychloroquine is often avoided because of reports of severe psoriasis flares.
  • Apremilast (PDE4 inhibitor): good for skin and joints, likely weaker than biologics.
  • TNF inhibitors: axial and peripheral; skin, joints, enthesitis, dactylitis, nails; prevent radiographic progression.
  • IL-12/23 inhibitors: great for skin, good for joints, no effect on axial disease.
  • IL-17 inhibitors: great for skin, effective for axial and joints.
  • JAK inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors: peripheral joints and skin; axial effect uncertain.

You should know when biologics are considered, not choose between them.

⚠ DMARD toxicity (not on these slides)

Standard teaching to check against the course: methotrexate (liver toxicity, cytopenias, teratogenicity, with folic acid); sulfasalazine (cytopenias, GI upset); leflunomide (liver toxicity, teratogenicity). Biologics raise infection risk.

Take-home: a young person with inflammatory back pain, enthesitis, uveitis, psoriasis, IBD or a recent GI or GU infection should make you think SpA, even with a negative RF.
