Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Recommend a management plan for fractures and identify possible fracture complications
    • Describe the basic principles of fracture management
    • Describe the unique principles of management of open fractures and the potential complications of open fractures
    • Describe significant complications of fractures and fracture care, including VTE, compartment syndrome, non-union and mal-union, AVN, heterotopic ossification, and post traumatic arthritis

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: the bone usually heals, but limbs and lives are lost to complications. Compartment syndrome is the one you can't afford to miss.

Objectives of fracture management

Restore anatomy (length, alignment, rotation, translation), allow early motion, avoid complications of immobility and poor healing, and improve function.

The five steps

  1. Resuscitate as needed. Bones bleed, and sharp edges injure vessels.
  2. Obtain a reduction if displaced.
    • Open: in the OR under anesthesia
    • Closed: adequate analgesia and sedation, longitudinal traction, exaggerate the deformity, then reverse it while keeping traction
    • The choice depends on fracture type and location, displacement, open vs closed, and fitness for surgery
  3. Maintain the reduction: internal fixation (ORIF), external fixation, or cast/splint.
  4. Post-reduction X-rays confirm it's adequate and being held.
  5. Rehabilitate: as much safe movement as possible, as early as possible; protect weight bearing; PT and OT.

Open fractures

Definition: the fracture or hematoma is exposed to the environment through a traumatic break in soft tissue and skin. Any fracture with a wound, even a distant one, is open until proven otherwise.

Complications: higher rates, especially infection, plus nonunion, delayed union and incomplete recovery.

Goal: turn an unstable contaminated wound into a stable clean environment.

Principles:

  • Early antibiotics (cefazolin 2 g IV in the case)
  • Tetanus prophylaxis
  • Stabilize while waiting for ortho (closed reduction and splint)
  • Early surgery: remove devitalized tissue, gently irrigate with sterile saline, stabilize
  • Early soft tissue closure, with plastics for flaps if needed

Fracture healing

  • Primary (contact): bone surfaces directly apposed, as with internal fixation
  • Secondary (spontaneous): natural stages with callus
  • Healing is a clinical diagnosis: no tenderness, no motion at the fracture site, weight bearing possible
  • X-ray: fracture line widens at 10–14 days; callus at 2 weeks; union shows mature bridging callus with remodelling
  • Timelines: upper limb 3–10 weeks; lower limb 6–12 weeks
  • Delayed union: takes twice as long as expected

The seven complications

1. Thromboembolic disease. Virchow's triad fits most ortho trauma. Risks: multiple injuries, spinal cord or head injury, lower limb or pelvic fracture, older age, prolonged surgery or wait. Prevent mechanically (pneumatic compression; IVC filter prevents embolization, not clot) or chemically (ASA, LMWH), according to the patient.

2. Compartment syndrome. Pressure in a closed fascio-osseous space exceeds tolerance, so blood can't flow. Muscle and nerve are most vulnerable, with transient injury within 2 hours of ischemia.

  • External causes: tight cast, direct pressure, circumferential eschar
  • Internal causes: hematoma, fluid leak, burns, post-reperfusion, crush
  • Presentation (clinical diagnosis):
    • Pain out of proportion to the injury
    • Pain on passive stretch
    • Paresthesia (midterm)
    • Paralysis (very late)
    • Pulselessness isn't typical; by then it's been missed
  • Sites: most often lower leg or forearm. Classic: a tibial fracture with rising narcotic needs.
  • No test is required. If the patient can't communicate, measure pressure: diastolic BP minus compartment pressure under 20, or absolute pressure over 30 mmHg.
  • Non-orthopedist: call ortho urgently, elevate the limb, remove compressive dressings and casts, cool the limb, monitor. Definitive treatment is fasciotomy.

3. Nonunion. Failure to heal in twice the expected time (radiology slide: no healing in 6–9 months). Atrophic (no significant bone, impaired healing), hypertrophic (lots of bone, suggests stabilization is insufficient), infected. Manage with stabilization, biologic help (nutrition, metabolic issues, smoking cessation, bone graft), eradicating infection, reconstruction.

4. Malunion. Healed in an abnormal position. Assess in all planes, and consider the patient: if it affects comfort or function, consider osteotomy.

5. AVN (osteonecrosis). Bone death from lost blood supply, most common with a retrograde blood supply: femoral head, talus, scaphoid. Presents with pain; management is surgical.

6. Post-traumatic arthritis. After a periarticular fracture. Uncommon in hip and knee, very common with ankle fractures (over 50%). Treat like degenerative OA.

7. Heterotopic ossification. Ectopic bone after trauma. Risks: polytrauma, head injury, burns over 30% TBSA. Hip, knee and elbow are most at risk. Causes stiffness, even ankylosis; early redness and swelling. X-ray and CT are negative early; bone scan is more effective. Control inflammation and pain; excise once mature if problematic.
