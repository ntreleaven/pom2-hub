Contraception — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Source: Dr. Stephanie Black, "Contraception Options" (Contraception 2026.pdf), Elentra Learning Objectives

Duration target: ~10-12 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • List contraceptive options and their failure rates
  • Counsel regarding the most effective contraceptive methods
  • Understand the mechanism of action of contraceptive options
  • Discuss emergency contraception options
  • Apply new knowledge to clinical case scenarios

Cold open

Up to half of all pregnancies in Canada are unintended. That single fact drives almost everything in this module — the range of options that exist, why we counsel the way we do, and why "most effective" and "most commonly used" are often two very different lists. By the end of this script you'll be able to name the major contraceptive categories, their real-world (not just theoretical) failure rates, how each one actually works, and what to do when someone needs emergency contraception.

Why this matters

About 22% of all pregnancies in Canada end in induced abortion, and unintended pregnancy carries psychosocial, financial, and physical health consequences — not just for the patient but at a population level. And here's a detail worth remembering for counselling: when people discontinue a method, 42% do so without ever consulting a health care provider, and 69% switch to a less effective method. That's a counselling opportunity we're missing constantly.

Efficacy: perfect use vs. typical use

Every method has two numbers — a perfect-use failure rate and a typical-use failure rate — and the gap between them is where real life happens. Take condoms: perfect use failure is about 2%, but typical use is 18%. That gap is entirely about human behaviour: not using them for the whole act, putting them on after penetration, using the wrong lubricant, not leaving space at the tip. When you counsel patients, lead with typical-use numbers — that's the honest picture.

Natural methods and barrier methods

Natural family planning — basal body temperature, the calendar/rhythm method, cervical mucus monitoring, withdrawal, and lactational amenorrhea (which only counts under strict conditions: under 6 months postpartum, fully breastfeeding, and no return of menses) — carries a failure rate of 12–25%. It's still better than nothing, and for some patients it's the only culturally or religiously acceptable option, so it deserves real counselling, not dismissal.

Condoms are the most commonly used method worldwide. Male condoms: latex protects against STIs; polyurethane increases slippage and breakage; lambskin gives no HIV protection. Typical-use failure is 18%. Diaphragms, cervical caps, and spermicide are used infrequently now — and spermicide with nonoxynol-9 can actually damage vaginal epithelium and increase HIV transmission risk, so it's not recommended for high-risk individuals.

Permanent contraception

Tubal interruption — clips, rings, cautery, salpingectomy — has a 10-year cumulative failure rate under 2%. The single most common complication isn't a medical one: it's regret, and younger age is the biggest risk factor for regretting the decision. Vasectomy is the safer, cheaper, less invasive option for permanent contraception — failure around 1 in 2,000 — but it requires a follow-up semen analysis confirming azoospermia, and it takes time (and continued contraception) to become effective.

Hormonal contraception — the progestin-only family

The progestin-only pill ("mini-pill") has a typical-use failure around 9%. It thickens cervical mucus, impairs sperm motility, and suppresses ovulation in only about half of users — so it's less forgiving of missed doses than combined methods.

DMPA (the injection) is given every 12–13 weeks and works by suppressing pituitary gonadotropins and creating a hypoestrogenic state. Non-contraceptive benefits are substantial: high rates of amenorrhea, reduced endometrial cancer risk, improved PMS and chronic pelvic pain. The trade-off is a reversible decrease in bone mineral density in the first two years (not osteoporosis, and it recovers) and a tendency toward early weight gain that predicts ongoing weight gain — so counsel about appetite changes up front.

The progestin implant (Nexplanon) has one of the lowest failure rates of any method — about 1 in 2,000 — and lasts roughly three years.

Combined hormonal contraception

Pills, the patch, and the vaginal ring all combine estrogen and progestin. The progestin does most of the contraceptive work; estrogen mainly controls nuisance bleeding. Failure rate across all combined methods is around 9% typical use. When choosing a pill, side-effect patterns can guide selection — breakthrough bleeding responds to a higher-estrogen pill, acne responds better to certain progestins, PMDD may respond to Yaz, and endometriosis often does best on continuous dosing with low estrogen and high progestin/androgenicity.

Absolute contraindications to estrogen-containing methods matter clinically: smoking over age 35 with heavy use, uncontrolled hypertension, history of VTE without anticoagulation, active or recent cardiovascular disease, migraine with aura, current breast cancer, and complicated liver disease, among others.

Intrauterine contraception

This is the category to know cold, because it's the most effective reversible option available. The copper IUD has a failure rate around 0.8% and works through a sterile inflammatory reaction toxic to sperm and ova — no hormones at all. The hormonal IUD (LNG-IUS) has a failure rate closer to 0.2%, releasing levonorgestrel locally to thicken cervical mucus and cause endometrial changes; it also suppresses ovulation in up to 40% of users. Continuation rates are excellent — about 80% at one year. True absolute contraindications are actually very few: current pelvic infection, an undiagnosed abnormal uterine cavity, unevaluated abnormal bleeding, and a couple of rare malignancy scenarios. Most patients — including adolescents, nulliparous patients, and patients with complex medical histories — are candidates.

Emergency contraception

Four options, and timing determines which to reach for. Levonorgestrel (Plan B) works up to 5 days after unprotected intercourse but is most effective within 72 hours, and becomes ineffective once the LH surge has begun — it may also be less effective at higher BMI. Ulipristal acetate is a selective progesterone receptor modulator that remains effective even during the LH rise, unlike levonorgestrel — making it the better oral choice later in the fertile window. The copper IUD is the single most effective form of emergency contraception, usable up to 7 days after unprotected intercourse, and it has the added benefit of leaving the patient with ongoing highly effective contraception afterward.

Bringing it together with clinical cases

An 18-year-old with a strong family history of clotting disorders who's afraid of weight gain: avoid estrogen given the family history until thrombophilia is ruled out, and steer clear of DMPA because of the weight gain association — a progestin-only pill or an IUD/IUS fits well.

A 41-year-old who's completed childbearing, has light regular periods, and struggles with the cost of daily pills: a copper IUD is inexpensive, effective, avoids surgery, and doesn't require daily adherence.

A 36-year-old with a complex psychosocial history, heavy smoking, and high risk of unintended pregnancy who needs something highly effective and low-maintenance: estrogen is contraindicated by her smoking status, and a long-acting reversible method — often an IUD placed the same day as a negative pregnancy test — is preferred precisely because there are essentially no true contraindications to IUD insertion in her situation.

Closing summary

Long-acting reversible contraception — IUDs, IUS, and implants — are the most effective and highly acceptable options for most patients across the reproductive lifespan. Condoms remain essential as backup and for STI protection regardless of what other method someone uses. Every contraceptive conversation should include a plan for emergency contraception when a less effective method is chosen, should address future fertility desires, and should always be framed around typical-use — not perfect-use — failure rates.
