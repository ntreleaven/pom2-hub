Framing

Early pregnancy complications are among the most common reasons patients present to emergency departments and early pregnancy assessment clinics. This module covers two related but distinct problems: spontaneous abortion (miscarriage), and ectopic pregnancy — a true obstetric emergency. Both present with the same cardinal symptoms — bleeding and/or pain in early pregnancy — which is exactly why a structured approach matters: you cannot tell them apart on symptoms alone.

Spontaneous abortion: categories

Spontaneous abortion is pregnancy loss before 20 weeks gestation. It's categorized by clinical and ultrasound findings:

  • Threatened abortion: vaginal bleeding with a closed cervix and a viable intrauterine pregnancy on ultrasound. Roughly half will go on to miscarry regardless of intervention.
  • Inevitable abortion: bleeding with an open cervical os, but products of conception not yet passed — loss is essentially certain.
  • Incomplete abortion: some products of conception have passed, but retained tissue remains in the uterus, usually with an open os and ongoing bleeding.
  • Complete abortion: all products of conception have passed, the uterus is empty on ultrasound, bleeding and cramping are resolving, and the cervical os is typically closed.
  • Missed abortion: fetal demise has occurred, but the cervix remains closed and no tissue has passed — often discovered incidentally on a routine ultrasound rather than through symptoms.
  • Septic abortion: any of the above complicated by intrauterine infection — fever, uterine tenderness, and a foul discharge — and a genuine emergency requiring prompt evacuation and antibiotics.

Roughly 10–15% of clinically recognized pregnancies end in miscarriage, and the great majority are due to chromosomal abnormality (aneuploidy) — which is why, for a first isolated miscarriage, extensive investigation usually isn't warranted.

Management options

Management mirrors what you learned in the abortion module: expectant, medical, and surgical approaches all have a role, and the choice depends on gestational age, hemodynamic stability, patient preference, and whether infection is present.

  • Expectant management allows spontaneous completion over days to weeks — appropriate for a hemodynamically stable patient with an incomplete or missed abortion who is comfortable waiting, with close follow-up arranged.
  • Medical management typically uses misoprostol to promote uterine evacuation, avoiding a procedure — appropriate for stable patients wanting to avoid surgery, similar in principle to medical termination.
  • Surgical management (D\&C or manual vacuum aspiration) is fastest and most predictable, and is preferred when there's heavy bleeding, hemodynamic instability, infection, or patient preference for a quicker resolution.

All Rh-negative patients experiencing a pregnancy loss should receive Rh immunoglobulin to prevent alloimmunization in future pregnancies.

Ectopic pregnancy — the emergency to never miss

An ectopic pregnancy is any pregnancy implanted outside the endometrial cavity — over 95% occur in the fallopian tube (most commonly the ampulla), with rarer sites including the cervix, ovary, cesarean scar, and abdominal cavity. It occurs in roughly 1–2% of all pregnancies, but accounts for a disproportionate share of first-trimester maternal mortality because of the risk of catastrophic intra-abdominal hemorrhage if the tube ruptures.

Risk factors center on anything that impairs normal tubal transport of the fertilized egg: prior ectopic pregnancy (the single strongest risk factor), prior pelvic inflammatory disease or tubal surgery, current IUD use (rare overall pregnancy risk, but a pregnancy that does occur with an IUD in place is more likely to be ectopic), assisted reproductive technology, smoking, and a history of tubal ligation.

Classic presentation: the triad of amenorrhea, vaginal bleeding, and unilateral pelvic pain in a patient with a positive pregnancy test — though presentations vary widely, from asymptomatic to hemodynamically unstable with peritoneal signs from rupture and hemoperitoneum. Any patient with a positive pregnancy test and pain or bleeding needs an ectopic ruled out until proven otherwise — this is the single most important clinical reflex from this module.

Diagnosis combines quantitative beta-hCG with transvaginal ultrasound. A key concept is the discriminatory zone — the beta-hCG level (roughly 1,500–3,500 IU/L, lab- and protocol-dependent) above which a normal intrauterine pregnancy should be visible on transvaginal ultrasound. If beta-hCG is above the discriminatory zone and no intrauterine pregnancy is seen, ectopic pregnancy is presumed until proven otherwise. In a normal pregnancy, beta-hCG roughly doubles every 48–72 hours in early gestation; a rise slower than this — or a plateau — raises suspicion for either a failing intrauterine pregnancy or an ectopic.

Management depends on stability, hCG trend, and ectopic size:

  • Expectant management for a very early, spontaneously resolving ectopic with low and falling hCG.
  • Medical management with methotrexate (same antimetabolite mechanism discussed in the abortion module) for a hemodynamically stable patient with an unruptured ectopic, no fetal cardiac activity, and hCG below a defined threshold — with serial hCG monitoring to confirm resolution.
  • Surgical management (laparoscopic salpingostomy or salpingectomy) for a ruptured ectopic, hemodynamic instability, or when medical criteria aren't met.

As with spontaneous abortion, Rh-negative patients need Rh immunoglobulin.

Bringing it together

The unifying clinical skill this module is building is a reflex, not a fact list: bleeding or pain plus a positive pregnancy test means you confirm pregnancy location before you do anything else, because a missed ectopic pregnancy is one of the few genuinely time-critical emergencies in early pregnancy care. Once ectopic is excluded, spontaneous abortion management becomes a conversation about patient preference among expectant, medical, and surgical options — mirroring the same framework used for induced termination, because physiologically the uterus doesn't distinguish between the two.
