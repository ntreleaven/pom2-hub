Week 12 — Rheumatology Investigations Review

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Source: Dr. Janet Pope, "Rheumatology Investigations Review" (Elentra, 26 min): three short lessons on RA, connective tissue disease and vasculitis blood tests. There is no slide PDF in the download; this script uses the module's lesson text.

Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Develop a plan for appropriate investigations of a patient with a possible rheumatologic condition
    • Describe common rheumatologic blood tests including RF, ANA, ANCAs, ESR, CRP and HLA B27 and identify when they should be ordered and also when ordering them is inappropriate
    • Describe the expected results when testing for inflammatory conditions (including RA, SLE, seronegative arthropathies, JIA, GCA/PMR)

High-Yield Content

The rule of the whole module: no rheumatology diagnosis is made by a blood test alone. There must be clinical features. The one exception: gout can be diagnosed by finding uric acid crystals in synovial fluid. Order a test only when you suspect the disease it tests for.

Rheumatoid arthritis tests

Rheumatoid factor (RF):

  • An autoantibody, often IgM, directed against the Fc portion of IgG.
  • Not specific for RA. Also positive in connective tissue diseases, hepatitis C and healthy older people.
  • Many people with RA are RF positive. A positive RF goes with more severe RA: erosions on X-ray and extra-articular disease (interstitial lung disease, rheumatoid nodules).

Anti-CCP (the common ACPA test):

  • Antibodies against citrullinated peptides. Common in RA and fairly specific (not totally).
  • Also marks more severe, erosive RA.
  • Don't order it unless you suspect RA (it isn't reimbursed in Ontario).
  • Better than a test: a referral that says what you see, e.g. "I suspect early RA: swelling of wrists, MCPs and PIPs, and MTP stiffness."

Inflammation labs in RA and other chronic inflammatory disease: elevated ESR, elevated CRP, anemia of chronic disease.

Connective tissue disease tests

ANA (antinuclear antibody):

  • Ordered far too often. SLE affects about 1 in 1,000 women, RA about 1 in 100 people, yet ANAs are ordered 10× more than RF.
  • About 1/3 of women have a positive ANA, but it is relevant in at most 1/1,000. Most positives are false positives.
  • Sensitive, not specific: a negative ANA helps rule out SLE; a positive ANA does NOT make a diagnosis.
  • Don't repeat a positive ANA. Don't repeat a negative one unless new symptoms suggest a connective tissue disease.
  • A strongly positive result is more likely to matter than a borderline one. Patterns: speckled and homogeneous are common and less helpful; peripheral/rim, nucleolar and centromere are less common and possibly more helpful.

Anti-dsDNA:

  • Double-stranded DNA is only in the nucleus, so anti-DNA can't be positive without a positive ANA.
  • Order only if you suspect SLE and the ANA is positive.
  • Only about half of SLE patients are ever anti-DNA positive, so a negative test does not rule out SLE.
  • Varies with disease activity and is higher in lupus nephritis.
  • Modern kit assays are cheaper but less specific than the old method.

ENA (extractable nuclear antigen panel):

  • Don't order if the ANA is negative. Generally a specialist test.
  • If you learn one: anti-Ro.
    • SLE with photosensitive rash
    • Anti-Ro + anti-La: Sjögren's syndrome
    • Anti-Ro-positive pregnant patients are followed for fetal congenital heart block (rare); babies may get neonatal lupus (very rare)
  • Lung disease antibodies: anti-Jo1 (ILD in dermatomyositis) and anti-Scl-70 / topoisomerase I (scleroderma ILD).

Complements: usually a specialist test. Low in active SLE, e.g. renal lupus, and vary with activity.

Vasculitis tests

ANCA (anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody): order only when you really suspect vasculitis.

  • Patterns: cANCA (cytoplasmic; antibody to PR3) and pANCA (perinuclear; antibody to MPO).
  • cANCA is more likely to mean real vasculitis; pANCA has more false positives.
  • Different ANCAs go with different vasculitides (GPA, EGPA).
  • pANCA and cANCA both positive → think cocaine-induced vasculitis (from levamisole cut into cocaine).
  • Levels may vary with disease activity, but not reliably.

Tests named in the objectives but not in the lesson text

⚠ Not on this module's lesson text (covered in Week 11 lectures):

  • ESR and CRP: non-specific inflammation markers. Typically high in GCA/PMR (ESR often very high) and active RA; usually normal in OA and fibromyalgia.
  • HLA-B27: supports a seronegative spondyloarthropathy (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis) with the right clinical picture; common in healthy people, so not a screening test.
  • JIA: ANA in oligoarticular JIA flags uveitis risk (screen eyes), not the diagnosis; RF is usually negative.
  • Seronegative arthropathies: RF and anti-CCP negative by definition.
