Endometriosis — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teachers: Erin Lovett, Stephanie Black | Duration on Elentra: 15 min

Duration target: ~7-8 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Define endometriosis
  • Describe clinical symptoms and physical exam findings
  • Outline an approach to discussion of dyspareunia
  • Apply treatments for endometriosis
  • Describe clinical outcomes of endometriosis

Definition

Endometriosis is the presence of endometrial-like glands and stroma outside the uterine cavity — most commonly on the ovaries, pelvic peritoneum, and uterosacral ligaments, though implants can occur almost anywhere, including distant sites like the diaphragm or lung in rare cases. This ectopic tissue remains hormonally responsive, undergoing cyclic proliferation and breakdown in response to estrogen and progesterone just like the normal endometrium — but with nowhere for the shed tissue and blood to go, which drives the local inflammation, scarring, and pain that define the disease. The leading explanatory model is Sampson's theory of retrograde menstruation (menstrual tissue flowing backward through the fallopian tubes into the pelvis), though embryonic rest, coelomic metaplasia, and lymphatic/vascular spread theories all contribute to a broader multifactorial understanding — no single theory fully explains why some people with retrograde menstruation (which is actually common) develop endometriosis and most don't, implicating immune and genetic factors as well.

Clinical symptoms

The classic triad is dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and chronic pelvic pain — but presentation is genuinely variable, and a key teaching point is that disease severity does not correlate with symptom severity: minimal disease can cause debilitating pain, and extensive disease can be nearly silent. Other symptoms depend on where implants are located: cyclic dyschezia (painful defecation) or hematochezia with bowel involvement, cyclic dysuria or hematuria with bladder involvement, and infertility, which affects a substantial proportion of patients with endometriosis (as covered in the Fertilization & Infertility module) through multiple mechanisms — inflammatory peritoneal fluid, impaired implantation, and distorted pelvic anatomy.

Physical exam findings

Exam can be entirely normal, which is important to communicate to patients — a normal exam does not rule out endometriosis. When findings are present: tenderness or nodularity along the uterosacral ligaments or posterior cul-de-sac on bimanual exam (suggesting deep infiltrating disease), a fixed or retroverted uterus (suggesting adhesions), and adnexal masses (suggesting endometriomas, sometimes called "chocolate cysts" for their appearance on drainage). The gold standard for definitive diagnosis remains laparoscopy with histologic confirmation, but because that's invasive, most clinical diagnoses today are made on the basis of characteristic symptoms plus response to empiric hormonal therapy, supported where available by advanced ultrasound with structured pelvic mapping.

Approaching dyspareunia in conversation

Dyspareunia is common in endometriosis but is a symptom many patients find difficult to raise unprompted — so a proactive, normalized approach to asking about it matters clinically. Useful framing: ask directly and matter-of-factly as part of a standard pelvic pain history ("Do you experience pain with intercourse? Is it with entry, or deeper, or both?"), since deep dyspareunia specifically (pain with deep penetration, often worse in certain positions) points toward implants or nodularity in the posterior cul-de-sac/uterosacral region, distinguishing it from entry-level pain (which has a different differential, more related to pelvic floor tension or vulvar conditions). Validate that the symptom is real and treatable rather than something to simply tolerate, and explore its impact on the patient's relationships and quality of life — this is often where the functional burden of endometriosis is greatest, and addressing it explicitly (rather than focusing only on menstrual pain) is frequently what patients most want acknowledged.

Treatment

Hormonal suppression is first-line for patients not actively trying to conceive, since endometriosis is estrogen-driven: combined oral contraceptives (often continuous, skipping placebo weeks, to avoid the withdrawal bleed that reactivates implants), progestin-only options (the pill, DMPA, or the LNG-IUS), or Visanne (dienogest). Second-line for refractory symptoms: GnRH agonists or antagonists (Lupron, Orilissa), inducing a reversible medical menopause that shrinks estrogen-dependent implants — effective, but limited by menopausal side effects and used with add-back therapy to protect bone density during longer courses.

Surgical management (laparoscopic excision or ablation of implants) is considered for diagnostic confirmation, for pain refractory to medical therapy, or for patients trying to conceive where suppression isn't an option — surgery can improve spontaneous pregnancy rates for a period afterward in early-stage disease, though endometriomas are approached cautiously in patients wanting to preserve fertility, since removing them risks damaging surrounding healthy ovarian tissue and reducing ovarian reserve.

Multimodal pain management — NSAIDs, pelvic floor physiotherapy (particularly relevant given how often chronic pelvic floor tension co-exists with and amplifies endometriosis pain), and a chronic pain framework more broadly — is increasingly recognized as an essential complement to hormonal and surgical treatment, not an afterthought.

Clinical outcomes

Endometriosis is a chronic disease without a cure — treatment manages symptoms and disease activity rather than eliminating it, and recurrence after surgery is common if hormonal suppression isn't continued afterward. Long-term outcomes patients should understand: many achieve good symptom control with appropriate hormonal management; fertility outcomes vary by disease stage, with early-stage disease often compatible with spontaneous conception (sometimes aided by surgery) and advanced-stage disease more often requiring IVF; and quality-of-life impact — on physical function, mental health, relationships, and work — can be substantial and is itself a legitimate treatment target, not just a byproduct of controlling pain. Diagnostic delay remains a persistent, well-documented problem in endometriosis care, often years between symptom onset and diagnosis — which is part of why proactively asking about dysmenorrhea and dyspareunia, rather than waiting for patients to raise it, matters so much in practice.

Bringing it together

Endometriosis is ectopic, hormonally-active endometrial tissue causing pain and infertility through inflammation and anatomic distortion, with a symptom burden that doesn't reliably track disease severity on imaging or laparoscopy. Diagnosis is usually clinical rather than surgical in the first instance, treatment is staged from hormonal suppression through to surgery and multimodal pain management, and this is fundamentally a chronic disease requiring ongoing, patient-centred management — including a proactive, normalized conversation about dyspareunia that many patients won't initiate themselves.
