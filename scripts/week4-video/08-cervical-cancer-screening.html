Cervical Cancer Screening — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Cady Zeman | MCC: Periodic health encounter/Preventive health advice [74] | Duration on Elentra: 39 min

Duration target: ~9-10 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why cervical cancer screening is effective in preventing cervical cancer
  • Explain the process for cervical cancer screening and the management of abnormalities
  • Describe HPV's role in the development of cervical cancer, the benefits of vaccination, and the role of HPV testing in screening

Why screening works

Cervical cancer is almost unique among cancers in having a long, well-characterized precancerous phase — cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) — that can persist for years before progressing to invasive cancer. That slow timeline is what makes screening so effective: it catches and treats precancerous change long before it becomes cancer, which is why cervical cancer incidence and mortality have dropped dramatically everywhere organized screening programs exist. This also explains why screening intervals are measured in years, not months — the disease process itself is slow.

HPV as the necessary cause

Virtually all cervical cancer is caused by persistent infection with high-risk HPV subtypes, most importantly 16 and 18, which together account for roughly 70% of cases. HPV is extremely common — most sexually active people acquire it at some point — but most infections clear spontaneously within one to two years through normal immune clearance. Cancer risk comes specifically from persistent infection, where the virus isn't cleared and its oncoproteins (E6 and E7) progressively disrupt normal cell-cycle control, driving the stepwise progression from low-grade to high-grade dysplasia to invasive cancer.

HPV vaccination targets the high-risk subtypes before exposure, and it is most effective when given before sexual debut — though it still provides benefit afterward, since most people haven't been exposed to every vaccine-covered subtype. Population data from vaccinated cohorts is already showing measurable reductions in high-grade dysplasia, which is the earliest hard endpoint we can measure before the (decades-later) cancer incidence data matures.

The screening process

Modern cervical screening programs (Ontario included) are shifting toward primary HPV testing — testing directly for high-risk HPV DNA — because it's more sensitive than cytology (the Pap smear) alone for detecting the women actually at risk, and a negative HPV test allows a longer safe interval before the next screen. Cytology remains important as a triage tool: when HPV testing is positive, cytology (or direct colposcopy, depending on the pathway) helps determine whether abnormal cells are actually present and how urgently they need further evaluation. Screening generally starts around age 25 (given the low cancer risk and high spontaneous HPV clearance rate in younger patients) and continues at extended intervals — such as every 5 years with HPV-based screening — through to around age 65-70, with screening able to stop after that in patients with a consistently normal screening history.

Managing an abnormal result

An abnormal screen doesn't mean cancer — it means further evaluation is needed, and the pathway depends on the degree of abnormality:

  • Low-grade changes (or a positive HPV test with normal/low-grade cytology) are often managed with repeat testing at a shorter interval, since many will resolve spontaneously as the immune system clears the infection.
  • High-grade changes warrant colposcopy — direct visualization of the cervix with acetic acid application to highlight abnormal areas, plus biopsy of any suspicious region to confirm the histologic grade.
  • Confirmed high-grade CIN (CIN2/3) is typically treated with an excisional or ablative procedure — most commonly a LEEP (loop electrosurgical excision procedure) — which both treats the lesion and provides a specimen confirming there's no unsuspected invasive component.

After treatment, patients need continued surveillance, since treated CIN carries a modestly elevated risk of recurrence or new lesions compared to the general population, and follow-up HPV/cytology co-testing at defined intervals catches this.

Bringing it together

The logic chain to hold onto: HPV is the necessary cause, vaccination prevents the initial persistent infection, screening catches the precancerous phase that unfolds over years if infection does persist, and treatment of high-grade lesions interrupts progression before invasive cancer develops. Each piece — vaccination, screening, and treatment of abnormalities — is a separate intervention point along the same causal pathway, which is exactly why cervical cancer is one of the most preventable cancers when a population has good access to all three.
