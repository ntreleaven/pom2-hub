Genetic Screening for Pregnancy in Canada — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Maha Saleh | Duration on Elentra: 50 min

Duration target: ~11-12 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Develop an approach to asking relevant family and genetic history
  • List ethnicities (and conditions) requiring specific fetal screening
  • Identify red flags and indications for Prenatal Genetics referral
  • Describe the timing and process of fetal genetic screening
  • Define non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)
  • Define invasive prenatal testing
  • Define preimplantation genetic screening and diagnosis
  • Assess your own and future patients' values and beliefs on genetic testing and actions

Taking a genetic and family history — the approach

A structured prenatal genetic history covers, at minimum: maternal and paternal age (advanced maternal age, generally ≥35 at delivery, raises aneuploidy risk; advanced paternal age is linked to a small increase in de novo mutations); ethnicity and ancestry of both partners (directly determines which population-specific carrier screening is offered — see below); consanguinity (increases the risk of autosomal recessive conditions being expressed in offspring); prior pregnancy history (previous losses, stillbirths, or a child with a genetic condition or congenital anomaly); and a three-generation family history on both sides, specifically asking about intellectual disability, congenital anomalies, early infant deaths, and known genetic conditions. Asking about consanguinity and genetic conditions requires sensitivity and normalization — framing these as routine questions asked of every patient, not questions that single anyone out, improves both accuracy and patient comfort.

Ethnicity-specific screening

Certain conditions cluster strongly by ancestry, and recognizing this is part of comprehensive prenatal care:

  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry: an expanded panel is offered, covering conditions including Tay-Sachs disease, Canavan disease, familial dysautonomia, Gaucher disease, and cystic fibrosis, among others — carrier frequencies for several of these conditions are markedly elevated in this population.
  • Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Southeast Asian ancestry: increased carrier frequency for the thalassemias (alpha and beta) and hemoglobinopathies.
  • Sub-Saharan African, Caribbean, and some Mediterranean ancestries: increased carrier frequency for sickle cell disease and related hemoglobinopathies.
  • French Canadian ancestry: specific founder mutations for certain recessive conditions are more prevalent in Quebec-descended populations.

Cystic fibrosis carrier screening, notably, is increasingly offered to all patients regardless of ancestry given its prevalence across populations, rather than being restricted to ethnicity-based criteria the way some other conditions are.

Red flags for a Prenatal Genetics referral

Refer for prenatal genetics counselling when there is: a screen-positive result on NIPT or maternal serum screening; an abnormal finding on ultrasound (structural anomaly, or soft markers in combination); a family history of a known or suspected genetic condition; recurrent pregnancy loss (as covered in the Fertilization & Infertility module); consanguinity with a positive family history; advanced maternal age combined with patient interest in invasive diagnostic testing; or either partner being a known carrier of an autosomal recessive or X-linked condition. The threshold for referral should be low — genetic counsellors are specifically trained to walk patients through complex probabilistic information and the values-based decisions that follow from it, which is a different skill set from routine prenatal counselling.

Timing and process of fetal genetic screening

First trimester (roughly 11–14 weeks): nuchal translucency ultrasound measurement combined with maternal serum markers (PAPP-A and free beta-hCG) — the combined first-trimester screen — estimates risk for trisomy 21, 18, and 13. NIPT (see below) is increasingly offered as either a primary screen or as follow-up to a positive combined screen, and can be performed from about 10 weeks onward.

Second trimester (roughly 15–20 weeks): a detailed anatomy ultrasound assesses fetal structural development in detail and can also identify soft markers associated with aneuploidy risk. Maternal serum quad screening (when first-trimester screening was missed) can still estimate aneuploidy and neural tube defect risk at this stage.

The overall principle: screening is staged, non-invasive tests are offered first and used to refine risk, and invasive diagnostic testing is reserved for patients with an elevated risk result or a specific indication, given its small but real procedural risk.

Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)

NIPT analyzes cell-free fetal DNA circulating in maternal blood (originating from the placenta) to screen for the common trisomies (21, 18, 13) and, optionally, sex chromosome aneuploidies. It has substantially higher sensitivity and specificity than traditional serum screening for these conditions — but it is critically a screening test, not a diagnostic one: a positive NIPT result requires confirmation with diagnostic testing before any irreversible decision is made, because false positives occur (including from confined placental mosaicism, a vanishing twin, or maternal chromosomal changes). This distinction — screening estimates risk, diagnosis confirms — is one of the most important concepts for patients to genuinely understand before testing, not just be told once during consent.

Invasive prenatal testing

Chorionic villus sampling (CVS), performed around 11–14 weeks, samples placental tissue transabdominally or transcervically for direct chromosomal/genetic analysis. Amniocentesis, performed from about 15 weeks onward, samples amniotic fluid containing fetal cells. Both provide definitive diagnostic results (unlike screening tests) but carry a small procedure-related miscarriage risk — modern estimates put this well under 1% for both procedures in experienced hands, lower than older teaching often suggested, which is worth updating in counselling conversations. These are offered when screening has identified elevated risk, when a specific genetic condition needs direct confirmation, or when a patient prefers diagnostic certainty over screening probability from the outset.

Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT)

Performed in the context of IVF, before embryo transfer: a small number of cells are biopsied from a developing embryo (typically at the blastocyst stage) and tested either for aneuploidy (PGT-A, screening for the correct chromosome number) or for a specific known familial genetic condition (PGT-M, for monogenic conditions, when a parent is a known carrier). This allows selection of embryos for transfer before pregnancy is established, which is a meaningfully different decision point — and a different values conversation — than prenatal testing during an established pregnancy.

Values and beliefs — why this matters as its own objective

Genetic screening and testing decisions are values-based, not purely medical, and different patients will reasonably reach different decisions given identical risk information. Some patients want maximum information regardless of what they'd do with a result; others prefer to decline screening entirely, finding the anxiety of probabilistic risk information more burdensome than reassuring; and results can raise complex, sometimes unanticipated decisions about continuing or not continuing a pregnancy, which patients deserve to have anticipated and discussed before testing, not only after a result arrives. Part of competent prenatal genetic counselling is being aware of your own assumptions and comfort level with these conversations, presenting information neutrally rather than steering toward a particular choice, and being prepared to support patients through whichever decision aligns with their own values — which may not match your own.

Bringing it together

Prenatal genetic care follows a logical staged sequence: a thorough family and ethnicity-informed history identifies who needs targeted carrier or condition-specific screening; non-invasive screening (combined first-trimester screen, NIPT, anatomy ultrasound) estimates risk without procedural danger to the pregnancy; elevated-risk results are confirmed with invasive diagnostic testing when the patient chooses to pursue it; and throughout, the clinician's role is to present accurate, values-neutral information and refer early to genetics specialists when complexity exceeds routine prenatal counselling — always remembering that the right decision is the one that fits the individual patient's own values, not a single "correct" clinical answer.
