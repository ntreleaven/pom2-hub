Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

    • Identify the distinguishing features of the bones and joints of the upper extremity and relate these to their appearance on plain radiographs.
  2. Create a differential diagnosis and appropriate management plan for patients presenting with upper extremity complaints (numbness, weakness, pain, limitations of range of motion).
    • Describe the anatomic basis of common elbow and arm complaints including medial and lateral epicondylitis, distal biceps rupture, olecranon bursitis, ulnar neuropathy, arthritis, subluxation/dislocation and injury to the ulnar collateral ligament.

This module covers the joint and ligament problems plus bursitis. The tendon problems are in "Muscles of the Distal Upper Extremity", and ulnar neuropathy is in "Neurovascular Supply of the Distal Upper Extremity".

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: you can't read an elbow or wrist X-ray, or localize elbow pain, without the bony landmarks. The clinical half gives a framework that every elbow complaint this week hangs on: sort it by which structure is at fault and where it hurts.

Objective 1: bones and joints you need to recognize

Humerus (distal end):

  • Medial epicondyle: the common flexor origin. The ulnar nerve runs behind it.
  • Lateral epicondyle: the common extensor origin.
  • Capitulum (lateral condyle) meets the radial head. Trochlea (medial condyle) meets the ulna's trochlear notch.
  • Coronoid fossa (front) takes the coronoid process in full flexion. Olecranon fossa (back) takes the olecranon in full extension.
  • Radial (spiral) groove on the back of the shaft carries the radial nerve and profunda brachii.

Radius (lateral, shorter): its head meets the capitulum and the radial notch of the ulna and pivots inside the annular ligament. Then the neck, and the radial tuberosity, where biceps inserts.

Ulna (medial, longer, the stabilizer): the olecranon takes triceps, the coronoid process takes brachialis, the trochlear notch holds the trochlea, and the radial notch holds the radial head.

Elbow joint: a synovial hinge (uniaxial: flexion and extension) with two articulations, humeroulnar and humeroradial. The capsule is weak in front and behind (to allow flexion and extension) and reinforced at the sides:

  • Radial (lateral) collateral ligament: lateral epicondyle → blends into the annular ligament.
  • Ulnar (medial) collateral ligament: medial epicondyle → coronoid and olecranon. It has three bands: anterior (cord-like, strongest), posterior (fan-like, weakest) and oblique (deepens the trochlear socket).
  • The subcutaneous olecranon bursa sits between the skin and the olecranon.

Carrying angle: look for symmetry between sides.

Radioulnar joints (pronation and supination):

  • Proximal: synovial pivot between the radial head and the radial notch, inside the elbow capsule, held by the annular ligament.
  • Intermediate: a syndesmosis, the interosseous membrane.
  • Distal: synovial pivot.

Wrist and hand. Carpals, lateral to medial, proximal row first: "Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle" (scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform; trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate).

  • Scaphoid: the most commonly fractured carpal.
  • Lunate: the most commonly dislocated carpal (still uncommon). It can compress the median nerve in the carpal tunnel.
  • Pisiform + hook of hamate form the ulnar (Guyon's) canal.
  • Metacarpals: 5. Phalanges: 2 in the thumb, 3 in each other digit. Sesamoid bones sit at the thumb.
  • Joints: CMC, MCP, IP. Digits are numbered 1–5 (thumb, index, long, ring, small).

Objective 2: a clinical approach to the elbow

  • History: OLDCARS. Is it acute (traumatic, inflammatory) or chronic (degenerative, overuse)? Overlay the anatomy (what's medial, what's lateral).
  • Physical: look (swelling, deformity), feel (where is it tender, is it hot), move (is range restricted?), then structure-specific tests. Don't forget the nerves.
  • Management: if there are no red flags, apply the pattern. Then educate → non-pharmacologic → pharmacologic → interventional, and remember the patient is a person.

Differential by structure: bone, ligament or joint (arthritis, subluxation/dislocation, UCL sprain/tear, fracture) · muscle or tendon (medial and lateral epicondylosis, distal biceps, distal triceps) · other (olecranon bursitis, ulnar neuritis). Differential by location: anterior = arthritis, distal biceps · medial = medial epicondylosis, ulnar neuritis, UCL · lateral = lateral epicondylosis · posterior = olecranon bursitis, arthritis, triceps tendinopathy. A fracture could be anywhere.

Joint and ligament problems affect range of motion, stability and pain.

Arthritis of the elbow

Types: degenerative (OA), inflammatory (RA, gout, psoriatic) and post-traumatic (earlier OA, possibly in a different pattern).

  • OA presentation: degenerative history, gradual onset, somatic ache with flares, mechanical symptoms (stiffness, loss of range of motion, locking).
  • X-ray: joint-space loss, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts.
  • Management: educate (a "grumbling", long-term course with no cure) · ice · PT to keep range of motion, OT to keep function · topical anti-inflammatories, oral acetaminophen or NSAIDs (mind the side effects), steroid injection · surgery (debridement or arthroplasty) only if end-stage and intolerable.

Subluxation and dislocation: two joints, two very different patients

Elbow (humeroulnar) dislocation.

  • Mechanism: commonly a FOOSH. Posterior is most common.
  • It is a red-flag condition: capsule and ligament rupture, skin integrity, ulnar nerve at risk (it sits behind the medial epicondyle), and compartment syndrome.
  • Treatment: closed reduction and a splint at 90°. This may fail if soft tissue is interposed or the reduction can't be held, in which case ORIF.

Radial head subluxation ("nursemaid's elbow") at the proximal radioulnar joint.

  • Who: almost exclusively young children (rare after age 5), more often girls and the left arm.
  • Why: the annular ligament is thinner and weaker distally in children.
  • Presentation: acute pain after longitudinal traction on the arm. The child refuses to use the arm and holds it flexed against the body.
  • Management: imaging only to rule out a fracture. Closed reduction by pronation and extension, with no cast or orthosis.

Ulnar collateral ligament injury

  • The UCL stabilizes the medial elbow. Injury is usually from chronic, repetitive overuse, classically baseball pitchers.
  • Presentation: a pop over the medial elbow if it tears suddenly, pain on palpation of the medial elbow, no end point on valgus stress, and excessive motion.
  • Workup: probably needs an exam by sports medicine or orthopaedics. MRI may help.
  • Management: educate (ligaments heal very slowly, if at all) · ice · PT to strengthen the dynamic elbow stabilizers plus proprioception · an orthosis · topical or oral anti-inflammatories. Reconstruction ("Tommy John" surgery, using a tendon graft such as palmaris longus) is only if needed for functional goals, followed by 1–2 years of physiotherapy.

Olecranon bursitis

  • There are more than 150 bursae: thin, flat, fluid-filled sacs that let tissue glide over bone. An irritated bursa fills with fluid, causing pain, tenderness and swelling.
  • The olecranon bursa is the most common superficial bursitis. Causes: one hard blow (a fall on the elbow), sustained or repetitive pressure ("elbow leaners": plumbers, HVAC workers, office jobs; it develops over months), or infection after a skin break.
  • The key task is spotting the bad one. Septic bursitis has the usual signs plus erythema and systemic features. Aspirate it and choose antibiotics (± incision and drainage).
  • "Not scary" bursitis: often no investigations. X-ray if there was trauma, US or MRI if it doesn't behave typically. Gout or inflammatory arthritis can make the whole elbow swell and mimic bursitis, and need their own treatment.
  • Management once red flags are ruled out: educate (benign, self-limited if the cause is removed) · ice, RICE · pad the elbow, stop leaning · topical anti-inflammatories or NSAIDs · bursectomy if chronic or infected.

Bottom line: capitulum ↔ radial head, trochlea ↔ trochlear notch. Scaphoid = most fractured carpal, lunate = most dislocated. A posterior elbow dislocation is a red flag (ulnar nerve, compartment syndrome). Nursemaid's elbow is reduced by pronation and extension with no cast. UCL = medial pain and valgus laxity in a pitcher. A red, hot olecranon bursa gets aspirated.
