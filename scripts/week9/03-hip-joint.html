High-Yield Content

Why this matters: a hip fracture in an older adult is one of the most consequential injuries in medicine. To manage it well you need the anatomy, especially the femoral head's blood supply, which decides how a femoral neck fracture is fixed. You also need a structured approach that asks why the patient fell, not just what broke.

Objective 1: osteology

Hip bone = ilium + ischium + pubis, all of which contribute to the acetabulum.

  • Ilium: the iliac crest (its top is the L4 level, the lumbar puncture landmark), ASIS, AIIS, PSIS, PIIS, and the greater sciatic notch (made into a foramen by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments). The gluteal lines bound the gluteus medius and minimus attachments.
  • Ischium: the ischial spine (sacrospinous ligament), the ischial tuberosity (you sit on it, and the hamstrings start there) and the lesser sciatic notch.
  • Pubis: the pubic tubercle (inguinal ligament) and the superior and inferior rami.
  • Obturator foramen: bounded by the ischium and pubis, it passes the obturator nerve and vessels.
  • Acetabulum: the lunate (articular) surface, the acetabular fossa, and the acetabular notch.

Femur:

  • head, neck, greater trochanter (superolateral) and lesser trochanter (posteromedial)
  • intertrochanteric line (anterior) and intertrochanteric crest (posterior), trochanteric fossa
  • gluteal tuberosity, linea aspera
  • condyles and epicondyles, the adductor tubercle, the intercondylar fossa

Tibia and fibula:

  • The tibial plateau is formed by the condyles. The tibial tuberosity takes the patellar ligament.
  • Gerdy's tubercle takes the IT band. The soleal line is where soleus starts.
  • The fibula bears no weight.

Objective 1: hip joint stability vs the glenohumeral joint

The hip is a ball-and-socket synovial joint, multiaxial. It is the most stable joint in the body:

  • Bony congruency: more than 50% of the femoral head sits in the acetabulum, deepened further by the fibrocartilaginous acetabular labrum. The transverse acetabular ligament bridges the acetabular notch.
  • Stout ligaments, which are thickenings of the capsule:
    • Iliofemoral ("Y ligament of Bigelow"): anterior and superior, the strongest ligament in the body, limits extension.
    • Pubofemoral: anterior and inferior, limits abduction.
    • Ischiofemoral: posterior.
  • The capsule attaches to the intertrochanteric line in front but not to the femur behind. Its fibres wind across the femoral neck, "screwing" the head into the socket in extension and medial rotation.
  • Dynamic support from the surrounding muscles.

The trade-off: the hip sacrifices some mobility for stability, and the glenohumeral joint sacrifices stability for mobility (a shallow glenoid, reliance on the rotator cuff).

The ligament of the head of the femur adds no real strength. Its job is to carry a small artery.

Objective 1: blood supply of the femoral head

  • Medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries (from the profunda femoris) provide most of it, via retinacular arteries. The medial circumflex is the most important.
  • The artery of the ligament of the head (in the ligamentum teres) is the main supply in children but minor in adults.
  • Collateral circulation: the inferior gluteal artery anastomoses with the circumflexes.

Clinical note: a femoral neck fracture can tear the circumflex/retinacular supply. That leaves only the artery of the ligament of the head, which is inadequate, so the result is avascular necrosis of the femoral head. These fractures are especially common in women over 60 with osteoporosis.

Other clinical notes:

  • Posterior hip dislocation (dashboard injury, force along the femur): the limb is shortened and medially rotated, and the sciatic nerve is at risk.
  • Congenital dislocation comes from a malformed acetabulum and/or femur.
  • Hip OA: severe disease ends in replacement.

Objective 2: hip fractures

Definition: "hip fracture" means a proximal femur fracture.

Risk factors amount to anything that raises the risk of falling plus anything that raises the risk of osteoporosis:

  • Intrinsic: previous falls; comorbidities (PVD, COPD, stroke, weakness); polypharmacy (sedatives, diuretics, psychotropics, antiarrhythmics); poor nutrition; poor eyesight.
  • Extrinsic: environmental factors.

A five-step approach:

1. Is this a hip fracture?

  • History: sudden onset after a fall, pain in the groin or lateral upper thigh, sharp or deep ache, worse with movement and weight bearing, possibly radiating to the knee.
  • Keep high suspicion in the elderly even when the pain or trauma is minimal.
  • Exam:
    • Look: the leg is shortened, externally rotated and abducted, and the patient can't weight bear.
    • Feel: tender over the hip.
    • Move: painful ROM, log roll and straight leg raise.
  • Investigations: X-ray (AP pelvis + hip). If the X-ray is negative but suspicion stays high, get a CT.
  • Types:
    • femoral neck (subcapital): intracapsular, threatens the blood supply
    • intertrochanteric
    • subtrochanteric

2. Is the patient stable? Any associated injuries?

  • Especially in a young, high-energy trauma patient: ABCs, pelvic fracture, vascular or nerve injury.
  • Check distal pulses and nerve function, especially the sciatic nerve.

3. Why did they have a hip fracture?

  • Why did they fall? Recent illness or dehydration, syncope or presyncope, cardiac symptoms. Check a CBC, electrolytes, troponin, an ECG (atrial fibrillation?) and a chest X-ray (pneumonia).
  • Why did they fracture? Look for a pathologic fracture (bone tumour). A fragility fracture automatically qualifies as osteoporosis, so start the workup.

4. What's the plan?

  • Stabilize the patient.
  • The vast majority get surgery, because it allows a quicker return to weight bearing and fewer complications of immobility. For displaced femoral neck fractures, surgery is required to protect the femoral head's blood supply.
  • Standard of care: surgical fixation within 24–48 hours.
  • Consider non-operative care only for a very non-displaced femoral neck fracture in a poor surgical candidate.
  • By type:
    • Femoral neck, non-displaced: maybe non-op, maybe screws, or arthroplasty.
    • Femoral neck, displaced: arthroplasty, either hemiarthroplasty (replace the femoral head) or total (head + acetabulum).
    • Intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric: treated the same, essentially all surgically, with an intramedullary nail.

5. After surgery:

  • Prevent complications, which all come back to immobility:
    • VTE: prophylaxis, LMWH for 14–30 days (Thrombosis Canada)
    • pneumonia: a common cause of death
    • muscle atrophy, infection, UTI
    • delirium
    • pressure sores
  • Early mobilization with interdisciplinary rehab.
  • Falls prevention: optimize comorbidities, fix environmental factors, OT/PT and gait aids.
  • Fracture prevention: vitamin D and calcium, a secondary osteoporosis workup, antiresorptive therapy.

Bottom line:

  • The hip trades mobility for stability: >50% head coverage, the labrum, and the iliofemoral "Y" ligament (strongest in the body). The shoulder makes the opposite trade.
  • The medial circumflex femoral artery feeds the femoral head, so a displaced neck fracture risks AVN and gets arthroplasty.
  • Hip fracture: shortened, externally rotated leg. If the X-ray is negative and suspicion stays high, get a CT. Fix within 24–48 h.
  • Always ask why the patient fell and why the bone broke. Then prevent VTE, delirium and the next fracture.
