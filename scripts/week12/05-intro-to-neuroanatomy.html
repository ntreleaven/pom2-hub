High-Yield Content

Why it matters: knowing where things are lets you localize a lesion from signs and symptoms — and knowing the meninges and their spaces explains epidural, subdural and subarachnoid bleeds.

1. Terms and divisions

Structural division:

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord, in the skull and vertebral canal, protected by meninges and CSF.
  • PNS = spinal nerves, cranial nerves, ganglia, sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves.
  • Grey matter = cell bodies. White matter = axons, no cell bodies.
  • In the brain, grey matter is also on the outside as a cortex (cerebrum and cerebellum). Other grey matter clusters are nuclei.

Functional division: sensory input (afferent, to the CNS) → integration → motor output (efferent, to muscles and glands). A pain withdrawal reflex doesn't need the brain; planning does.

Directions: rostral–caudal, dorsal–ventral, superior–inferior, anterior–posterior. Planes: coronal (dorsal to ventral), axial/horizontal (parallel to the horizon), sagittal (front to back).

Brain divisions:

  • Forebrain: telencephalon (cerebral hemispheres + deep nuclei) and diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus, subthalamus, epithalamus)
  • Midbrain
  • Hindbrain: pons, medulla and cerebellum
  • Brainstem = midbrain + pons + medulla

2. Neuroimaging

Modality What it shows

| X-ray | Bone only; nervous tissue invisible | | CT | Sectional, ~2 mm; lesions differ in density from brain | | MRI T1 | Brain tissue vs fluid; some grey–white difference | | MRI T2 | CSF bright; less grey–white contrast | | FLAIR | CSF dark; best for white matter abnormalities |

3. Cerebral cortex and lobes

  • The cerebrum is about 80% of brain mass.
  • Longitudinal fissure splits right and left; transverse fissure separates cerebrum from cerebellum. Sulci are grooves, gyri are ridges.
  • Central sulcus separates frontal from parietal. Lateral sulcus sits above the temporal lobe. Parieto-occipital sulcus separates parietal from occipital.
Lobe Functions

| Frontal (anterior cranial fossa) | Voluntary and planned movement (primary motor cortex), eye movement, speech production | | Parietal | Somatosensory processing, spatial awareness, understanding speech | | Temporal (middle cranial fossa) | Auditory and olfactory cortex; recognizing objects, words, faces | | Occipital | Visual cortex | | Insula | Visceral sensation, taste (gustatory cortex) | | Limbic lobe (medial: cingulate and parahippocampal gyri) | Memory and emotion |

White matter tracts connect cortex to cortex, brainstem and cord. The corpus callosum links the two hemispheres.

4. Basal ganglia and cerebellum

  • Basal ganglia = deep forebrain nuclei (caudate, putamen = striatum; with the substantia nigra of the midbrain) that integrate cortical activity into one motor output. They regulate movement intensity, rhythmic repetitive tasks and habit learning.
  • Basal ganglia disease → movement disorders: too little movement (Parkinson's — substantia nigra) or too much (chorea, e.g. Huntington's — caudate).
  • Basal forebrain nuclei make acetylcholine (arousal, learning, memory); their degeneration is linked to Alzheimer's.
  • Cerebellum (posterior to the brainstem) coordinates movement. It connects to the brainstem through the superior, middle and inferior cerebellar peduncles.

5. Diencephalon

  • Thalamus: relay for all conscious senses except smell. Everything reaching the cortex relays through a thalamic nucleus. Also links to the limbic system and basal ganglia.
  • Hypothalamus: main visceral control centre — autonomic function, temperature, hunger and thirst, sleep, emotion, and the endocrine system.
  • Epithalamus: pineal gland → melatonin.

6. Brainstem

Rostral to caudal: midbrain → pons → medulla. It carries the tracts between cerebrum and cord, innervates the face and neck (10 of 12 cranial nerves attach here) and runs survival reflexes.

"Rule of 4" for cranial nerve nuclei:

Level CN nuclei Key features

| Midbrain | III, IV | Cerebral peduncles; superior colliculi (visual reflexes) and inferior colliculi (auditory reflexes) | | Pons | V, VI, VII, VIII | Pontine nuclei relay cerebrum ↔ cerebellum | | Medulla | IX, X, XI, XII (XI nucleus is in the upper cervical cord) | Cardiac, vasomotor, respiratory centres; pyramids with decussation (each hemisphere controls the opposite body); olives |

Cranial nerves I–XII: olfactory, optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, abducens, facial, vestibulocochlear, glossopharyngeal, vagus, spinal accessory, hypoglossal.

7. Spinal cord

  • Dorsal grey horn = sensory; ventral grey horn = motor.
  • Dorsal rootlets (sensory) and ventral rootlets (motor) → dorsal and ventral roots → spinal nerve → dorsal and ventral rami.

8. Ventricles and CSF

  • Two C-shaped lateral ventricles (anterior, posterior and inferior horns) → third ventricle (in the diencephalon) → cerebral aqueduct (through the midbrain) → fourth ventricle (between brainstem and cerebellum) → openings into the subarachnoid space.
  • CSF is made by ependymal cells of the choroid plexus.

9. Vessels and barriers

  • Blood–brain barrier: tight junctions, basement membrane, astrocyte foot processes, pericytes. Lipid-soluble substances cross by diffusion; others need active transport.
  • Middle meningeal artery (from the external carotid) supplies the dura; rupture → epidural hematoma.
  • Dural venous sinuses: superior sagittal, inferior sagittal, straight, transverse, sigmoid.
