Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Interpret plain radiographs of fractures
    • Describe fractures using a systematic approach (description of radiographic views provided and fracture features including location, complexity, pattern, displacement/deformity, joint surface involvement, other findings)
    • List any specific views that may be helpful in identifying and characterizing fractures or other pathology (ie. dislocations) of specific bones/joints.
    • Define typical timelines for fracture healing and describe the basic appearance of a healing fracture on X-ray

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: a good fracture description lets an orthopedic surgeon picture the injury over the phone. Each fracture gets more than one descriptor. The body is always assumed to be in anatomic position.

The mnemonic: OLD ACIDS

  • Open or closed
  • Location
  • Degree: complete or incomplete
  • Articular surface involvement
  • Comminution and types
  • Intrinsic bone quality
  • Displacement: the descriptors
  • Soft tissue injury

Fracture description order

Views, location, type, descriptors, joint involvement, other findings, bone quality, healing.

Views

  • Ankle mortise view
  • Scaphoid: ulnar deviation, clenched fist view
  • Shoulder: Y view
  • Spiral fractures: need multiple views to see the spiral

Location

Epiphysis, physis, metaphysis, diaphysis, and apophysis, a non-articular bony prominence where muscles or tendons attach, exposed to traction (calcaneal tuberosity). Note mature or immature skeleton.

Types

Complete:

  • Transverse
  • Oblique
  • Spiral: twisting injury; can look oblique
  • Longitudinal/linear
  • Comminuted: including segmental and butterfly fragment

Incomplete: bowing, buckle, greenstick. Salter-Harris injuries involve growth plates in the immature skeleton.

Others: avulsion, open.

Displacement descriptors

Rule: describe the distal fragment relative to the proximal. The exception is the clavicle: proximal relative to distal, because neck muscles pull the proximal fragment.

  • Angulation: by the direction of the apex, or the direction the distal fragment points
    • Varus: apex lateral, angulation away from the midline
    • Valgus: apex medial, angulation toward the midline
  • Translation (displacement): ends moved apart, in terms of bone width (half shaft width). If it exceeds the width of the bone, it's off-ended.
  • Rotation: distal relative to proximal; hard to see, so check joints above and below. Internal or external.
  • Distraction: fragments pulled apart lengthwise
  • Impaction: fragments driven into each other; foreshortened, "telescoped in"
  • Override: fragments overlap; foreshortened

⚠ The slide also says the distal fragment is "directed laterally" in varus and "medially" in valgus. Standard usage is the reverse, so learn these by the apex, and check this against the course.

Joint involvement

Intra-articular Extra-articular
Involves the articular surface Doesn't involve the joint
Risk of secondary OA
Reduction must be anatomic Reduction can be relatively anatomic
Longer to heal Bones remodel, particularly with weight bearing

If you can't see the fracture, look for secondary signs: hemarthrosis and a fat-fluid level. Also look for subluxation or dislocation.

Other findings

  • Open fractures
  • Periprosthetic fractures
  • Amputation
  • Diastasis: widening or incongruence at a joint, with or without fracture (pubic symphysis, AC joint)

Bone quality

Pathological fracture: in weakened bone, usually transverse, spontaneous or after trivial trauma.

Healing

  • Primary (contact): directly apposed surfaces, as in internal fixation; little callus
  • Secondary: natural stages, most common
  • Clinical healing: no tenderness, no motion, weight bearing possible
  • X-ray: fracture line widens at 10–14 days; callus at 2 weeks; radiological union shows mature bridging callus with remodelling and a restored medullary space
  • Upper limb: 3–10 weeks, longest in distal humerus (8–10)
  • Lower limb: 6–12 weeks, longest in femoral neck and shaft
  • Delayed union: twice the expected time
  • Nonunion: no healing in 6–9 months
  • Malunion: non-anatomic healing

Take-home: trace the cortex, look for secondary signs, and use the descriptors.
