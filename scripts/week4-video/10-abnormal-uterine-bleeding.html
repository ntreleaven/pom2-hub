Abnormal Uterine Bleeding — Video Script

Duration target: ~10-11 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Define the normal amount of bleeding in a menstrual cycle
  • List the components of PALM-COEIN
  • Identify main causes of anovulation
  • Analyze pelvic examination findings using an understanding of causes of AUB
  • Identify the structural causes of AUB on pathology
  • Describe the clinical presentations of cervical cancer or endometrial cancer with regards to AUB
  • List investigations used in the diagnosis of AUB
  • Select appropriate medical treatments for AUB

What counts as normal

A normal menstrual cycle runs 21–35 days, with bleeding lasting up to about 8 days and total blood loss under 80mL per cycle. In practice, blood loss is judged clinically rather than measured — bleeding that regularly soaks through protection every hour or so, or that includes clots larger than a loonie, is a practical marker of heavy menstrual bleeding worth investigating. Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) is any deviation from that pattern in frequency, regularity, duration, or volume.

PALM-COEIN — the classification framework to memorize

FIGO's PALM-COEIN system splits causes into structural (PALM) and non-structural (COEIN):

Structural (PALM):

  • Polyp
  • Adenomyosis
  • Leiomyoma (fibroids)
  • Malignancy and hyperplasia

Non-structural (COEIN):

  • Coagulopathy
  • Ovulatory dysfunction
  • Endometrial (a primary disorder of local endometrial hemostasis, a diagnosis of exclusion)
  • Iatrogenic (anticoagulants, hormonal contraception, IUDs)
  • Not otherwise classified

This framework is the organizing structure for the entire workup — every AUB case gets systematically worked through these nine categories rather than approached ad hoc.

Anovulation — the main causes

Ovulatory dysfunction is one of the most common causes of AUB, and the major drivers are: PCOS (the single most common cause of chronic anovulation in reproductive-age women), thyroid dysfunction (both hyper- and hypothyroidism disrupt normal cyclicity), hyperprolactinemia (suppresses GnRH pulsatility), extremes of the reproductive lifespan (perimenarchal, when the HPO axis is still maturing, and perimenopausal, as ovarian function declines), significant weight change or excessive exercise (hypothalamic suppression), and stress. Anovulatory bleeding is classically irregular and unpredictable in timing and volume, because without a corpus luteum there's no organized progesterone withdrawal to trigger a coordinated shed — instead the endometrium builds up under unopposed estrogen and sheds erratically.

The pelvic exam, interpreted through the PALM-COEIN lens

A bimanual exam and speculum exam should be interpreted with the differential actively in mind: an enlarged, irregular uterus suggests fibroids; a diffusely enlarged, boggy, tender uterus suggests adenomyosis; a visible cervical lesion needs biopsy to exclude cervical cancer; and a normal-feeling uterus with heavy bleeding points more toward a non-structural cause (coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, or an endometrial disorder) — pushing the workup toward bloodwork and endometrial sampling rather than imaging.

Structural causes on pathology

Polyps are focal overgrowths of endometrial glands and stroma around a vascular core — usually benign, causing intermenstrual bleeding by disrupting the overlying endometrial surface. Adenomyosis is ectopic endometrial glands and stroma within the myometrium, causing a diffusely enlarged, tender uterus with heavy, painful menses. Leiomyomas (fibroids) are benign smooth muscle tumors — their impact on bleeding depends heavily on location, with submucosal fibroids (distorting the endometrial cavity directly) causing the most bleeding, far more than subserosal fibroids on the outer uterine surface. Malignancy and hyperplasia reflect unopposed estrogen exposure driving abnormal endometrial proliferation, ranging from hyperplasia without atypia (low malignant potential) to atypical hyperplasia (a significant precursor lesion) to frank endometrial carcinoma.

Cervical and endometrial cancer presentations

Cervical cancer classically presents with postcoital bleeding and intermenstrual bleeding — reflecting a friable exophytic lesion on the cervix that bleeds with contact — which is why any postcoital bleeding warrants direct cervical visualization, not just a screening Pap. Endometrial cancer classically presents as postmenopausal bleeding — any bleeding after menopause is endometrial cancer until proven otherwise, given roughly a 10% baseline risk of malignancy in that population — or, in a premenopausal patient, as persistent intermenstrual bleeding or AUB in someone with unopposed estrogen risk factors (obesity, PCOS, nulliparity, tamoxifen use).

Investigations

Bloodwork: CBC (assessing for anemia from chronic blood loss), TSH, prolactin, and a coagulation screen when history suggests a bleeding disorder (heavy bleeding since menarche, family history, or bleeding at other sites) — von Willebrand disease specifically is underdiagnosed in patients with heavy menstrual bleeding and worth actively considering. Pregnancy test in any reproductive-age patient, always, before anything else. Pelvic ultrasound (often transvaginal) to assess for fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, and endometrial thickness. Endometrial biopsy is indicated for anyone at increased risk of hyperplasia or malignancy — generally patients over 40–45 with AUB, or younger patients with persistent unopposed estrogen exposure (obesity, PCOS, chronic anovulation) — and is mandatory for any postmenopausal bleeding. Sonohysterogram or hysteroscopy can further characterize an intracavitary lesion identified on ultrasound.

Medical treatment options

For heavy bleeding without a structural cause requiring surgery: the hormonal IUD (LNG-IUS) is first-line — highly effective at reducing bleeding, often achieving amenorrhea over time; combined hormonal contraception is another good option, both controlling bleeding and providing contraception; tranexamic acid (non-hormonal, taken only during menses) reduces blood loss by 30–50% through its antifibrinolytic mechanism, ideal for patients wanting to avoid hormones; NSAIDs both reduce blood loss modestly and treat associated dysmenorrhea; and cyclic or continuous progestins can be used to oppose estrogen and stabilize the endometrium, particularly useful in anovulatory bleeding.

For structural causes, medical management may still be first-line depending on severity (fibroids and polyps can sometimes be managed medically first), but definitive treatment is procedural — hysteroscopic polypectomy or myomectomy, uterine artery embolization for fibroids, or endometrial ablation/hysterectomy for refractory cases where childbearing is complete.

For hyperplasia, progestin therapy (oral, or via the LNG-IUS) is first-line for hyperplasia without atypia; atypical hyperplasia usually requires hysterectomy or, in patients wishing to preserve fertility, high-dose progestin therapy with close surveillance biopsies.

Bringing it together

PALM-COEIN gives you a complete, memorable differential for any AUB presentation. The clinical workflow that follows from it is consistent: rule out pregnancy first, always; then use history and exam to weight the differential toward structural versus non-structural causes; confirm with targeted bloodwork, imaging, and biopsy when malignancy risk factors are present; and match treatment to the specific cause identified — hormonal management for functional/ovulatory causes, tranexamic acid or NSAIDs as non-hormonal options, and procedural intervention for structural lesions that don't respond to medical therapy.
