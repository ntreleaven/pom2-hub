Estrogens, progestins, and androgens — the pharmacologic basics

Estrogens act through estrogen receptors alpha and beta, driving endometrial proliferation, supporting bone density, and providing negative feedback on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis at physiologic levels (and positive feedback triggering the LH surge at the mid-cycle peak). Clinically we use estrogens — usually ethinyl estradiol in contraceptives, or estradiol in menopausal hormone therapy — for contraception, cycle control, and symptom management.

Progestins act on progesterone receptors to convert a proliferative endometrium into a secretory one, thicken cervical mucus, and suppress the LH surge (blocking ovulation). Different progestins vary in their androgenic activity — levonorgestrel and desogestrel sit at different points on that spectrum — which is why side-effect profiles differ between formulations even when the estrogen dose is identical.

Androgens (testosterone and its derivatives) act through the androgen receptor; in gynecology they're relevant both as agents to be blocked (in hirsutism, PCOS, or acne management) and, less commonly, as therapy (testosterone for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in select patients).

Combined contraceptive products

Combined hormonal contraceptives pair an estrogen (almost always ethinyl estradiol) with a progestin, delivered by three routes: oral pill, transdermal patch, or vaginal ring. The progestin is the primary contraceptive agent — suppressing ovulation and thickening cervical mucus — while the estrogen mainly stabilizes the endometrium to control breakthrough bleeding. Formulations vary in estrogen dose (as low as 10mcg up to 35mcg) and in progestin type, which lets prescribers tailor a pill to a patient's side-effect profile (for example, higher-androgenicity progestins for a patient prioritizing acne control, or lower-estrogen formulations for someone prone to breast tenderness or headaches).

Comparing contraceptive categories

  • Combined hormonal contraception (pill/patch/ring): most effective as ovulation suppressants when taken correctly, but estrogen brings a real (if small) VTE risk and a list of absolute contraindications — smoking after 35, migraine with aura, uncontrolled hypertension, active VTE, or history of certain cancers.
  • Progesterone-only contraception (mini-pill): appropriate when estrogen is contraindicated, but less forgiving of missed doses since it doesn't reliably suppress ovulation in all users.
  • Long-acting progestins (DMPA injection, the subdermal implant, the hormonal IUD): don't require daily adherence, and are among the most effective reversible methods available; DMPA specifically causes a hypoestrogenic state with a reversible early effect on bone density.
  • Emergency contraception: a short, high-dose exposure meant to delay or prevent ovulation (levonorgestrel, ulipristal) or to work through a direct anti-implantation/spermicidal effect (copper IUD) — fundamentally different pharmacology from ongoing contraception, and time-sensitive.

SERMs and SPRMs in breast cancer and endometriosis

Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) — tamoxifen is the classic example — act as estrogen antagonists in breast tissue (useful in ER-positive breast cancer) while behaving as partial agonists in the endometrium and bone, which is why tamoxifen carries an endometrial cancer risk even as it treats breast cancer.

Selective progesterone receptor modulators (SPRMs) — ulipristal acetate is the one you already met as emergency contraception — block progesterone's action on target tissue. In gynecology beyond emergency contraception, SPRMs and related hormonal-suppression strategies (GnRH agonists/antagonists like Lupron or Orilissa) are used to induce a reversible medical menopause in endometriosis, shrinking estrogen-dependent lesions by removing the hormonal signal that drives them.

Androgen agonists and antagonists

Androgen antagonists — spironolactone is the workhorse in gynecology — block the androgen receptor and are used off-label for hirsutism and hormonal acne in PCOS. Cyproterone acetate (found in some contraceptive formulations, like Diane-35) has combined antiandrogenic and progestogenic activity, useful for the same indications. On the agonist side, androgens are occasionally used therapeutically for confirmed androgen deficiency states, always weighed against virilizing side effects.

The menstrual cycle, briefly

Two phases, driven by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis: the follicular phase (rising FSH recruits a dominant follicle, which produces estradiol; the mid-cycle estradiol peak triggers the LH surge, causing ovulation), and the luteal phase (the corpus luteum produces progesterone, maintaining a secretory endometrium; if no implantation occurs, the corpus luteum regresses, progesterone falls, and menstruation follows). This axis is the target of essentially every hormonal contraceptive discussed above — each one interrupts it at a different point.

NSAIDs and tranexamic acid for menstrual symptoms

NSAIDs (mefenamic acid, ibuprofen, naproxen) inhibit cyclooxygenase, reducing endometrial prostaglandin production — this both relieves dysmenorrhea (prostaglandins drive the uterine cramping) and modestly reduces menstrual blood loss, since prostaglandins also affect endometrial vascular tone. Tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic — it blocks plasminogen activation, stabilizing clots within the endometrium and reducing heavy menstrual bleeding by 30–50%, without any hormonal effect at all. That makes it a useful non-hormonal option for patients who want to avoid hormonal contraception but still need heavy bleeding controlled, and the two agents (NSAIDs and tranexamic acid) can be combined during menses for both pain and blood loss.

Closing

The unifying idea across this whole module is that gynecologic pharmacology mostly works by manipulating one axis — the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis — at different points and to different degrees: full suppression (combined contraceptives, GnRH agonists), partial suppression (progestin-only methods), tissue-selective modulation (SERMs, SPRMs), or symptom-targeted non-hormonal approaches (NSAIDs, tranexamic acid). Once you can place a drug on that map, its indications, benefits, and risks tend to follow logically.
