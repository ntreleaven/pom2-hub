Learning Objectives

  • Build a differential diagnosis for structural causes of AUB & pelvic pain based on knowledge of anatomy and pathology
  • Narrow differential diagnosis based on age, common reasons & clues in history & physical examination
  • Compose an oral narrative which explains to patients reasons for AUB & pelvic pain

This is a synthesis session, not a new-content session

This module is explicitly about applying the anatomy and pathology you already know — from the AUB module, the pelvic pain modules, and the histology/anatomy blocks earlier in your training — to build differentials from first principles, rather than learning new disease entities. Treat this as a framework for clinical reasoning.

Building the differential from anatomy

The most efficient way to generate a structural differential for AUB or pelvic pain is to work through the pelvic organs systematically by layer and location, asking "what pathology can arise here, and would it cause bleeding, pain, or both":

  • Endometrium: polyps (bleeding, rarely pain), hyperplasia/malignancy (bleeding, pain only if advanced), chronic endometritis (bleeding and pain).
  • Myometrium: fibroids — bleeding if submucosal/cavity-distorting, pain if degenerating or large enough to cause bulk symptoms; adenomyosis — both heavy bleeding and cyclic pain from ectopic endometrial tissue within the muscle.
  • Ovary/adnexa: functional cysts (usually asymptomatic or mild pain, rarely bleeding), endometriomas (pain, sometimes AUB from associated hormonal disruption), torsion (acute severe pain, not bleeding), tubo-ovarian abscess (pain plus systemic symptoms).
  • Peritoneum/pelvic sidewall: endometriosis implants (cyclic and sometimes chronic pain, not typically AUB directly), adhesions from prior surgery or infection (chronic pain, not bleeding).
  • Cervix: polyps or malignancy (postcoital/intermenstrual bleeding, pain usually only with advanced disease).

Working this way — organ by organ, asking what's structurally possible there — reliably generates the same differential as PALM-COEIN, but builds it from anatomic reasoning rather than memorization, which is the actual clinical skill being tested.

Narrowing by age and history clues

Age reframes probability dramatically. In an adolescent, AUB is disproportionately anovulatory (immature HPO axis) or, if severe from menarche, a bleeding disorder like von Willebrand disease; structural pathology and malignancy are rare. In a reproductive-age adult, the full PALM-COEIN differential is in play, weighted by history — cyclic pain worsening over time suggests endometriosis or adenomyosis, acute unilateral pain suggests a cyst accident or torsion, fever and discharge suggest PID. In a perimenopausal patient, anovulatory bleeding from erratic ovarian function becomes common again, but this is also the age band where hyperplasia and early endometrial cancer start entering the differential meaningfully, so the threshold for endometrial sampling drops. In a postmenopausal patient, any bleeding is malignancy until proven otherwise, and new pelvic pain warrants a similarly heightened index of suspicion for malignancy over benign causes.

Specific history clues that shift probabilities efficiently: postcoital bleeding points to the cervix; bleeding since menarche with easy bruising or family history points to coagulopathy; cyclic pain that's progressively worsening points to endometriosis/adenomyosis; acute-onset unilateral pain points to an ovarian event (cyst rupture, torsion); pain with fever and abnormal discharge points to infection; and a change in a previously stable chronic pattern (new bleeding pattern, new pain character) should always raise concern rather than being attributed to "the same old thing."

Composing the patient narrative

The objective explicitly asks you to be able to explain the reasoning to a patient, not just hold it in your head — and that's a distinct skill worth practicing deliberately. A useful structure: (1) name what's happening in plain language ("your uterus lining is being told to grow by one hormone but isn't being told to stop and shed regularly, so it builds up and then bleeds irregularly"), (2) connect it to what you found on history/exam/tests ("your ultrasound showed a normal-looking uterus, which fits with this being a hormonal pattern rather than a growth"), (3) explain what happens next and why ("we'll try an IUD first because it directly treats the lining, with fewer side effects than pills"). Avoiding jargon (say "uterine lining" not "endometrium," "womb" if that's the patient's preferred term) and checking understanding are part of the actual clinical competency being assessed here, not just bedside manner.

Bringing it together

This module is a rehearsal, not new material: take the anatomy you know, walk through each pelvic structure asking what pathology could arise there and whether it causes bleeding, pain, or both; use the patient's age and specific history clues to weight that differential toward what's actually likely; and then practice translating that clinical reasoning into a plain-language explanation a patient can actually use to understand their own body and the plan being offered to them.
