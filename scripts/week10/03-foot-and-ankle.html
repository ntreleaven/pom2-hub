High-Yield Content

Why this matters: foot and ankle problems are common, and the management theme repeats: education first, because improvement is slow.

Functional anatomy

Bones: seven tarsals (talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, three cuneiforms), metatarsals, phalanges. Sustentaculum tali supports the talus. The 5th metatarsal tuberosity takes fibularis brevis, and a quick inversion can avulse it.

Dorsum: extensor retinacula. EDB and EHB originate on the calcaneus and are supplied by the deep fibular nerve.

Sole: plantar aponeurosis protects the sole and supports the arches. Four muscle layers work as a group during stance.

Ankle (talocrural): hinge. Most stable in dorsiflexion (wide anterior talus wedged in the mortise), least stable in plantarflexion.

Ligaments:

  • Lateral (weak): anterior talofibular, posterior talofibular, calcaneofibular
  • Medial (deltoid, strong): anterior and posterior tibiotalar, tibiocalcaneal, tibionavicular

Subtalar joint: plane joint; most inversion and eversion happen here. Transverse tarsal joint: calcaneocuboid and talonavicular.

Spring ligament (calcaneonavicular) supports the talar head, the keystone of the medial arch.

Arches: medial longitudinal is higher (tibialis anterior, tibialis posterior, fibularis longus); lateral longitudinal is flat; transverse runs across the cuneiforms, cuboid and metatarsal bases.

Nice to know (grey box): the long plantar ligament runs from the calcaneus to the cuboid, with fibres to the metatarsal bases.

Vessels and nerves

  • Dorsalis pedis: continuation of anterior tibial; gives lateral tarsal, arcuate and deep plantar arteries. A weak or absent pulse suggests vascular insufficiency. Five P's: pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, pulselessness.
  • Posterior tibial: through the tarsal tunnel, splits into medial and lateral plantar arteries. Feel its pulse between the medial malleolus and calcaneal tendon.
  • Collateral: the deep plantar arch joins lateral plantar and deep plantar arteries, so sole wounds bleed from both ends.
  • Veins: plantar network to dorsal network, then great and small saphenous veins.
  • Superficial fibular nerve: sensation over the dorsum, except the first web space.
  • Deep fibular nerve: dorsal foot muscles and the first web space.
  • Tibial nerve: through the tarsal tunnel to medial and lateral plantar nerves.

Ottawa Foot and Ankle Rules

Ankle X-ray is required only with pain in the malleolar zone and any of:

  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the lateral malleolus (distal 6 cm)
  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the medial malleolus (distal 6 cm)
  • Inability to bear weight both immediately and in the ED

Foot X-ray is required only with pain in the midfoot zone and any of:

  • Bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal
  • Bone tenderness at the navicular
  • Inability to bear weight both immediately and in the ED

Ankle sprain

  • Up to 85% are lateral, in inversion and plantarflexion: ATFL > CFL > PTFL
  • Swelling, ecchymosis, tenderness over the ligament; anterior drawer tests the ATFL
  • Management: education, RICE, brief immobilization if needed, early mobilization, strengthening, proprioception, NSAIDs; reconstruction only for ongoing instability

When it isn't just a sprain:

  • Unimalleolar: may be non-operative, depending on talar stability
  • Bimalleolar: forceful eversion; usually surgery
  • Trimalleolar: unstable; surgery
  • Maisonneuve: forceful external rotation, interosseous ligament disruption; usually surgery

Achilles

Tendinopathy: runners after increased training; pain at the posterior heel, worse with passive dorsiflexion. Education, activity modification, footwear, cautious heel wedge, PT with eccentric strengthening, NSAIDs. Surgery is rare.

Rupture: active people in their 30s–40s. Surgery may slightly lower re-rupture but adds infection, wound and sural nerve risks, so share the decision.

Plantar fasciitis

Chronic insertional inflammation. Pain at the anteromedial calcaneus on first morning steps and with activity; typically 40–60, F>M; often a tight Achilles. Cushioned heel pad, arch support, weight loss, stretching and strengthening, night splints, NSAIDs. Surgical release is very rare.

Tibialis posterior dysfunction

Hypovascular tendon distal to the medial malleolus. Risks: trauma, steroids, inflammatory arthritis, obesity, diabetes. Spectrum: medial pain, weakness, flexible then rigid flat foot. "Too many toes" sign. RICE, physio, orthosis, NSAIDs; surgery by severity.

Bunions and Morton's neuroma

Bunion (hallux valgus): footwear and metatarsus varus. Pain at the medial first MTP, secondary toe deformities. Wider toe box, positioning if flexible, surgery. Treat early.

Morton's neuroma: perineural fibrosis of a common digital nerve, usually between the 3rd and 4th metatarsals. Middle-aged, F>M; burning, provoked by high heels and narrow toe boxes; squeeze test; sensory findings possible, no motor findings. Lower heels, wider toe box, metatarsal pad, neuropathic agents, steroid injection, excision.

Diabetic foot

Poor blood flow starves skin and nerves. Neuropathy means unnoticed injury, dry skin and intrinsic atrophy, and possibly Charcot arthropathy. Ulcers occur in 15% of patients with diabetes. Debridement or amputation may be needed. Prevention is key: monofilament testing, appropriate socks and shoes, daily foot checks with mirrors.
