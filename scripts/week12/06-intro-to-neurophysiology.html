Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

Upon completing this module, students will be able to:

  • Use basic neurophysiology to predict how disruption of normal physiology can cause symptoms and signs
  • Describe the microscopic organization of the nervous system (neurons, axons, dendrites, action potentials, receptors, inhibition, excitation).

High-Yield Content

The theme: neurons talk with all-or-none action potentials and chemical synapses. Break the myelin and signals slow (MS); change a receptor and you have a drug effect; damage a specialized cortical area and you get a specific deficit.

1. The brain as a network

  • About 100 billion neurons and 10× as many glia. Each neuron contacts about 1,000 others; memories live in these connections.
  • Neurons sit in a thin, folded sheet of grey matter; axon tracts form the white matter.
  • The brain is 1–3% of body mass but uses ~20% of the body's energy, so only a small fraction of neurons fire at once.
  • Grey matter needs more oxygen, and blood flows to the active areas — the basis of fMRI. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) shows axon tracts.
  • Dense interconnection is why epilepsy spreads: abnormal activity in one spot can spread into a seizure.

2. The neuron

  • Excitable cells (neurons and muscle) generate and respond to electrical signals.
  • Soma (cell body); dendrites collect and integrate input; axon sends signals long distances; synapse contacts other neurons and is the site of many drugs and toxins.
  • Resting potential ≈ −65 mV (inside negative). Depolarization = less negative; hyperpolarization = more negative.
  • An action potential starts at the axon hillock when depolarization reaches threshold (~−55 mV), then travels all the way to the terminal. In health it's all-or-none.
  • Myelin greatly speeds conduction: saltatory conduction jumps from node to node. Demyelinating diseases such as MS make conduction slow and unreliable.

3. How information is coded

Because each spike is all-or-none, the pattern of spikes carries the message. For a touch:

  • How hard? → firing frequency on one axon, plus how many axons fire (frequency coding)
  • How long? → duration of firing
  • Where? → which axons (each comes from a different body part)
  • What kind? → which receptor the axon connects to (heat, cold, pain, light vs heavy touch)

Labeled-line coding: each modality has dedicated pathways, and the brain assigns meaning by pathway (spikes in the optic nerve are "seen").

4. Chemical synapses

  1. The action potential reaches the terminal → Ca²⁺-mediated release of neurotransmitter.
  2. Transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors.
  3. Transmitter is cleared by diffusion, reuptake or enzymatic breakdown.

Two receptor classes:

Ionotropic Metabotropic

| Mechanism | Binding opens an ion channel | Second messenger (e.g. G-protein coupled) | | Effect | Fast and local | Slower, widespread, longer-lasting | | Examples | Glutamate (NMDA, AMPA), GABA-A, ACh at the NMJ | Dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine |

Excitation and inhibition:

  • EPSP = brief depolarization. Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter in the brain. NMDA receptors are modifiable → synaptic plasticity, the basis of learning and memory.
  • Acetylcholine is the excitatory transmitter at the neuromuscular junction.
  • IPSP = brief hyperpolarization. GABA is the main inhibitory transmitter in the brain (target of many sedatives). Glycine is the inhibitory transmitter in the spinal cord.

5. Neuropharmacology

  • Drugs act as receptor agonists or antagonists, or change reuptake or breakdown.
  • More than 50 transmitters in three classes:
    • Amino acids: GABA, glutamate, glycine
    • Amines: acetylcholine, dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin
    • Peptides: substance P (pain and inflammation), dynorphin (opioid)
  • GABA-A receptor (ionotropic, fast hyperpolarization) has many binding sites that influence each other: benzodiazepines act more strongly when ethanol is present. Ethanol also antagonizes glutamate receptors.

6. Circuits and the cortex

  • Divergence: one neuron reaches many → motor control (coordinated actions, reflexes).
  • Convergence: many neurons reach one → sensory feature extraction.
  • Six cortical layers. Input arrives in layer 4 (from the thalamus) — thickest in primary sensory cortex (V1 is "striate cortex" because of its thick layer 4). Output leaves from layers 3 and 5 — thickest in primary motor cortex.
  • Brodmann divided cortex into areas by anatomy; each has a specific function (functional localization), so lesions can cause very specific deficits.

7. Two classic lesions

Wernicke's aphasia Broca's aphasia

| Site | Left posterior temporal cortex | Left frontal cortex | | Speech | Fluent but nonsense words | Slow, effortful | | Comprehension | Impaired | Intact | | Type | Perceptual syndrome | Motor syndrome | | Awareness | Often unaware | Aware of the deficit |

Predicting symptoms from physiology

  • Demyelination → slowed or blocked conduction → weakness, numbness, visual loss (MS).
  • Drugs that boost GABA (benzodiazepines, alcohol) → sedation; together they add up.
  • Spreading abnormal cortical activity → seizure.
