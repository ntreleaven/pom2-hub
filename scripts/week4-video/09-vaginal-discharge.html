Vaginal Discharge — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Laura Sovran | Duration on Elentra: 25 min

Duration target: ~7 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the changes in vaginal discharge over the menstrual cycle
  • Describe the assessment of vaginal discharge

Normal discharge across the cycle

Vaginal discharge is a normal, hormonally-driven physiologic process — not automatically a sign of pathology — and its character changes predictably across the menstrual cycle, tracking estrogen and progesterone levels. Right after menses, discharge is typically minimal and thick. As estrogen rises through the follicular phase approaching ovulation, cervical mucus becomes progressively more abundant, clear, and stretchy — the classic "egg-white" quality that peaks right around ovulation, when it's actually functionally optimized to help sperm transport. After ovulation, under progesterone's influence in the luteal phase, discharge becomes thicker, stickier, and more opaque again, decreasing in overall quantity. Recognizing this normal pattern is what lets you distinguish physiologic discharge from something that needs treatment — patients often present worried about "abnormal" discharge that's simply mid-cycle ovulatory mucus.

A structured assessment approach

When a patient presents with a discharge complaint, a systematic assessment covers:

History: onset and duration, color, consistency, odor, associated itching or irritation, relationship to the menstrual cycle, sexual activity and new partners, contraceptive method (some methods alter vaginal flora), recent antibiotic use (predisposes to candidiasis), and any systemic symptoms (fever, pelvic pain) that might suggest ascending infection.

Examination: speculum exam to directly assess discharge characteristics (color, consistency, odor, amount, and where along the vaginal walls or cervix it's coming from), plus assessment for cervical motion tenderness or adnexal tenderness that would suggest upper genital tract involvement rather than simple vaginitis.

Testing: vaginal pH (normal is under 4.5; elevated pH points toward bacterial vaginosis or trichomoniasis), the whiff test (an amine/fishy odor on adding KOH suggests bacterial vaginosis), and microscopy of a wet mount — looking for clue cells (bacterial vaginosis), motile trichomonads, or pseudohyphae (candidiasis). NAAT testing is added when an STI is suspected, per the approach covered in the STI modules.

The differential, briefly

This ties directly to what you've covered in the STI and public health modules: candidiasis (thick, white, "cottage cheese" discharge, normal pH, intense itching, often post-antibiotic) is not an STI; bacterial vaginosis (thin, grey-white, fishy odor, elevated pH, clue cells) reflects a disrupted vaginal flora rather than a single pathogen and is not classically sexually transmitted either, though it's associated with sexual activity; trichomoniasis (frothy, yellow-green, malodorous, elevated pH, motile organisms on wet mount) is a true STI requiring partner treatment. Physiologic ovulatory mucus, correctly identified by its timing and quality, requires no treatment at all — and reassurance alone is the correct intervention.

Bringing it together

The core clinical skill here is discrimination: knowing what normal cyclical discharge looks like well enough to recognize it confidently, so that abnormal discharge — distinguished by timing, character, odor, associated symptoms, and simple point-of-care testing — gets appropriately worked up rather than either over-treated or dismissed.
