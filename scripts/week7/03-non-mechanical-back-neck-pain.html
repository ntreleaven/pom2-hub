Week 7 — Clinical Approach to Non-Mechanical Back & Neck Pain

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the functional anatomy of the neck and back as it relates to non-mechanical spine pathology, including dermatomes, myotomes, and the expected sensory/motor pattern of root lesions at C5–C8 and L3–S1.
  • Build a differential diagnosis and management plan for axial pain that isn't simple mechanical pain: recognize red flags, distinguish inflammatory from non-inflammatory back pain, and recognize the clinical pictures of radiculopathy, spinal stenosis, cauda equina syndrome, and cervical myelopathy.
  • Recognize normal spinal anatomy and common anomalies on plain X-ray.
  • Develop an approach to investigating and managing these non-mechanical spine conditions.

High-Yield Content

This module picks up where mechanical back pain leaves off — it's about the pain patterns that aren't "just" degenerative wear and tear, where missing the diagnosis actually costs the patient something. The module is organized in two halves: the neurology-focused problems, and then the broader "everything else" red-flag categories (inflammatory, fracture, visceral, infectious, neoplastic).

Spinal cord and nerve root anatomy, the piece that actually matters clinically. The spinal cord itself ends around the L1/2 vertebral level in adults, but all 31 pairs of spinal nerves still exit the vertebral column at their named level — which means below L1/2, the nerve roots are travelling down inside the thecal sac as the cauda equina before they exit. This matters for understanding disc herniation patterns: because the roots exit on a steep downward angle in the lumbar spine, a paracentral disc herniation at a given level typically affects the nerve root that's about to exit the level below (so an L5/S1 paracentral herniation classically hits S1, not L5 — L5 has already exited more proximally). A far lateral herniation, by contrast, is more likely to catch the root exiting at that same level.

Functionally: the dorsal root carries general sensory input (pain, proprioception, pressure, touch, temperature) into the dorsal horn, with the cell body sitting in the dorsal root ganglion; the ventral root carries motor output from the ventral horn out to muscle. Three meningeal layers wrap the cord for protection, same as the brain.

Radiculopathy — "problems to the side." A compressed or irritated nerve root produces a recognizable pattern along its dermatome/myotome: pain radiating in a band down the limb, sensory change in that distribution, weakness in the muscles that root supplies, and a reduced or absent reflex if that root contributes to a testable reflex arc. Knowing the C5–C8 and L3–S1 patterns cold is worth the effort because it's how you localize a lesion from the bedside before any imaging exists.

Central problems — "problems to the centre," split by region:

Lumbar red flag — cauda equina syndrome. This is the one true surgical emergency in this module. Central compression of the cauda equina (massive central disc herniation, tumour, epidural abscess/hematoma) causes saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness/sensory change, and bowel/bladder dysfunction (classically urinary retention with overflow incontinence). This needs emergent MRI and surgical consult — it isn't a "wait and see" diagnosis.

Cervical red flag — cervical myelopathy. Chronic cervical cord compression presents more insidiously than cauda equina: gait disturbance (often described as unsteadiness or a wide-based gait), loss of fine motor dexterity in the hands (the classic teaching example is a patient noticing they keep dropping things), and sometimes radiating neck/arm pain. Upper motor neuron signs — hyperreflexia, clonus, a positive Hoffman's sign — distinguish it from a simple radiculopathy. Because the clinical picture can be subtle and progress slowly, it's easy to under-recognize; any patient with chronic neck pain plus new dexterity loss, gait change, or bladder symptoms should prompt an MRI of the cervical spine rather than being written off as "just" mechanical neck pain.

Inflammatory back pain — distinguishing it from mechanical pain is a classic exam distinction. Inflammatory back pain is worse with rest (classically worse overnight, prompting the patient to get up and move around), improves with exercise and movement, and is associated with prolonged morning stiffness. This is the opposite aggravating/relieving pattern from mechanical pain, which is worse with movement and better with rest. Inflammatory back pain can also travel with extra-articular features that point to a spondyloarthropathy — SI joint involvement, rash (psoriasis), eye symptoms (uveitis), or a family history of inflammatory arthritis — so a positive review of systems in those domains should raise your suspicion even if the back pain itself sounds nonspecific.

The rest of the red-flag categories, applied to the spine specifically:
- Fracture — think trauma, osteoporosis, or prior fragility fracture; look for point tenderness, and remember spine fractures get staged by whether the posterior elements are involved (posterior involvement \= unstable).
- Visceral — back pain can be referred from abdominal/retroperitoneal pathology (think of a leaking or dissecting aortic aneurysm, pancreatitis, renal colic, or pyelonephritis), so the review of systems and abdominal exam are part of a complete back pain workup, not an afterthought.
- Infection — epidural abscess or vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis: fevers, sweats, IV drug use history, immunosuppression, and new neuro deficits in the setting of back pain should raise this; it's a diagnosis that's easy to miss because early imaging can look deceptively normal.
- Neoplasm — unremitting pain that's worse at night and doesn't improve with rest, unintentional weight loss, a history of cancer (metastatic disease to the spine is far more common than primary spinal tumours), and age extremes (very young or older) should all raise suspicion.

Plain X-ray in this context: the same systematic read applies as elsewhere in the spine — alignment, bone cortex and vertebral body height, the disc spaces/facet joints, and soft tissues — with the goal of catching fracture, spondylolysis, or scoliosis. X-ray has real limits here though: it won't show cord compression, disc herniation, or early infection, so a convincing red-flag history should prompt MRI even when the X-ray looks unremarkable.

Putting it together: the clinical skill this module is building isn't memorizing every differential — it's pattern-matching aggravating/relieving factors (worse with rest \= think inflammatory or neoplastic; worse with movement \= think mechanical) and screening systematically for red flags, because that's what separates "reassure and treat conservatively" from "this needs an MRI and a same-day referral."
