High-Yield Content

Why this matters: pain is defined as both sensory and emotional. A patient's distress isn't separate from the pain, and treating only the tissue misses much of it.

Definition

The International Association for the Study of Pain: "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage."

Assessing Fred

A 47-year-old man, off work and supported by WSIB, has ongoing pain and disability one year after a crush injury with well-healed tibial and fibular fractures.

Assessment:

  • Duration and nature of pain
  • Physical and psychological symptoms, including suicidal ideation
  • Past care, treatments and home remedies: what has worked
  • Functional implications
  • Review all past investigations; others only as clinically indicated
  • Pain charting: numerical 0–10, faces, visual analogue, McGill Pain Questionnaire, pain diary

Psychological symptoms and pain

The relationship runs on a spectrum: emotional and cognitive aspects of pain, adjustment disorder, psychological factors affecting a medical condition, somatic symptom disorders, and comorbid psychological symptoms.

Affective disorders go both ways (from the Viana slides): depression is common in chronic pain (5–87%), with somatic overlap in appetite, sleep, energy and weight. Pain intensity, number of painful areas and frequency of severe pain predict depression. Anxiety is one of the most disabling aspects, and leads to activity avoidance, deconditioning and social isolation.

Cognitive distortions: mental filter, catastrophizing, emotional reasoning.

Medically unexplained symptoms

Types: pain in different locations, functional disturbance of organ systems, fatigue or exhaustion.

May be caused by: physiological disturbance, emotional problems, or pathology not yet diagnosed.

Prevalence: 15–25% in primary care, 39–52% in specialist clinics, over 60% in neurology.

Functional somatic syndromes: IBS, chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia. Female preponderance, significant overlap with anxiety and depression.

Somatic symptom and related disorders (DSM-5)

DSM-5 moved away from mind-body dualism and from implying that symptoms aren't real. The focus is on distressing symptoms with abnormal thoughts, feelings and behaviours. The group includes somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder, conversion disorder, psychological factors affecting other conditions, and factitious disorder.

Somatic symptom disorder:

  • A: one or more somatic symptoms that are distressing or disrupt daily life
  • B: excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviours about them
  • C: symptomatic state persists over 6 months, though any one symptom may come and go

Conversion disorder (functional neurological symptom disorder):

  • Altered voluntary motor or sensory function
  • Clinical findings incompatible with recognized neurological or medical conditions
  • Not better explained by another disorder
  • Causes significant distress or impairment

Factitious disorder: presenting self or another as ill or injured, with deception, even without obvious external rewards. Imposed on self, or on another.

Gain and consciousness

Behaviour Gain
Somatic symptom and related disorders Unconscious Inner psychic gain
Factitious disorder Conscious Unconscious; the focus is the sick role
Malingering Conscious External gain

Assessing factitious disorder or malingering:

  • Thorough history with a clear chronology
  • Inconsistent, selective or misleading information
  • Symptoms out of proportion
  • Gaps between index event and onset
  • Many tests and treatments with little benefit
  • Collateral information and triangulation of the data
  • Inconsistent exam findings
  • Neuropsychological assessment in some cases

Management

Multimodal, multidisciplinary, collaborative, with realistic measurable goals: management, not cure. Start with the patient.

Biological:

  • Physical: TENS, heat and cold, acupuncture, massage, physio, injections
  • WHO ladder: non-opioid ± adjuvant (NSAIDs, acetaminophen); weak opioid + non-opioid ± adjuvant; strong opioid + non-opioid ± adjuvant
  • Adjuvants: analgesic antidepressants, anticonvulsants

CBT: connects thoughts, feelings and behaviours; manages rather than cures pain; individual or group. Components: education, contingency management, relaxation, problem solving, communication, distraction, behavioural activation, cognitive restructuring.

Other: psychodynamic psychotherapy, relaxation and meditation.

Rehabilitation: education and adaptation, ergonomics, pacing, exercise, vocational coordination.

Secondary prevention: time is vital; intervene before 12 weeks. Early and active, with maintained activity and return to work. Patient as partner, good communication with workplace and family, follow-up.

General strategies:

  • One physician, regular visits
  • Goal directed
  • Understand the communication
  • Limit investigations and operations
  • Motivational interviewing
  • Monitor thyself
