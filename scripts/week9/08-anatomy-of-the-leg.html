Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the lower extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of leg pathology.
    • Identify the bony landmarks of the lower extremity and relate these to their appearances on plain radiographs.
    • Identify the lumbosacral plexus and its branches and describe what functional consequences would occur with lesions at different levels.
    • Identify the compartments of the thigh and leg and describe the function and innervation of the muscles in each compartment.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: the leg is the cleanest example of "one compartment, one job, one nerve". Once you know that the anterior compartment dorsiflexes (deep fibular nerve), the lateral everts (superficial fibular nerve) and the posterior plantarflexes (tibial nerve), you can predict foot drop after a fibular head injury and work out where a nerve lesion is.

Objective 1: osteology

Tibia: the major weight-bearing bone of the leg.

  • Medial and lateral condyles (the tibial plateau), the tibial tuberosity (patellar ligament), and the soleal line on the back (soleus origin).
  • The medial malleolus transfers weight to the talus.
  • It is subcutaneous at the "shin", so the periosteum bruises easily.
  • It fractures most often at its narrowest point, between the middle and distal thirds, which is also the area with the poorest blood supply.

Fibula: posterolateral, and non-weight-bearing, which makes it the bone of choice for grafts.

  • The lateral malleolus helps hold the talus.
  • It fractures most often just above the lateral malleolus, when the talus is pushed in extreme inversion. Because the interosseous membrane is strong, the fibula often breaks along with the tibia.

Foot:

  • Talus: held between the malleoli. It has no muscle or tendon attachments and transfers weight to the calcaneus and forefoot.
  • Calcaneus: the heel and the largest foot bone.
    • The calcaneal tuberosity takes the Achilles tendon.
    • The sustentaculum tali is a shelf for the talus and a pulley for the FHL tendon.
  • Navicular: its tuberosity anchors the spring (calcaneonavicular) ligament, which supports the medial longitudinal arch.
  • Cuboid (most lateral) and the three cuneiforms, whose wedge shape builds the transverse arch.
  • Metatarsals:
    • The tuberosity of the 5th metatarsal takes fibularis brevis. A sudden inversion can cause an avulsion fracture there.
    • Metatarsal fractures come from a crush injury, or a "dancer's fracture" when a ballet dancer lands with all their weight on the metatarsals.
  • Sesamoid bones sit in the flexor hallucis brevis tendons under the 1st MTP joint.

Objective 3: organization of the leg

  • The crural fascia wraps the leg. The tibia, fibula, interosseous membrane, and anterior and posterior intermuscular septa divide it into anterior, lateral and posterior compartments. A transverse intermuscular septum splits the posterior compartment into superficial and deep parts. That makes four compartments.
  • Retinacula stop tendons bowstringing at the ankle:
    • superior and inferior extensor retinacula for the anterior compartment
    • superior and inferior fibular retinacula for the lateral compartment
    • the flexor retinaculum, which roofs the tarsal tunnel, for the deep posterior compartment
  • Compartment syndrome: the fascia seals each compartment, so swelling from exercise, infection or trauma raises the pressure, compresses nerves and arteries, and starves the tissue. It may need a fasciotomy.
  • Movements: dorsiflexion and plantarflexion at the ankle; inversion and eversion at the foot.

Muscles by compartment

Anterior compartment: dorsiflexion. Deep fibular (peroneal) nerve.

  • Tibialis anterior: lateral tibia + interosseous membrane → medial cuneiform and base of the 1st metatarsal. Dorsiflexes and inverts. Myotome L4–L5.
  • Extensor digitorum longus: → digits 2–5. Dorsiflexes and extends the lateral four toes.
  • Extensor hallucis longus: → distal phalanx of the great toe. Dorsiflexes and extends the big toe. Myotome L5.
  • On the dorsum of the foot, extensor digitorum brevis and extensor hallucis brevis (deep fibular nerve) help EDL and EHL extend the toes.
  • Shin splints: microtrauma to the tibial periosteum where the anterior muscles (especially tibialis anterior) attach. It is a mild form of anterior compartment syndrome, seen in deconditioned walkers and runners who don't warm up or cool down.

Lateral compartment: eversion. Superficial fibular (peroneal) nerve.

  • Fibularis (peroneus) longus: head and upper fibula → plantar base of the 1st metatarsal and medial cuneiform. Everts, weakly plantarflexes, and supports the transverse arch.
  • Fibularis (peroneus) brevis: lower fibula → tuberosity of the 5th metatarsal. Everts and weakly plantarflexes.

Superficial posterior compartment: plantarflexion. Tibial nerve.

  • Gastrocnemius: two heads from the femoral condyles → calcaneus via the calcaneal (Achilles) tendon. Plantarflexes with the knee extended, raises the heel when walking, and flexes the knee (it crosses two joints). Myotome S1.
  • Soleus: tibia (soleal line) and fibula → the calcaneal tendon. Plantarflexes, working with the knee bent too. Myotome S1.
  • Plantaris: the femoral supracondylar line → the calcaneal tendon. Weak plantarflexor, used for tendon grafts.
  • The slide's knowledge check: a standing calf raise (knee straight) works gastrocnemius and soleus. A seated calf raise (knee bent) isolates soleus, because a flexed knee slackens gastrocnemius.
  • Calcaneal tendinitis: microtears just above the insertion, common in runners.
  • Calcaneal tendon rupture: usually poorly conditioned people with forceful plantarflexion. They feel an audible snap, the gap is palpable, and they can't plantarflex against resistance. Surgery for young active patients; older or inactive patients may be managed non-operatively.

Deep posterior compartment: tibial nerve. All pass under the flexor retinaculum.

  • Popliteus: lateral femoral condyle and lateral meniscus → posterior tibia above the soleal line. Weakly flexes the knee and "unlocks" the knee by laterally rotating the femur.
  • Flexor digitorum longus: posterior tibia → the lateral four toes. Flexes the toes, plantarflexes, supports the longitudinal arches.
  • Tibialis posterior: tibia, fibula and interosseous membrane → mainly the navicular tuberosity (plus the cuneiforms, cuboid and metatarsals 2–4). Plantarflexes and inverts, supports the arches. Myotome L5.
  • Flexor hallucis longus: posterior fibula → great toe (it runs under the sustentaculum tali). Flexes the hallux and supports the medial arch.

The tarsal tunnel lies behind the medial malleolus, under the flexor retinaculum. Its contents from anterior to posterior: "Tom, Dick, And Very Nervous Harry":

  • Tibialis posterior
  • flexor Digitorum longus
  • posterior tibial Artery and posterior tibial Vein
  • tibial Nerve
  • flexor Hallucis longus

In the posterior leg the bellies lie in a different order, medial to lateral: FDL, TP, FHL.

Tarsal tunnel syndrome: swollen tendon sheaths compress the tibial nerve under the flexor retinaculum. The commonest symptom is heel pain.

Arteries and veins

Arteries:

  • The popliteal artery divides at the lower border of the popliteal fossa:
    • Anterior tibial artery: passes through the top of the interosseous membrane, runs with the deep fibular nerve in the anterior compartment, and becomes the dorsalis pedis on the dorsum of the foot, between the malleoli. The dorsalis pedis pulse is felt there.
    • Posterior tibial artery: runs with the tibial nerve in the deep posterior compartment and passes behind the medial malleolus in the tarsal tunnel. Its largest branch is the fibular (peroneal) artery to the posterior and lateral compartments.

Veins:

  • Superficial veins are in the subcutaneous tissue:
    • The great saphenous vein starts at the dorsal venous arch, runs anterior to the medial malleolus, up the medial leg and thigh, through the saphenous opening into the femoral vein.
    • The small saphenous vein runs lateral to the calcaneal tendon, between the gastrocnemius heads, into the popliteal vein.
  • Deep veins run deep to the deep fascia, are paired with the arteries, and have more valves.
  • Perforating veins have valves that let blood flow only from superficial to deep.

Venous return against gravity:

  • valves, which prevent backflow
  • the arteriovenous pump, where paired deep veins are squeezed as the artery pulses
  • the musculovenous pump, where contracting muscle bellies inside the tight fascia "milk" the veins toward the heart

Varicose veins: the great saphenous vein and its tributaries dilate so the valve cusps no longer close, and blood flows backward (down). Laser treatment scars and closes the vein.

Objective 2: nerves and the consequences of lesions

Motor:

  • The sciatic nerve (L4–S3) splits at the popliteal fossa into:
    • Tibial nerve: the nerve of the posterior compartment.
    • Common fibular (peroneal) nerve: wraps around the neck of the fibula, where it is subcutaneous and vulnerable, then divides into:
      • Superficial fibular nerve: the lateral compartment (eversion). It then becomes sensory to the anterolateral leg and the dorsum of the foot.
      • Deep fibular nerve: the anterior compartment (dorsiflexion) and the dorsal foot muscles. It is sensory only to the first web space (between the big and 2nd toe).

Sensory map:

  • Saphenous nerve (from the femoral nerve): medial leg and foot.
  • Lateral sural cutaneous nerve (common fibular): posterolateral leg.
  • Medial sural cutaneous nerve (tibial): posterior leg.
  • Sural nerve (where the two sural branches join): lateral ankle and foot.
  • Superficial fibular nerve: anterolateral leg and dorsum of the foot.
  • Deep fibular nerve: the first web space.
  • Medial and lateral plantar nerves (tibial): the sole.

Common fibular nerve injury at the fibular head → foot drop:

  • Loss of the anterior and lateral compartments, so no dorsiflexion and no eversion, plus numbness of the dorsum of the foot.
  • Compensations to clear the toes:
    • steppage gait: lift the whole leg high
    • waddling gait: hike the hip
    • swing-out gait: swing the leg out laterally
  • With no eccentric dorsiflexor control, the forefoot slaps down after heel strike.

Other entrapments:

  • Superficial fibular nerve: stretched by repeated ankle sprains. Gives anterolateral leg and dorsal foot pain and paresthesia.
  • Deep fibular nerve: compressed by tight boots (ski boots, cleats) under the inferior extensor retinaculum. Gives pain radiating to the first web space.

Bottom line:

  • Anterior compartment = dorsiflexion = deep fibular nerve. Lateral = eversion = superficial fibular. Posterior = plantarflexion = tibial.
  • The common fibular nerve wraps the fibular neck, so injury there causes foot drop and steppage gait.
  • Gastrocnemius works with the knee straight, soleus with it bent (the seated calf raise isolates soleus).
  • Tarsal tunnel: Tom, Dick, And Very Nervous Harry.
  • Tibialis posterior inserts on the navicular and inverts. Fibularis brevis inserts on the 5th metatarsal tuberosity (avulsion fracture).
  • The great saphenous vein runs in front of the medial malleolus, and the small saphenous vein ends in the popliteal vein.
  • Tibial fractures happen at the mid-distal third, where blood supply is poor. The fibula is the graft donor.
