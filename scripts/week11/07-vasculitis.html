High-Yield Content

Why this matters: vasculitis is rare but can be fatal, and in giant cell arteritis a delay can mean permanent blindness. The skill is recognizing a sick patient with several organ systems involved.

What vasculitis is

A group of diseases with inflammation of the blood vessel wall, with immune cell infiltration on histology. It is systemic: patients feel sick, with fevers, weight loss, fatigue, rapid pulse and diffuse aches. It can involve virtually any organ, and some types are uniformly fatal untreated.

Primary vs secondary

Secondary (identifiable cause):

  • Infection: hepatitis B or C, HIV, other viral, bacterial, fungal
  • Toxins and drugs: allergic reaction, cocaine
  • Cancer: hematologic, paraneoplastic
  • Connective tissue disease: lupus, RA, Sjögren's

Primary: no known cause, over 20 disorders, with symptoms set by the size and distribution of vessels affected.

When to suspect primary vasculitis

  • Chronically and systemically unwell
  • Fever or chills, and/or weight loss
  • Symptoms in multiple organ systems
  • Purpura
  • Very sick with multi-organ failure, especially pulmonary hemorrhage plus renal dysfunction
  • AND no underlying infection or malignancy

Why diagnosis is hard: it's rare, symptoms vary and can be nonspecific for years, many organs are involved, and it mimics common disease.

Case 1: a 45-year-old man with 6 weeks of painful, non-blanching rash on the legs, fatigue and bilateral ankle pain and swelling. The differential for purpura is coagulopathy or vasculitis; the case is cutaneous leukocytoclastic vasculitis.

Diagnosis and investigations

History and exam are critical. Rule out infection before diagnosing. Biopsy of involved tissue best clinches it.

  • Systemic inflammation (nonspecific): normocytic anemia, reactive thrombocytosis, raised ESR and CRP, low albumin
  • Organ involvement: kidney and lung disease can be silent, so always check creatinine, urine blood and protein, and chest X-ray
  • Infection: WBC; blood, urine and sputum cultures (including fungal and AFB); liver enzymes; hepatitis B and C; cryoglobulins and complement; HIV; syphilis
  • Cancer: age-appropriate screening, serum protein electrophoresis

If you suspect vasculitis: head-to-toe history and exam, rule out infection, cancer screening up to date, CBC, creatinine, liver enzymes, ESR/CRP, urinalysis, chest X-ray, urgent referral to rheumatology, plus other services by organ.

Giant cell arteritis

Pathophysiology: a large vessel vasculitis with a predilection for cranial arteries, particularly the temporal artery. Histology: transmural inflammation with giant cells.

Who: only over 50, most over 70. Lifetime risk 1% in women, 0.5% in men. More common in Northern European descent.

Case 3: a 75-year-old woman with new right temporal headache worsening over 1 week, pain brushing her hair, jaw pain when eating, and 2 months of morning shoulder and hip stiffness, fatigue and weight loss. Hgb 99, ESR 70, CRP 50. Given prednisone 50 mg the same day; 100% better in 5 days.

Cranial symptoms:

  • Headache; temporal and scalp tenderness
  • Diplopia, vision loss
  • Jaw and tongue claudication
  • Strokes

Other symptoms:

  • Fatigue, fever, weight loss
  • Polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms: limb girdle pain and stiffness, worse in the morning
  • Limb claudication, Raynaud's or digital ischemia, chest pain, arthritis

Exam: tender temples, swollen rope-like temporal artery, optic disc swelling, pulselessness, bruits, BP differential.

Vision loss:

  • Occurs in 15–20%
  • Can be transient: amaurosis fugax
  • Without treatment, total vision loss in the same and other eye within days to months
  • Acute painless vision loss is permanent

Differential: sinusitis, dental infection, temporomandibular arthritis, acute-angle glaucoma, meningitis or encephalitis, cranial neuropathies, intracranial malignancy, severe hypertension, chronic headache syndromes, neck pain.

Diagnosis:

  • ESR and CRP immediately. If neither is elevated, GCA is highly unlikely (under 2% are negative for both).
  • Temporal artery biopsy within 1–2 weeks. Transmural inflammation with giant cells is 100% specific, but 50–70% are negative because of skip lesions, so a negative biopsy can't rule out GCA.
  • Doppler ultrasound in specialized centres: the halo sign, specificity over 85%, absent in about 30%, unreliable elsewhere.

Initial management:

  • Start prednisone 1 mg/kg (max 60 mg daily) as soon as GCA is suspected, after blood is drawn
  • If blood can't be drawn immediately, do not wait to start, and don't wait for results
  • Protect against steroid effects: PPI, calcium and vitamin D, bisphosphonate
  • Vision loss or stroke symptoms: emergency department for urgent ophthalmology or neurology
  • Arrange biopsy or ultrasound; refer to rheumatology

ANCA-associated vasculitis

A rare systemic small vessel vasculitis (20–30 per million), fatal if untreated, with high morbidity and common relapses.

Three types: GPA (formerly Wegener's), MPA, EGPA (Churg-Strauss).

ANCA tests:

Pattern Target (ELISA) Most common in
c-ANCA (cytoplasmic) Anti-PR3 GPA
p-ANCA (perinuclear) Anti-MPO MPA, EGPA
  • Immunofluorescence detects a collection of antibodies. ELISA detects one and is more specific.
  • False positives: infection (endocarditis), cancer, medications, cocaine
  • Up to 30% of GPA and 50–60% of MPA/EGPA are ANCA negative, so a negative test doesn't rule it out

Most common manifestations:

  • Sinonasal inflammation: bloody or crusty discharge, facial pain
  • Retro-orbital mass (diplopia)
  • Dyspnea: lung nodules, adult-onset severe asthma, pulmonary embolism
  • Pulmonary hemorrhage
  • Inflammatory arthritis
  • Mononeuritis multiplex
  • Renal dysfunction: raised creatinine, proteinuria, hematuria
  • Skin: purpura, urticarial vasculitis, ulcers

Workup: head-to-toe assessment; CBC, creatinine, liver enzymes, ESR/CRP, urinalysis; chest X-ray; rule out infection.

Biopsy: important for single-organ disease. Punch biopsy for purpura or urticarial vasculitis in primary care; deep excisional for ulcers; refer for other tissues (renal if active sediment, sural nerve if neuropathy on EMG, lung nodule, sinonasal mass, ocular pseudotumour, lymph node).

When to order ANCA: only if small vessel vasculitis is suspected. Usually specialists order it; primary care may if concerned and no timely appointment exists.

Initial management:

  • Unstable vitals, pulmonary hemorrhage, significant dyspnea or renal failure: admit
  • Active urine sediment with normal creatinine: urgent nephrology referral
  • Others: urgent rheumatology, plus other specialists by organ

Take-home: systemically sick, multi-organ, purpura, lung plus kidney: think vasculitis, exclude infection, refer urgently. Older patient with new headache: start steroids before the biopsy.
