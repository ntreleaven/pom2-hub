Part 2 covers anterior knee pain (PFPS), bursitis, ITB friction syndrome and Baker's cyst, knee injection therapies, and an overall approach to knee pain.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: most knee pain without an injury is not a torn structure. It is a loading problem like patellofemoral pain or ITB friction. Recognizing these clinically spares the patient unnecessary imaging. The closing framework (sort by trauma, then swelling and timing, then location) is the tool to use at the bedside.

Objective 1: the patellofemoral joint and patellar stability

  • The Q-angle is the angle between a line from the ASIS to the patella and a line from the patella to the tibial tuberosity. It exists because the femur runs diagonally down the thigh.
  • Because of it, the quadriceps pull the patella superolaterally, so it tends to sublux laterally during extension.
  • Two things resist this:
    1. the pull of vastus medialis
    2. an extra buttress of bone on the lateral patellar surface of the femur
  • Genu varum (bowlegged): the femur is more vertical, the Q-angle is smaller, and more load goes through the medial compartment.
  • Genu valgum (knock-kneed): the Q-angle is larger, and more load goes through the lateral compartment.

Objective 2: patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS)

(Case: an 18-year-old woman with 3 months of worsening bilateral anterior knee pain after starting running. It began with exercise and is now there with prolonged sitting and stairs. No swelling, and a deep aching pain.)

  • What it is: anterior knee pain during or after repetitive loading of the extensor mechanism.
  • Risk factors: females, adolescents, and an increased tibiofemoral angle and Q-angle.
  • Presentation:
    • very common, 30% lifetime prevalence
    • gradual onset (biomechanical overload), or traumatic (patellar contusion)
    • anterior aching pain, worse after activity and with prolonged sitting (the "theatre sign")
    • no joint swelling
  • Evaluation: a clinical diagnosis. No imaging is needed. X-rays or MRI only to rule out other problems if treatment fails.
  • Treatment:
    • activity modification
    • physiotherapy targeting dynamic misalignment and the balance between vastus lateralis and vastus medialis
    • bracing or taping
    • graded return to play

Objective 2: other knee pathology

Knee bursitis:

  • Bursae: prepatellar (over the patella), superficial and deep infrapatellar (around the patellar ligament), and suprapatellar (under the quadriceps tendon, connected to articularis genu and the synovium).
  • Common, usually from chronic friction. Clinical diagnosis: mild swelling and tenderness over the bursa.
  • Treatment: PRICE and NSAIDs.
  • Don't confuse it with septic bursitis or trauma.
  • Eponyms from the notes: housemaid's knee = prepatellar (kneeling on the patella); clergyman's knee = superficial infrapatellar (kneeling on the tibial tuberosity).

Popliteal (Baker's) cyst:

  • A cystic extension of the synovial cavity or a bursa (gastrocnemius/semimembranosus) into the popliteal fossa through a defect in the posterior capsule.
  • In adults: uncomfortable popliteal swelling that may limit flexion and can extend down to mid-calf.
  • Investigations: ultrasound confirms the cyst. X-ray looks for associated orthopaedic injury. Perhaps MRI.
  • Differential: popliteal artery aneurysm, soft tissue tumour, DVT, meniscal cyst.
  • Treatment: conservative for 6 weeks unless there is vascular or neural compromise: PRICE, NSAIDs, perhaps an IA steroid. If it doesn't respond, MRI and surgical referral. Surgery treats the underlying joint problem, not just the cyst.

ITB friction syndrome:

  • Friction between the IT band and the lateral femoral epicondyle during stance phase, at below 30° of knee flexion.
  • Mechanism: running, especially downhill. It is less likely with sprinting.
  • Presentation: aching lateral knee pain during running.
  • Treatment: PRICE and stretching the ITB.

Knee injection therapies

Injection What it is Benefits Limitations
Corticosteroid Local potent anti-inflammatory for joints, bursae and tendons Rapid onset, cheap (<$20) Short-acting (<4–12 weeks); weak evidence of chondrotoxicity / OA progression if given intra-articularly
Hyaluronic acid Replaces a natural synovial fluid component, for viscoelasticity, shock absorption and lubrication >26 weeks of relief, good safety Expensive ($300–500), heterogeneous studies
Platelet-rich plasma Concentrated autologous platelets and growth factors from 20–60 mL of blood May help tissue repair; generally safe Expensive ($500–1,500), high-bias studies, not standardized; Cochrane 2014: no clinically significant benefit; IOC 2010: firm recommendations "cannot be given"
Mesenchymal stem cells Proposed cartilage regeneration Not shown better than other injections Very limited human data; $3,000–10,000; Health Canada (2019) regulates them as drugs needing approval

Celebrity PRP use (Tiger Woods, A-Rod, Kobe Bryant) is not evidence.

Objective 2: a clinical approach to the knee

History:

  • Symptoms:
    • pain (aching, burning, throbbing, stabbing)
    • inflammatory (swelling, stiffness, redness, warmth)
    • neurologic (tingling, shooting pain, numbness, heaviness)
  • Function:
    • structural (instability, catching, giving way, locking)
    • pain or neurologic (weakness, limping, trouble walking)
  • Provoking factors: weight bearing, stairs, sitting, inactivity.

Traumatic knee pain: sort by swelling and its timing.

  • Swelling within 6 hours (hemarthrosis): ACL tear, fracture, patellar dislocation, large chondral injury.
  • Swelling after 6 hours: OA flare, meniscal injury, PCL injury, high-grade MCL sprain.
  • No swelling, sorted by location:
    • Anterior: PFPS flare, prepatellar or infrapatellar bursitis.
    • Posterior: PCL injury, gastrocnemius or hamstring strain, popliteus strain.
    • Medial: MCL sprain, OA flare, hamstring strain.
    • Lateral: ITB bursitis, OA flare, biceps femoris injury, superior tibiofibular joint injury.

Non-traumatic knee pain, by location:

  • Anterior: PFPS, patellar and quadriceps tendinopathy, infra- and prepatellar bursitis, apophysitis, patellar stress fracture, referred hip pain, neoplasm.
  • Posterior: PCL, popliteus or gastrocnemius tendinopathy, Baker's cyst, biceps femoris tendinopathy, DVT, neoplasm, referred hip pain, radiculopathy.
  • Medial: medial meniscus, medial knee OA, pes anserine bursitis, MCL, referred hip pain, radiculopathy.
  • Lateral: ITB friction syndrome, biceps femoris tendinopathy, lateral meniscus, peroneal nerve injury, lateral knee OA, superior tibiofibular joint, referred hip pain, radiculopathy, neoplasm.

Always consider referred pain from the hip in knee pain, especially in children and older adults.

Bottom line:

  • The patella wants to sublux laterally (the Q-angle); vastus medialis and the lateral femoral buttress hold it.
  • PFPS: young, female, anterior aching worse with sitting and stairs, no swelling. Clinical diagnosis with no imaging, treated with physio for VM/VL balance.
  • ITB syndrome: downhill runner, lateral knee, under 30° of flexion. Stretch the ITB.
  • Baker's cyst: ultrasound confirms it. Rule out a popliteal aneurysm and DVT.
  • Injections: steroid is fast and cheap but short-acting; HA lasts longer but costs more; PRP and stem cells lack evidence.
  • Traumatic knee: swelling within 6 h means ACL, fracture or patellar dislocation; after 6 h means meniscus, PCL or OA.
