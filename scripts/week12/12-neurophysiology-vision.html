Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

Upon completing this module, students will be able to:

  • Use basic neurophysiology to predict how disruption of normal physiology can cause symptoms and signs
  • Describe eye and vision physiology.

High-Yield Content

The thread through the module: light is focused by the lens and pupil → detected by rods and cones → processed first in the retina → split at the chiasm by hemifield → relayed by the LGN → analyzed in V1 → sent along a dorsal "where" and a ventral "what" stream. A lesion at each level gives a predictable, specific deficit.

1. The eye: getting an image onto the retina

  • Photoreceptors respond to light of 400–700 nm, a tiny slice of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • Light passes through the cornea, pupil and lens. Focus depends on lens shape and pupil diameter.
  • Accommodation: ciliary muscles relax → lens flat → distance; ciliary muscles contract → lens round → near.
  • Myopia (sees near, not far) → concave lenses. Hyperopia (sees far, not near) → convex lenses.
  • Presbyopia: with age the lens loses elasticity and stays too flat for near → reading glasses (myopes need bifocals).
  • Pupillary light reflex: bright light constricts the pupil so photoreceptors don't saturate. Light in one eye constricts both pupils — a test of CN II and III. A smaller pupil also increases depth of focus.

2. The retina

Five cell types:

  • Vertical flow: photoreceptors (rods and cones) → bipolar cells → ganglion cells. Ganglion cell axons form the optic nerve, and only ganglion cells fire action potentials.
  • Lateral (integrating) flow: horizontal and amacrine cells.
  • Light passes through the overlying cells to reach the photoreceptors.

Rods vs cones:

Rods Cones

| Where | Peripheral retina (~120 million per eye) | Concentrated in the fovea (cones only) | | Sensitivity | High (bigger outer segment) — night vision | Lower | | Colour | No | Yes — 3 types (trichromatic) | | Acuity | Low (big receptive fields) | High |

  • Blind spot at the optic disc (no photoreceptors), nasal to the fovea in the retina → in the temporal visual field. The brain "fills in".
  • Why the fovea is sharpest: it's a pit (overlying cells pushed aside, less scatter), and each foveal ganglion cell gets input from few photoreceptors → small receptive fields (~0.03° vs ~3° in the periphery). This comes from convergence.
  • The fovea covers only the central 2° of the field but uses ~50% of the optic nerve. In very dim light the fovea is basically blind (look slightly away from a dim star to see it).
  • Tests: a letter chart tests foveal acuity; a visual field test finds scotomas (blind areas).

Ganglion cell types:

P (parvocellular) M (magnocellular)

| ~90%; small, slow | 5–10%; large, fast | | Small receptive fields; colour (from cones) | Large fields; no colour; mostly from rods; flicker and motion | | → LGN layers 3–6 → ventral "what" stream | → LGN layers 1–2 (and midbrain) → dorsal "where" stream |

Colour blindness: about 8% of males (2% lack red or green pigment = dichromats; 6% anomalous trichromats) vs ~0.5% of females — X-linked.

Centre–surround receptive fields (made by bipolar, horizontal and amacrine cells): on-centre cells signal "brighter than the background", off-centre cells "darker". They accentuate edges and give constancy (the brain cares about relative contrast, not absolute light).

Three eye diseases:

  • Cataract — clouded lens
  • Glaucoma — high intraocular pressure compresses ganglion cell axons → peripheral loss first ("tunnel vision")
  • Macular degeneration — deposits or abnormal vessels under the retina → central (foveal) vision distorted or lost

3. Visual pathways and field defects

  • Optic nerve (~1 million ganglion cell axons) → optic chiasm → optic tract → lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) of the thalamus → optic radiation → V1 around the calcarine sulcus.
  • Nasal retinal fibres cross at the chiasm. Nasal retina sees the temporal field. So the left visual field goes to the right hemisphere (right eye temporal retina + left eye nasal retina).
Lesion Field defect

| Optic nerve | Blindness in that eye | | Optic chiasm (crossing fibres; e.g. pituitary tumour) | Bitemporal hemianopia | | Optic tract (right) | Left homonymous hemianopia |

  • About 90% of optic tract axons go to the LGN; about 10% go to the midbrain — superior colliculus (orienting) and Edinger–Westphal nucleus (pupillary light reflex).
  • The LGN has 6 monocular layers: 1–2 magnocellular, 3–6 parvocellular. Each LGN neuron gets input from only one eye.

4. V1 (primary visual cortex)

  • Also called striate cortex or area 17 (stripy from heavy LGN input to layer 4). Mostly on the medial occipital surface around the calcarine fissure.
  • Retinotopy: an orderly map of the retina. ~50% of V1 processes the fovea.
  • Feature detectors (Hubel and Wiesel, Nobel 1981), built by convergence:
    • Simple cells: stationary lines of a preferred orientation
    • Complex cells: orientation and direction of motion
    • Hypercomplex (end-stopped) cells: moving lines of a specific length
  • Hypercolumns (1 mm × 1 mm) each analyze one small patch of retina:
    • Input from each eye enters layer 4 (still monocular); binocular cells above and below give depth (stereopsis)
    • Blobs in the centre hold colour-sensitive double-opponent cells
    • Orientation columns radiate around the blobs like a pinwheel

5. Beyond V1: two streams

  • V1 → V2 → V3 → more than three dozen extrastriate areas, each with its own map and specialty (motion, colour, faces).
Dorsal stream Ventral stream

| Goes to | Posterior parietal cortex | Inferior temporal lobe | | Job | "Where" / action — spatial location, motion | "What" / perception — recognizing objects | | Input | Mostly M cells | Mostly P cells | | Lesion examples | Area MT → can't perceive motion (the world becomes snapshots). Parietal → optic ataxia (sees an object but can't reach it accurately in the periphery) | Lateral occipital cortex → visual agnosia (sees and can copy an object but can't name it by sight; fine by touch or sound). Fusiform face area → prosopagnosia (can't recognize faces, even their own; recognizes people by voice or gait) |

  • Seeing a flock of birds needs both: dorsal for direction of motion, ventral for fine features.

Predicting symptoms

  • Can't read up close after 45 → presbyopia.
  • Peripheral field loss, raised eye pressure → glaucoma. Central blur and distortion → macular degeneration.
  • Bitemporal field loss + headache → chiasmal compression (pituitary tumour).
  • Same-side field loss in both eyes → optic tract, radiation or occipital lesion on the opposite side.
  • Can see but can't name objects or faces → ventral stream (temporal) lesion. Can't reach accurately or see motion → dorsal stream (parietal/MT) lesion.
