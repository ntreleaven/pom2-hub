Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Propose an appropriate, tailored program of exercise for primary/secondary/tertiary prevention of various conditions
    • Summarize the health benefits (physiologic, effects on morbidity/mortality) of regular physical activity
    • List and categorize relative and absolute contraindications to exercise
    • Construct an exercise prescription using the FITT principle, taking into account any medical or socioeconomic factors that may impact upon activity selection and patient compliance
    • Distinguish between light, moderate and heavy physical activity and propose methods your patients can use to appropriately target the recommended exercise intensity

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: low fitness is an independent risk factor for death, as important as smoking, hypertension and high cholesterol. Only 25% of physicians counsel on it, yet 92% of patients say they'd follow that advice.

The case

Nancy, 68, is sedentary and worried about falling. She has newly diagnosed untreated hypertension (140/85). Her father had an MI at 70, her mother a hip fracture at 73. She used to walk her dog on trails. How would you write her prescription?

Benefits

Mortality: an independent risk factor for all-cause death. Some is good, more is better: the greatest gains come from moving inactive people to some activity. Trials suggest exercise can compare with drugs for mortality outcomes in several conditions.

Cardiovascular:

  • Up: contractility, coronary flow, stroke volume, oxygen uptake and use, endothelial function, plasma volume
  • Down: heart rate, BP, oxygen demand, blood viscosity

Musculoskeletal: 55% lower risk of hip fracture, mainly from fewer falls (strength, proprioception, coordination, gait, reaction time); a small 1–3% rise in bone density with high-impact training.

Evidence as medicine: includes anxiety, depression, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, COPD, cancer, dementia, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, RA, pain and stroke.

Canadian guidelines (adults)

  • At least 150 minutes a week of moderate to vigorous aerobic activity (or at least 75 minutes vigorous, per the US guideline shown)
  • Muscle strengthening at least 2 days a week
  • Build strength, sit less, improve balance
  • More activity gives more benefit

Intensity

Intensity Patient definition Examples
Light Can sing
Moderate Can talk; breathing and heart rate up, warmer, may sweat Brisk walking, stairs, biking, dancing, swimming
Vigorous Conversation impossible Sport, running, aerobics

Strengthening: a weight where no more than 10–15 repetitions are possible. Standing from sitting, stairs, heavy gardening, resistance bands, push-ups.

Taking a history

  1. Current activity
  2. Intention to be active
  3. Past activity and preferences: enjoyment, solo or group, indoors or outdoors, barriers, goals (playing with grandchildren counts)
  4. Is it safe? Symptoms and past medical history

Safety and contraindications

Vigorous exercise is low risk relative to everyday hazards (47 deaths per 100,000 a year, compared with 190 for motorcycle accidents and 260 for cancer, on the slide).

Pre-participation screening (ACSM): ask about regular exercise, known disease, and signs or symptoms. Any active symptoms mean stop and evaluate:

  • Chest, neck, jaw or arm pain
  • Dyspnea at rest or on mild exertion
  • Dizziness or syncope
  • Orthopnea or PND
  • Ankle edema
  • Palpitations
  • Claudication
  • Known murmur
  • Unusual fatigue

Absolute contraindications in CVD:

  1. New or uncontrolled arrhythmia
  2. Resting or uncontrolled tachycardia
  3. Resting SBP over 180 or DBP over 100
  4. Symptomatic hypotension
  5. Unstable or crescendo angina
  6. Acute or unstable heart failure
  7. Unstable diabetes
  8. Acute febrile illness

Risk stratification:

  • Asymptomatic, low risk: self-directed
  • Asymptomatic with significant risk factors: start low to moderate
  • Asymptomatic, high risk: possible investigations first
  • Symptomatic CVD: supervision or referral

⚠ The slides list absolute contraindications, not a separate "relative" list. Treat controlled risk factors and known conditions as reasons to adapt the plan, and check this against the course.

The prescription: FITT

  • Frequency: 5 times a week (aerobic)
  • Intensity: sing, talk, or no conversation
  • Time: 30 minutes
  • Type: walking, cycling, dancing, swimming, jogging

3 A's: Ask about activity, Advise on benefits, Act by signposting to support.

Tailor it: consider preferences, goals, social determinants (time, money, access), and behaviour change. Use shared decision making and follow up.

Hypertension considerations

Do: control BP over 180/100 with medication first; prolonged warm-up (15 min) and cool-down (10 min); keep the feet moving; one rest day a week.

Don't: very strenuous resistance training; vigorous exercise with known LVH; lying-down, isometric or overhead exercises.

Medications:

  • Beta-blockers: lower max heart rate and performance; avoid in athletes
  • ACE inhibitors, ARBs: minimal problems; recommended
  • Calcium channel blockers: small postural hypotension risk; extend cool-down
  • Diuretics: dehydration risk; accessible toilets

Adjust if: severe pain during exercise, pain that doesn't settle, more night pain, more joint swelling.

Nancy's prescription: BP is below the threshold, so start at low to moderate intensity. Brisk walking with her dog, 30 minutes, 5 days a week, at a "talk" level, plus strength and balance work 2 days a week to reduce her fall risk.
