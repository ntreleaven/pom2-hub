Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  • Develop an approach to a patient presenting with a soft tissue mass
  • Distinguish between tissue types and characteristics of benign and malignant soft tissue tumours
  • Identify clinical presentations concerning for musculoskeletal malignancies
  • Develop a management plan for a patient who presents with a soft tissue mass

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: most lumps are benign, so the common error is cutting out a sarcoma as if it were a lipoma. The module calls this the "whoops" excision, and it can reduce the chance of cure or lead to amputation.

Definitions and epidemiology

  • Soft tissue: non-epithelial, extraskeletal, mesoderm-derived: skeletal and smooth muscle, fat, fibrous tissue, vessels, nerve
  • Malignant soft tissue tumour = sarcoma
  • Benign to malignant is 100:1 (300 vs 2 per 100,000)
  • Incidence rises with age
  • Pediatric sarcomas: neuroblastoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma

Pathogenesis (99% unknown)

  • Environmental: asbestos (mesothelioma), vinyl chloride (angiosarcoma), radiation, herbicides
  • Virus: HHV-8 (Kaposi sarcoma)
  • Chronic lymphedema: Stewart-Treves angiosarcoma
  • Genetic: Li-Fraumeni (p53), retinoblastoma (Rb), neurofibromatosis (neurofibromin)

Classification

Named for the adult tissue they most resemble.

Tissue Benign Malignant
Fat Lipoma Liposarcoma
Skeletal muscle Rhabdomyoma Rhabdomyosarcoma
Smooth muscle Leiomyoma Leiomyosarcoma
Fibrous Fibroma Fibrosarcoma
Peripheral nerve Neurofibroma Neurofibrosarcoma

Behaviour categories:

  • Benign: limited growth, may recur, no metastasis
  • Intermediate: locally aggressive, no metastasis; or rarely metastasizing (5–10%)
  • Malignant: locally aggressive, frequent metastasis

History and exam

History: when noticed, size then and now (be persistent, and use fruit and nuts as size comparisons), trauma, recurrence, other lumps, family history.

Sarcomas are usually painless lumps. Significant symptoms should make you think of metastases.

Exam: location, size, skin changes, consistency, superficial vs deep, mobile, tethered or fixed, regional nodes (uncommonly involved).

The diagnostic pathway

Finding Action
Under 5 cm, no suspicious features (limited growth, minimal symptoms, superficial) Excise
Under 5 cm with suspicious features (rapid growth, pain, recurrent, deep, fixed, difficult location) Imaging (MRI preferred) or surgical expert, then biopsy
Over 5 cm Imaging (MRI preferred) or surgical expert, then biopsy

Rules:

  • Image before biopsy
  • Biopsy by a physician experienced in sarcoma, with resection in mind
  • Don't do a "whoops" excision

Biopsy options

  • FNAB: cheap, easy, fast, atraumatic, but poor yield and hard to type; not widely used for primary diagnosis
  • Excisional: complete removal with minimal margin; not ideal if sarcoma
  • Incisional: more tissue, more invasive
  • Core biopsy: the best compromise: less invasive, adequate tissue, good diagnosis

Pathology: routine histology generates a differential, then histochemistry, immunohistochemistry, cytogenetics, molecular tests and EM.

Management

  • Staging CT chest
  • Multidisciplinary tumour board for every potential sarcoma
  • Surgery ± radiotherapy; chemotherapy is uncommon
  • Radiation added to surgery cuts local recurrence from 30% to 10%
  • Pre-op radiation gives better long-term function: less tissue damage, and surgery removes most of the radiated tissue
  • Adjuvant chemotherapy is not standard care
  • Some sarcomas are curable with chemotherapy plus local therapy: osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma in young adults
  • Non-curable disease: palliative care can significantly improve quality of life

The four cases

  1. 33-year-old, big soft flank mass for 6 years, other lumps, uncle with lumps. Over 5 cm, so image. Spindle cell/pleomorphic lipoma (MDM2 negative, CD34 positive). Observe.
  2. 18-year-old, plum-sized tethered hip mass. Imaging, biopsy, tumour board, wide local excision. No recurrence at 5 years.
  3. 55-year-old with neurofibromatosis, football-sized thigh mass in muscle, dull ache. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour. Pre-op radiation, surgery 6–8 weeks later, CT chest. Lung metastases at one year: goals of care and palliative referral.
  4. 55-year-old, abdominal girth, early satiety, weight loss. Dedifferentiated liposarcoma. Well-differentiated liposarcoma recurs locally but doesn't metastasize; dedifferentiated disease can do both.

Take-home: benign tumours are common, sarcomas are rare and need a specialized path. Image first, core biopsy by an expert, tumour board, then surgery with or without radiotherapy.
