Part 2 covers the nerves of the hand and the two wrist neuropathies: carpal tunnel syndrome and ulnar neuropathy at the wrist (Guyon's canal).

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: carpal tunnel syndrome is "the bread and butter peripheral nerve diagnosis". You'll see it constantly in primary care. To diagnose it, and to tell it apart from a C6 radiculopathy or an ulnar problem, you need to know exactly which skin and which muscles each nerve supplies in the hand, and which branches leave before the wrist.

Objective 1: nerve supply of the hand

Median nerve:

  • Palmar cutaneous branch leaves proximal to the carpal tunnel and passes over the flexor retinaculum to supply the skin of the central palm.
  • The main nerve enters the hand through the carpal tunnel, deep to the flexor retinaculum.
  • Recurrent branch runs a superficial course just past the tunnel to the thenar muscles: opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis and the superficial head of flexor pollicis brevis. A shallow cut over the thenar eminence can sever it. It must be repaired or the thenar muscles waste.
  • The common and proper palmar digital nerves supply the 1st and 2nd lumbricals. They also carry sensation from the palmar side of the lateral 3½ digits and the distal dorsal tips, including the nail beds.

Ulnar nerve:

  • Palmar cutaneous branch passes over the retinaculum to the medial palm. The dorsal cutaneous branch supplies the medial half of the dorsum of the hand and the proximal dorsal medial 1½ digits. Both leave before the wrist.
  • It enters the hand through Guyon's (ulnar) canal, between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate, then splits:
    • Superficial branch: sensation to the palmar and distal dorsal medial 1½ digits.
    • Deep branch: motor to the hypothenar muscles (ODM, ADM, FDM), the 3rd and 4th lumbricals, all the interossei, adductor pollicis and the deep head of FPB.

Radial nerve: sensory only in the hand, to the dorsolateral hand (it has no hand muscles).

Median nerve injuries (from the notes):

  • At the wrist (a laceration or the carpal tunnel): wasting of the thenar muscles and the first two lumbricals, loss of thumb opposition, and numbness of the thumb and the adjacent 2½ fingers.
  • At the elbow: you also lose FDS, FDP to digits 2–3 and the lateral lumbricals. Trying to make a fist then produces the "hand of benediction".

Ulnar nerve injuries:

  • Proximal (most commonly behind the medial epicondyle): wrist flexion weakens and the hand drifts radially (FCR is unopposed). The patient can't flex the DIPs of digits 4–5, and the interossei waste, giving the ulnar "claw hand".
  • At the wrist (Guyon's canal): clawing may occur, but finger flexion is kept and there is no radial deviation.

Brachial plexus palsies (from the notes):

  • Erb-Duchenne (upper, C5–C6): a stretched neck–shoulder angle (motorcycle fall, difficult delivery) gives the "waiter's tip" position: shoulder adducted, arm medially rotated, elbow extended.
  • Klumpke (lower, C8–T1): the arm is yanked upward, which hits the intrinsic hand muscles and gives a claw hand.

Objective 1: the carpal tunnel

It is an osseofibrous tunnel. The floor is the carpal bones (hamate, capitate, trapezoid, trapezium). The roof is the flexor retinaculum (transverse carpal ligament), which runs from the scaphoid and trapezium to the pisiform and hook of hamate.

  • Inside: FPL, 4 FDS tendons, 4 FDP tendons, and the median nerve. The median nerve is the most pressure-sensitive structure in there.
  • Superficial to it: FCR, palmaris longus, the ulnar artery and nerve, and the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve.

Objective 2: carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS)

What it is: compression of the median nerve at the wrist.

Causes:

  • Extrinsic (less space): fracture, flexor tenosynovitis (the finger flexor tendons share the space; synovial sheath inflammation is the commonest cause in the notes), ganglia, rheumatologic inflammation, pregnancy (edema), acromegaly.
  • Intrinsic (more susceptible nerve): diabetes, amyloidosis, alcohol.
  • And very commonly, idiopathic.

Clinical presentation:

  • Numbness and tingling in the median nerve territory, often predominantly at night.
  • Worse with a flexed or extended wrist (driving, holding a phone).
  • Possible thumb weakness (abductor pollicis brevis).
  • Median function above the tunnel is normal, so the median forearm muscles are fine.
  • Maybe positive Tinel's and Phalen's signs.
  • The central palm is spared, because the palmar cutaneous branch doesn't pass through the tunnel.

Differential: the main item is C6 radiculopathy. Others: tendinopathy and CMC (thumb base) OA.

EMG/NCS is very helpful: it separates a muscle or bone problem from a nerve problem, separates CTS from C6, and establishes severity.

Management:

  • Education: it's common, and early disease is reversible. Advise on hand position.
  • Non-pharmacologic: a rigid wrist-hand orthosis worn overnight only, and avoiding aggravating positions and vibrating tools.
  • Pharmacologic: corticosteroid injection into the carpal tunnel.
  • Surgery: carpal tunnel release.

Objective 2: ulnar neuropathy at the wrist (Guyon's canal)

  • Guyon's canal is formed by the carpals (pisiform, hook of hamate) and the overlying ligament. It contains the ulnar artery and nerve.
  • Causes: fracture, ganglia, rheumatologic inflammation, and compression, classically "handlebar neuropathy" in cyclists who put weight on the hook of hamate.
  • Features: hypoesthesia of the medial 1½ digits and weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles.
  • Differential: the main item is C8 radiculopathy. Others: ulnar neuropathy at the elbow and tendinopathy. EMG/NCS separates elbow vs wrist vs C8 and shows severity.
  • Management: education about hand position and pressure (reversible if caught early), relieving pressure (padded gloves, different handlebars), and Guyon's canal release if needed.

Bottom line:

  • Median nerve: thenar muscles (recurrent branch), lumbricals 1–2, and sensation to the lateral 3½ digits. Its palmar cutaneous branch spares the palm in CTS.
  • Ulnar nerve (deep branch): hypothenar muscles, lumbricals 3–4, all interossei and adductor pollicis.
  • CTS: nocturnal median numbness, APB weakness, normal forearm muscles. Rule out C6, confirm with EMG/NCS, then treat with a night splint, an injection, or release.
  • Guyon's canal: medial 1½ digits plus intrinsic weakness, with finger flexion spared. Rule out C8 and an elbow lesion.
