Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

Upon completing this module, students will be able to:

  • Understand the distribution of white and gray matter in the central nervous system.
  • Know the major areas and structures of the cerebral cortex and be able to describe the operations these structures are involved in.
  • Deduce the effects of lesions in various cortical areas
  • Identify and describe the structure-function relationships of specific cortical areas including speech/language and learning/memory as they relate to clinical medicine.

High-Yield Content

Why it matters: knowing which cortical area does what — and which artery feeds it — lets you predict exactly what a stroke, tumour or injury will do.

1. Grey and white matter

  • Grey matter = neuron cell bodies: the cortex on the outside of the cerebrum and cerebellum, plus deep nuclei.
  • White matter = myelinated axons, deep to the cortex, bundled into tracts:
Fibre type Connects Examples

| Commissural | Left ↔ right hemisphere | Corpus callosum (largest) | | Association | Areas within one hemisphere | Arcuate fasciculus (Broca ↔ Wernicke), superior longitudinal fasciculus | | Projection | Cortex ↔ brainstem and cord (vertical) | Corona radiata → internal capsule (between the thalamus and basal ganglia) → crus cerebri; corticospinal tract |

2. How the cortex is organized

  • Primary areas receive input (via the thalamus) or send motor output, with little interpretation: primary motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory.
  • Association areas sit next to primary areas and interpret: unimodal (e.g. visual association) or multimodal.
  • Hierarchy: primary sensory cortex (awareness) → sensory association (meaning) → multimodal association (integration with memory, plan) → motor cortex (action).
  • Brodmann areas number cortical regions by microscopic structure.

Somatotopy: the motor and sensory homunculi — leg medial (near the midline), face and hand lateral. Cortex size matches sensitivity (lips, fingertips). Contralateral: the right hemisphere serves the left body.

Damage to a sensory association area → agnosia (sensation intact but can't recognize).

5. Language

  • Broca's aphasia (frontal): slow, non-fluent, effortful speech; comprehension intact; aware of the deficit.
  • Wernicke's aphasia (temporo-parietal): fluent but meaningless; poor comprehension; often unaware.
  • The two are linked by the arcuate fasciculus (association fibres).
  • The right-hemisphere homologue of Broca's handles the emotional tone of speech.

6. Multimodal association areas

  • Prefrontal (anterior) association area: executive function — reasoning, judgment, impulse control, long-term planning, social skills, empathy. Closely linked to the limbic system and mood. One of the last areas to mature. Tumours or injury here → personality change.
  • Parietal association: orienting attention, spatial awareness of the body; linked to the prefrontal cortex.
  • Temporal association: links a face or object to its meaning and identity.

7. Limbic system

Functions (HOME): Homeostasis (hypothalamus — autonomic and endocrine), Olfaction, Memory (hippocampus), Emotion and drives (amygdala).

  • Limbic lobe: a ring of cortex on the medial surface — cingulate gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, uncus.
  • Hippocampus: deep in the temporal lobe, forming the floor of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle. Converts short-term → long-term memory; "place cells" for spatial memory.
  • Amygdala: anteromedial to the hippocampus. Emotion, especially fear and aggression. Stimulation → fear, rage. (Lateral lesions → overeating; medial lesions → anorexia, hypersexuality.)
  • Memory types: declarative (explicit) = episodic (events) + semantic (facts); non-declarative = skills, habits, emotional memory.

Papez circuit: hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies → mammillothalamic tract → anterior thalamic nucleus → internal capsule → cingulate gyrus → cingulum → (entorhinal cortex) → hippocampus. Links memory and emotion.

8. Clinical memory disorders

  • Bilateral hippocampal damage → anterograde amnesia: no new memories (patient H.M. after bilateral medial temporal resection for epilepsy). Bilateral fornix damage does the same.
  • Alzheimer's disease: degeneration in the Papez circuit → can't learn new information; word- and name-finding trouble.
  • Transient global amnesia: rare, temporary episodic memory loss from medial temporal dysfunction.
  • Wernicke–Korsakoff (thiamine/B1 deficiency, usually alcohol): bilateral mammillary body necrosis.
    • Acute Wernicke triad: ataxia, eye-movement abnormalities (nystagmus to ophthalmoplegia), confusion; can progress to coma.
    • Survivors (Korsakoff): anterograde and retrograde amnesia, poor judgment, confabulation.
