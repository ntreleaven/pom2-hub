Week 10 — Nerve Injury

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Prognosticate recovery from a peripheral nerve injury
    • Differentiate between neurapraxia, axonotmesis, neurotmesis and describe the expected timeline of recovery for each of these types of nerve injury.
    • Describe an approach to localizing peripheral nerve pathology (ie. describe how you might distinguish between a terminal branch lesion and a root lesion)
    • List appropriate tests that can be done to diagnose nerve injuries and describe what types of information can be gleaned from each.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: four questions run the whole approach. Is it a nerve injury? Where is it? How bad is it? What now?

Nerve structure

  • Epineurium: around the whole nerve, holding fascicles together
  • Perineurium: around each fascicle
  • Endoneurium: around each axon, like a protective tube

Seddon classification

Type What is disrupted Recovery
Neurapraxia Myelin only; axon intact Temporary, typically resolves in weeks (6–12) once the cause is addressed. Example: Saturday night palsy
Axonotmesis Axon, with varying supporting tissue; epineurium intact Can recover spontaneously, but slowly, at 1 inch per month (1 mm per day)
Neurotmesis Epineurium: nerve discontinuity Cannot recover spontaneously, because regenerating axons have no track to follow

Mechanisms: compression, crush, stretch, cut.

After axon injury: Wallerian degeneration removes the axon distal to the injury. Schwann cells proliferate and clear debris, and the axon regrows, far better with an endoneurial tube to follow.

Anatomical classification

  • Motor neuronopathy: anterior horn cell
  • Radiculopathy: spinal roots
  • Plexopathy: brachial or lumbosacral plexus
  • Mononeuropathy: an individual named nerve
  • Polyneuropathy: generalized, diffuse

Root vs terminal branch

Radiculopathy Mononeuropathy
Lesion Spinal nerve root, typically compressed in the foramen Named nerve, typically compressed at a common site
Onset Sudden or insidious, usually a clear time Variable; chronic compression is insidious, transection acute
Pain Neck or back pain radiating down the limb in a dermatome, "fuzzy" borders Well-circumscribed sensory abnormality
Aggravated by Positions such as neck extension; relieved by reducing tension (hand overhead) Irritation, such as leaning on the elbow, driving, typing
Weakness Myotomal, across several nerves Only muscles of that peripheral nerve, not others from the same root

Dermatome: skin supplied by one root. Myotome: muscles supplied by one root, regardless of the peripheral nerve.

Numb thumb example: carpal tunnel syndrome or C6 radiculopathy. Their sensory territories overlap. C6 also supplies biceps through the musculocutaneous nerve, and sensation via the LCNFA. APB is C8 through the median nerve. So weak biceps favours C6; weak APB favours carpal tunnel.

Presentation

  • Negative phenomena: loss of sensation, weakness, loss of reflexes
  • Positive phenomena: pain, paresthesia
  • True sensory loss is neurogenic, but ask whether it's PNS or CNS

Electrodiagnostic studies

NCS: stimulate a nerve and record distally.

  • Size of response reflects the axon
  • Timing and speed reflect the myelin

EMG: a needle records muscle activity. Is it normal at rest? Denervated muscle isn't. Does it respond normally to voluntary activation? Which muscle exactly?

EDX can tell you:

  • Is there a nerve problem?
  • Where is it?
  • How severe is it, and will it recover?
  • Is myelin or axon involved, and is the nerve continuous?
  • Is it a muscle problem instead?

EDX can't tell you: the cause of all MSK pain (rotator cuff tear) or CNS disease (stroke). More than one study over time may be needed for prognosis.

Case: foot drop

A horseback rider hits a fence with the right knee and lands on the low back. Dorsiflexion, EHL and eversion are 0/5, and sensation is reduced over the dorsum of the foot with clear borders. Other muscles are 4+/5 from pain.

EMG: abnormal only in common peroneal muscles, sparing biceps femoris short head and paraspinals, with no response on sensory or motor NCS. Diagnosis: common peroneal axonopathy at the fibular head. You can't yet say axonotmesis or neurotmesis; you must wait and see if it recovers.

Alternate ending: abnormal L5 muscles across several nerves, including paraspinals, is L5 radiculopathy.

Take-home: the layers determine healing potential, clinical patterns localize the lesion, and EDX clarifies the diagnosis and prognosis.
