Week 9 — Upper Extremity Radiology

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Source: Dr. V. Kalia, "Upper Extremity Radiology" (Elentra online module, ~35 min): the module PDF "Upper Limb Radiology".

Learning Objective (from the module)

Upon completion of this module the learner will be able to:

  1. Identify the distinguishing features of the bones and joints of the upper extremity and relate those to their appearance on plain radiographs.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: in the upper limb, a plain X-ray is usually the first and often the only imaging you'll order. The skill is a systematic read: know the normal landmarks on each standard view, then check alignment lines that reveal subtle injuries. Examples are a posterior fat pad, a broken Gilula arc, or a humeral head sitting off the glenoid.

General concepts

  • Describe everything in anatomic position: medial/lateral, superior/inferior, proximal/distal, anterior/posterior.
  • Parts of a long bone:
    • epiphysis: the end, with a growth centre
    • physis: the growth plate, a lucent line in children that is fused and invisible once mature
    • metaphysis: the transition zone
    • diaphysis: the shaft
    • cortex: the dense outer layer, around the medullary cavity
  • The X-ray process: position the patient between the tube and the detector, the beam passes through and is attenuated (bone most), the images are stored in PACS, and a radiologist reports them.
  • A systematic approach for any film:
    1. Skeletal maturity: is the physis open or fused?
    2. Bone integrity: cortex, medullary cavity, density, generalized bone disease.
    3. Alignment: misalignment means fracture or dislocation.
    4. Joints: narrowing, inflammation, degeneration.
    5. Soft tissues: pathology can be there even when the bones look normal.
  • TIMS-C for categorizing bone pathology:
    • Trauma/Tumour
    • Infection/Inflammatory (RA, ankylosing spondylitis)/Idiopathic
    • Metabolic/Malignancy
    • Systemic (leukemia, lymphoma)/Senile (osteoarthritis)
    • Congenital, looked for mostly in immature skeletons

Shoulder

Standard series: AP (true AP/Grashey) view, axillary view, and Y-view. Special views (for instability) are outside the module.

AP view:

  • Identify the humeral head, greater and lesser tuberosities, glenoid, acromion, clavicle and coracoid.
  • The glenoid labrum is fibrocartilage and is not radio-opaque, so it's invisible on X-ray.
  • Two reference lines: the anatomical neck (junction of head and neck) and the surgical neck (just below the articular surface). They aren't visible structures, but they are used to describe fractures.

Axillary view: the arm is abducted and the beam passes through the axilla.

  • Find the distal clavicle, acromion (together they form the AC joint), glenoid, humerus, and the coracoid process.
  • The coracoid and lesser tuberosity are both anterior, which tells you which way is front.
  • Watch for an os acromiale, an unfused ossification centre that can look like a fracture.

Y-view:

  • The "Y" is formed by the coracoid (anterior limb), the scapular spine/acromion (posterior limb) and the scapular body (vertical limb). The glenoid sits at the centre.
  • Normally the humeral head is centred over the glenoid. If it's displaced anteriorly (usually subcoracoid), that is an anterior subluxation or dislocation. Posteriorly means a posterior one.
  • Subluxation is partial contact. Dislocation is complete loss of contact.

Shoulder pathology:

  • Osteoarthritis has three key findings, graded mild, moderate or severe:
    • joint space narrowing: cartilage loss, since cartilage is radiolucent
    • subchondral sclerosis: denser bone beneath the surface, but not stronger
    • marginal osteophytes
  • Surgical neck fracture: a fracture line along the surgical neck.
  • Dislocation: anterior dislocations lie subcoracoid. The Y-view distinguishes anterior from posterior.

Elbow

Views: AP (elbow extended) and lateral.

AP landmarks:

  • Orient by the medial side: the medial epicondyle is bigger and the ulna is medial.
  • From medial to lateral on the distal humerus: medial epicondyle, trochlea, capitellum, lateral epicondyle.
  • The sublime tubercle on the medial proximal ulna is where the ulnar collateral ligament attaches, a landmark after trauma.
  • The AP view is best for telling the capitellum from the trochlea.

Lateral read, step by step:

  1. Humeral shaft. The capitellum and trochlea overlap here, and the capitellum is the one next to the radius.
  2. Proximal ulna: olecranon, trochlear notch and coronoid, which are fracture-prone.
  3. Radius: head, neck, shaft and radial tuberosity (biceps).
  4. Fat pads.

Fat pads:

  • A small triangular anterior fat pad is normal.
  • A visible posterior fat pad is always abnormal. It means a joint effusion, usually from trauma.
    • In a child (open physis), suspect a supracondylar fracture.
    • In an adult, suspect a radial head or neck fracture.

Alignment lines:

  • Anterior humeral line (lateral view): drawn along the anterior humeral cortex, it should pass through the centre of the capitellum. If it passes anteriorly in a child, together with a posterior fat pad, that is highly suggestive of a supracondylar fracture.
  • Radiocapitellar line: the radial shaft axis should pass through the centre of the capitellum on every view. If it doesn't, suspect a radial head dislocation or another alignment problem.

Elbow pathology:

  • OA (narrowing, sclerosis, osteophytes, often with intra-articular osseous bodies).
  • A lipoma: normal bones but a well-defined, low-density soft tissue mass. This is the reason to always look at the soft tissues.
  • Check the olecranon bursa region for swelling.

Wrist

Views: AP (PA) with the palm down, and lateral.

AP landmarks:

  • Distal radius and ulna (distal radioulnar joint), the beak-like ulnar styloid and the broader radial styloid, and the radiocarpal joint.
  • Carpals, radial to ulnar:
    • proximal row: scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform
    • distal row: trapezium, trapezoid, capitate, hamate
    • Never reverse the order: scaphoid and trapezium are radial, pisiform and hamate are ulnar.
  • The TFCC is radiolucent and invisible, but calcification in that area hints at TFCC pathology.

Lateral:

  • Lister's tubercle, a small dorsal prominence of the distal radius. Check it for fractures.
  • The scaphoid and pisiform are anterior, the triquetrum posterior. The trapezoid hides behind the trapezium.
  • Because of the overlap, the AP view is essential for finding carpal fractures.

Arcs of Gilula (AP view):

  1. the proximal surfaces of the proximal row
  2. the distal surfaces of the proximal row
  3. the proximal surfaces of the distal row

All three should be smooth and continuous. A break, step-off or widened joint means carpal injury or instability.

Wrist pathology:

  • Scaphoid and triquetral fractures (thin lucent lines, the scaphoid one through the proximal pole) with arcs preserved.
  • A distal radius fracture with an ulnar styloid fracture, arcs intact.
  • A giant cell tumour: an expanded distal radius with a lytic lesion, thinned cortex, extending to the subarticular surface.

Bottom line:

  • Read every film in order: maturity, bone, alignment, joints, soft tissues. Categorize findings with TIMS-C.
  • Shoulder: AP, axillary and Y. On the Y-view the humeral head should sit centred on the glenoid.
  • Elbow: a posterior fat pad is always abnormal (supracondylar fracture in a child, radial head in an adult). The anterior humeral and radiocapitellar lines should both hit the centre of the capitellum.
  • Wrist: name the carpals radial to ulnar and check the three Gilula arcs.
  • Osteoarthritis = joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis and marginal osteophytes.
