Learning Objectives

  • Outline a clinical approach to acute pelvic pain
  • Recognize common causes of acute pelvic pain
  • List a differential diagnosis for acute pelvic pain
  • Outline appropriate investigations for pelvic pain
  • Identify treatment options for common causes of pelvic pain

The priority in acute pelvic pain: rule out the emergencies first

Acute pelvic pain — sudden or recent-onset pain, typically hours to days — demands a different mindset from the chronic pain approach: your first job is always to identify or exclude the small number of surgical or medical emergencies that require immediate action, before working through the broader differential. This is the pairing module to Early Pregnancy Complications and Approach to Chronic Pelvic Pain — together they cover the full spectrum from time-critical to long-standing pelvic pain, and this one sits firmly on the time-critical end.

Step one, every time: pregnancy status

In any reproductive-age patient with acute pelvic pain, a pregnancy test comes before almost everything else, because it immediately reframes the entire differential. A positive test shifts the leading concern to ectopic pregnancy (covered in detail in the Early Pregnancy Complications module) — the single most important diagnosis not to miss in this setting, given its potential for catastrophic hemorrhage if unrecognized.

The "can't-miss" differential

With pregnancy excluded, the emergent and urgent causes to actively rule out include:

  • Ovarian torsion: sudden, severe, often unilateral pain, frequently with nausea/vomiting, sometimes intermittent if the ovary is twisting and detwisting — a surgical emergency where time to intervention determines whether the ovary can be salvaged. An adnexal mass (often a cyst) is usually the underlying substrate that predisposes to torsion.
  • Ruptured ovarian cyst: sudden-onset pain, sometimes at a predictable point in the cycle (mid-cycle for a ruptured follicular/corpus luteum cyst), which can range from mild and self-limited to significant if there's associated hemorrhage requiring intervention.
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess / severe pelvic inflammatory disease: pain with fever, purulent discharge, and marked cervical motion/adnexal tenderness — a complication of ascending infection that can be life-threatening if it ruptures.
  • Appendicitis: classically periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant, with anorexia, low-grade fever, and localized peritoneal signs — essential to keep on the differential given significant overlap with right-sided gynecologic pathology, and a common source of diagnostic confusion in reproductive-age women.
  • Other surgical abdomen causes: diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, and urologic causes (renal colic — classically colicky flank-to-groin pain, often with hematuria) round out the non-gynecologic emergent differential.

Building the fuller differential

Beyond the can't-miss list, acute pelvic pain also includes: mittelschmerz (mid-cycle ovulatory pain, typically mild and self-limited), an acute flare of endometriosis or dysmenorrhea, uncomplicated PID/cervicitis, urinary tract infection, and — less commonly in this age group but not to be forgotten — degenerating fibroid (acute pain from a fibroid outgrowing its blood supply, sometimes precipitated by pregnancy).

A structured clinical approach

  1. Pregnancy test — always first.
  2. Vital signs and hemodynamic assessment — tachycardia, hypotension, or peritoneal signs on exam signal a surgical emergency requiring urgent escalation regardless of what the differential eventually turns out to be.
  3. Focused history: onset (sudden versus gradual), character and radiation, relationship to the menstrual cycle, associated GI or urinary symptoms, sexual history and STI risk, and prior similar episodes.
  4. Examination: abdominal exam for peritoneal signs and localization, pelvic exam (cervical motion tenderness, adnexal masses or tenderness, uterine tenderness), and a general assessment for signs of hemodynamic compromise.
  5. Targeted investigations, guided by the leading differential rather than ordered as a blanket panel.

Investigations

Beta-hCG (quantitative, always first in reproductive-age patients), CBC (assessing for leukocytosis suggesting infection, or a falling hemoglobin suggesting hemorrhage), and inflammatory markers when infection is suspected. Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal preferred) is the primary imaging modality — assessing for an intrauterine pregnancy versus ectopic, adnexal masses, free fluid (suggesting rupture or hemorrhage), and ovarian blood flow on Doppler when torsion is suspected, though a normal Doppler does not fully exclude torsion and shouldn't delay surgical consultation if clinical suspicion remains high. Urinalysis to assess for UTI or evaluate hematuria if renal colic is suspected. CT is reserved for cases where a non-gynecologic surgical cause (appendicitis, diverticulitis, bowel obstruction) is the leading concern, or when ultrasound is equivocal.

Treatment, by cause

Ectopic pregnancy: as detailed in the Early Pregnancy Complications module — expectant, medical (methotrexate), or surgical, based on stability and hCG/ultrasound findings.

Ovarian torsion: urgent surgical detorsion (laparoscopy), with ovarian conservation attempted whenever the tissue appears viable — this is time-sensitive, and delay meaningfully worsens the chance of salvage.

Ruptured cyst: usually managed conservatively with analgesia and observation if hemodynamically stable; surgical intervention is reserved for ongoing hemorrhage or instability.

PID/tubo-ovarian abscess: broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gonorrhea, chlamydia, and anaerobes, with hospitalization and possible drainage or surgical intervention for a large or ruptured abscess or failure to improve on outpatient therapy.

Non-gynecologic causes (appendicitis, renal colic, etc.): managed per their respective surgical/urologic pathways once identified — the key gynecologic contribution is recognizing when the presentation doesn't fit a gynecologic pattern and escalating appropriately rather than anchoring on a pelvic diagnosis.

Bringing it together

Acute pelvic pain management is fundamentally about sequencing: confirm pregnancy status first, assess for hemodynamic instability and peritoneal signs immediately, and actively exclude the small set of time-critical diagnoses — ectopic pregnancy, torsion, rupture with hemorrhage, tubo-ovarian abscess, and appendicitis — before settling into a more relaxed differential. Getting that sequence right, rather than the specific diagnosis, is the actual clinical skill this module is teaching.
