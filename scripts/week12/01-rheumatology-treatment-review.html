High-Yield Content

The big picture: steroids and NSAIDs calm inflammation fast but do not change the disease. DMARDs and biologics change the disease but take weeks to work. So a typical plan is steroid "bridge" + start a DMARD, then adjust to a target.

1. Steroids (systemic and intra-articular)

What prednisone does: a non-specific anti-inflammatory. It blocks transcription of pro-inflammatory proteins, decreases neutrophil migration, inhibits cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and decreases prostaglandins and leukotrienes. It works rapidly, so it is most useful in the acute setting: less pain, swelling, stiffness and disability.

Dose depends on the condition (the adrenals make the equivalent of about 7 mg/day):

Dose When Examples

| High (~1 mg/kg; IV "pulse" methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3 days if an emergency) | Life- or organ-threatening disease | ANCA vasculitis, lupus nephritis, inflammatory myositis, giant cell arteritis | | Medium | Acute flares without major organ involvement | RA flare: 20–40 mg with a taper while a DMARD starts. PMR: 15–20 mg, then taper. Acute gout: 25–30 mg × 3 days, taper by 5 mg every 3 days | | Low (≤5 mg) | Chronic use when nothing else works | |

  • PMR does not need high dose and is self-limited (6 months to 2 years). About 15% have GCA too, and then high dose is needed to prevent blindness.
  • Gout flares are self-limited and don't need high dose. NSAIDs, colchicine or prednisone are all options.

Routes:

  • PO: easy to taper.
  • IM: lasts 3–4 weeks; useful if adherence is a problem.
  • IV: rapid effect, e.g. GCA with vision involvement, before switching to high-dose oral.
  • Intra-articular ("cortisone injection"): for a few swollen joints or weight-bearing joints. Fewer systemic effects. Used in inflammatory arthritis, OA and rotator cuff tendinitis.

Side effects of chronic prednisone (good short term, not long term): increased appetite and weight gain, gastritis, mood change or psychosis, infection, Cushing's, acne, osteoporosis, avascular necrosis, hypertension, hyperglycemia, cataracts and glaucoma, proximal myopathy, delayed wound healing, thin skin.

Relative contraindications (use a steroid-sparing drug instead): diabetes, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, recurrent infections, glaucoma/cataracts.

2. Joint aspiration and injection

Informed consent for every procedure: explain benefits and complications, and confirm capacity (or a substitute decision maker). Complications to mention:

  1. Infection (septic joint), very low risk
  2. Bleeding
  3. Fat atrophy or skin depigmentation (if too superficial)
  4. Tendon rupture, very small risk
  5. Small systemic absorption (worse diabetes or blood pressure control)
  6. Post-injection flare, which resolves on its own

Two benefits of arthrocentesis: diagnostic and therapeutic.

  • Diagnostic: send fluid for only three things, mnemonic C3G: Cell count (lavender top), Crystals, Culture and Gram stain (sterile container). Cell count >2,000 = inflammatory; <2,000 = non-inflammatory. >50,000 with mostly neutrophils suggests a septic joint, so consult orthopedics.
  • Therapeutic: remove fluid to relieve pain; inject steroid (e.g. triamcinolone) or hyaluronic acid (OA).
  • Always aspirate a first acute monoarthritis to rule out infection.

Relative contraindications to a steroid injection:

  1. Septic joint (or high suspicion). Get fluid first and wait until the culture is negative.
  2. Overlying cellulitis or skin infection
  3. Psoriatic plaques over the site (skin flora raise septic-joint risk)
  4. Prosthetic joint
  5. Severe thrombocytopenia or bleeding disorder
  6. Allergy to the local anesthetic

3. NSAIDs, acetaminophen and opioids

  • NSAIDs reversibly inhibit COX-1 and COX-2 (non-selective) or COX-2 only (celecoxib), lowering prostaglandins. Coxibs spare the gut-protective prostaglandins, so less GI toxicity.
  • Efficacy is similar across NSAIDs; a patient may respond better to one than another.
  • Indications: OA, RA, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, acute gout, CPPD.
  • NSAIDs are NOT disease-modifying in RA: they help pain and swelling but do not prevent erosions.
  • Side effects: hypertension, MI, stroke, gastritis and PUD, renal insufficiency. Use with caution in cardiovascular, GI and renal disease. Check interactions (anticoagulants). Avoid in the 3rd trimester (premature closure of the ductus arteriosus). Screen CBC, ALT, creatinine.
  • Acetaminophen: analgesic, not anti-inflammatory. For OA, mechanical back pain and regional pain. Up to 3 g/day (max 2 g/day in cirrhosis). Minimal side effects: no gastritis, constipation, BP or cognitive effect.
  • Opioids have a very limited role. The goal is to treat inflammation, which treats pain. Opioid side effects and misuse risk make other options preferable.

4. DMARDs

Why treat early: joint damage happens early in RA. Erosions occur in >90% within 2 years, radiographic progression is fastest in the first 2 years, and 50% are work-disabled at 10 years.

Principles ("window of opportunity"): treat early (DMARD within 3 months of symptom onset), aggressively and appropriately. Triple therapy beats a single agent in early RA. Treat to target: the goal is remission (no swollen or tender joints); adjust every 3–6 months if not there.

Starting a DMARD: discuss side effects, interactions and contraindications, do baseline blood work, and give a form for monthly monitoring.

Common toxicity mnemonic for MTX, sulfasalazine and leflunomide: GHMI = GI upset, Hepatotoxicity, Myelosuppression, Infection.

DMARD Key adverse effects Monitoring and notes

| Methotrexate (20–25 mg PO/SC weekly; onset 6–8 wk) | GHMI, fatigue, oral ulcers, acute pneumonitis (dry cough, SOB, fever), teratogenic | Baseline hep B/C ± CXR; monthly CBC, ALT, creatinine. Folic acid 5 mg weekly. Avoid pregnancy, alcohol and sulfa antibiotics (sulfasalazine is fine). Dose by eGFR | | Hydroxychloroquine (onset 6–12 wk) | Retinal toxicity (bull's-eye maculopathy, rare), GI upset, rash, photosensitivity, headache. No myelosuppression | Baseline + annual eye exam; no lab monitoring. Safe in pregnancy | | Sulfasalazine (onset 6–8 wk) | GHMI (aplastic anemia), headache, photosensitive rash, reversible oligospermia | Monthly labs. Screen G6PD; no sulfa allergy. Safe in pregnancy | | Leflunomide (onset 6–8 wk) | GHMI, hypertension, peripheral neuropathy, alopecia, pneumonitis, teratogenic | Monthly labs; check BP; avoid alcohol and pregnancy; cholestyramine washout |

Strategy: start triple therapy (MTX + sulfasalazine + hydroxychloroquine) for 3–6 months and optimize doses. If the response is incomplete: switch MTX from PO to SC, add leflunomide, or add a biologic.

Quick checks: annual eye exam → hydroxychloroquine. Hypertension → leflunomide. Lung toxicity → methotrexate and leflunomide.

5. Biologics

  • Like DMARDs, biologics suppress inflammation and prevent damage. Unlike DMARDs (non-specific mechanisms), biologics target specific molecules or cells: TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, T-cell co-stimulation, B cells.
  • Classes in RA: TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, golimumab, certolizumab), IL-6 inhibitor (tocilizumab), T-cell co-stimulation blocker (abatacept), B-cell depletion (rituximab). Specific names are not required. Newer options: JAK inhibitors (small molecules, e.g. tofacitinib) and biosimilars.
  • Expensive (~$10–20,000/year), so patients usually must fail DMARDs first.
  • TNF-α drives IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8, which act on chondrocytes and osteoclasts to erode cartilage and bone.
  • TNF inhibitor adverse effects: injection or infusion reactions, infection including TB, demyelination (MS), worsened CHF, slight malignancy risk (skin, possibly lymphoma), drug-induced lupus.
  • Contraindications: active infection, recent or recurrent infection, CHF class III/IV, active malignancy, MS (or family history).
  • Hold if fever, infection or antibiotics; flag upcoming surgery (delayed wound healing).

6. Adults vs children

⚠ Not on this year's slide PDFs: the treatment slides cover adult RA only. For contrast (from Week 11 JIA): children with oligoarticular JIA are often managed with NSAIDs and intra-articular steroid injections first, then methotrexate and biologics if needed; growth and uveitis screening are extra pediatric concerns, and chronic systemic steroids are avoided because of growth effects.
