Clinical Approach to Chronic Pelvic Pain — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Stephanie Black | Duration on Elentra: 40 min

Duration target: ~9 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Outline a clinical approach to chronic pelvic pain
  • Recognize common causes of chronic pelvic pain
  • List a differential diagnosis for chronic pelvic pain
  • Outline appropriate investigations for pelvic pain
  • Identify treatment options for common causes of pelvic pain

Defining chronic pelvic pain

Chronic pelvic pain is typically defined as non-cyclic pain in the pelvis lasting six months or longer, of sufficient severity to cause functional impairment — distinguished from acute pelvic pain both by duration and by the diagnostic approach it demands. Where acute pain workups prioritize ruling out surgical emergencies quickly, chronic pain workups prioritize a broad, structured, often multidisciplinary search across multiple organ systems, since chronic pelvic pain frequently has more than one contributing cause simultaneously.

A clinical approach: think by system, not just by organ

Because chronic pelvic pain so often has overlapping contributors, the most useful framework organizes the differential by system rather than assuming a single gynecologic diagnosis will explain everything:

  • Gynecologic: endometriosis (one of the most common identifiable causes), adenomyosis, chronic pelvic inflammatory disease/adhesions, ovarian remnant or residual ovary syndrome after prior surgery, and pelvic congestion syndrome (venous congestion, often worse with prolonged standing).
  • Gastrointestinal: irritable bowel syndrome (extremely common comorbid diagnosis with chronic pelvic pain and often under-recognized as a contributor), inflammatory bowel disease, chronic constipation.
  • Urologic: interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome, recurrent UTI, chronic urinary retention.
  • Musculoskeletal: pelvic floor myofascial dysfunction (hypertonic pelvic floor musculature — increasingly recognized as a major, treatable contributor that's frequently missed if the pelvic floor itself isn't specifically examined), abdominal wall trigger points, hip pathology.
  • Neurologic/psychological: pudendal neuralgia, nerve entrapment (including post-surgical), and central sensitization — a genuine neurophysiologic phenomenon where the nervous system's pain processing becomes amplified over time regardless of ongoing peripheral tissue pathology, which matters clinically because it means pain can persist and even worsen after the original structural cause has been addressed.

Depression and anxiety are common in chronic pelvic pain, and it's important to communicate to patients that this is not implying the pain is "not real" — chronic pain and mood disorders have genuine bidirectional physiologic relationships, and treating both together improves outcomes.

History and exam clues

A detailed history should map pain characteristics against each system above: relationship to the menstrual cycle (suggests a gynecologic driver, though chronic pain can lose its cyclic pattern over time even when it started that way), relationship to bowel movements or specific foods (GI), relationship to bladder filling/emptying (urologic), positional or movement-related triggers (musculoskeletal), and any history of prior pelvic surgery, infection, or trauma. On exam, a systematic approach includes abdominal wall palpation with muscle tensing (Carnett's sign — pain that persists or worsens with abdominal wall tensing suggests a musculoskeletal/abdominal wall source rather than a visceral one), a dedicated pelvic floor muscle exam (assessing for tenderness and hypertonicity, distinct from the standard bimanual exam), and standard bimanual/speculum assessment for the gynecologic differential.

Investigations

Pelvic ultrasound remains first-line imaging to assess for structural gynecologic pathology (fibroids, adenomyosis, endometriomas, ovarian pathology). Beyond that, investigations should be targeted by the leading differential from history and exam rather than ordered reflexively: urinalysis/urine culture if urologic symptoms are prominent, STI testing if relevant risk factors are present, and referral for diagnostic laparoscopy when endometriosis is strongly suspected but hasn't responded to empiric hormonal therapy, or when the diagnosis remains unclear despite a thorough workup. MRI has a role in specific scenarios (deep infiltrating endometriosis, adenomyosis characterization, suspected nerve entrapment) but isn't first-line for most presentations.

Treatment, matched to the identified driver(s)

Because chronic pelvic pain is often multifactorial, treatment is frequently multimodal rather than a single intervention: hormonal suppression for a gynecologic/endometriosis-driven component (as detailed in the endometriosis module); pelvic floor physiotherapy specifically for myofascial/hypertonic pelvic floor contributors — this is one of the highest-yield, most under-utilized interventions in chronic pelvic pain and should be considered early, not as a last resort; targeted management of GI or urologic contributors when identified (dietary modification and antispasmodics for IBS, bladder-directed therapy for interstitial cystitis); neuropathic pain agents (such as gabapentinoids or tricyclic antidepressants at low dose) when central sensitization or a neuropathic component is suspected; and a structured chronic pain management approach — including psychological support (CBT has good evidence in chronic pelvic pain specifically) and, where available, a multidisciplinary pelvic pain clinic — recognizing that chronic pain management is rarely a single-visit fix.

Bringing it together

The core shift chronic pelvic pain demands, compared to acute pain, is thinking across systems from the outset rather than anchoring on a single gynecologic cause — because the pelvic floor, bowel, bladder, and nervous system are frequently co-contributors, and missing them is the most common reason chronic pelvic pain fails to improve despite gynecologic treatment. A structured, multi-system history and exam, targeted investigations, and a genuinely multimodal treatment plan reflect how this condition is actually managed well in practice.
