Week 7 — Clinical Approach to Mechanical Back & Neck Pain

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the functional anatomy of the neck and back relevant to spine pathology: the distinguishing features of vertebrae at different levels, the muscles of the back and their role in back pain, and the role of ligaments and discs in spinal stability.
  • Define dermatome and myotome, and know the basic sensory/motor pattern expected with root pathology at C5–C8 and L3–S1 (full detail covered more in the non-mechanical module).
  • Build a differential diagnosis and management plan for axial pain: recognize myofascial pain, SI joint pathology, mechanical back pain, spinal stenosis, cauda equina, radiculopathy, and cervical myelopathy from history and physical.
  • Distinguish inflammatory from non-inflammatory back pain, and spondylosis from spondylolysis from spondylolisthesis.
  • Recognize normal spinal anatomy and common anomalies on plain X-ray (fracture, spondylolysis, scoliosis).
  • Build an approach to investigating and managing the common painful spine conditions above.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: back and neck pain are some of the most common presenting complaints in primary care, and a lot of patients get conflicting information because a precise anatomic diagnosis is genuinely hard to pin down in "run of the mill" back pain. The honest framing from this module: you don't always need a specific structural diagnosis — what you need is confidence that you're not missing something dangerous.

The toolkit for sorting through axial pain:
- History, structured with OLDCARS (onset, location, duration, character, aggravating/alleviating factors, radiation, severity) — plus a deliberate red-flag screen.
- Red flags, grouped by category: neurologic (progressive weakness, sensory change, saddle anesthesia, loss of hand dexterity, ataxic gait, incontinence, erectile dysfunction, abnormal reflexes); inflammatory (morning stiffness, second-half-of-night pain, family history, rash, eye symptoms, improves with exercise); neoplastic (young or old age extremes, severe unremitting night pain, unintentional weight loss, night sweats, cancer history); infectious (fevers, sweats, IVDU, immunosuppression); fracture (trauma, osteoporosis, prior fragility fracture); and vascular (vertebral artery insufficiency — dizziness/drop attacks with neck movement — or distal claudication).
- Physical exam, looking for axial abnormalities, contributory limb findings, neuro deficits, and signs pointing at the red-flag categories above.
- Imaging, used thoughtfully: X-ray is cheap and good for alignment/fracture screening, CT is best for bony detail/trauma, MRI is best for soft tissue (disc, cord, nerve roots), bone scan picks up multifocal disease. The key teaching point: most low back pain does not need imaging. If there are no red flags and symptoms have been present under 6 weeks, imaging isn't indicated — degenerative changes are extremely common on imaging even in people with zero back pain, so an abnormal scan doesn't necessarily explain the symptoms.

Mechanical back pain, defined simply: axial pain that is worse with movement and better with rest, anywhere on the acute-to-chronic spectrum, usually an accumulation of degenerative change that got "tweaked" by something minor. No red flags, by definition.

The anatomy underlying it — bones, discs, and facets. The vertebral column has four curves (cervical lordosis, thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, sacral kyphosis) and each vertebra has a body, arch, pedicles, lamina, and spinous/transverse/articular processes, with the vertebral foramen housing the cord and the intervertebral foramen letting the spinal nerve exit. Between vertebrae sit the intervertebral discs (annulus fibrosus around a nucleus pulposus, acting as shock absorbers) and the facet (zygapophyseal) joints, which are true synovial joints. Regional differences worth knowing: cervical vertebrae are small with transverse foramina (for the vertebral arteries) and bifid spinous processes; thoracic vertebrae have costal facets for rib articulation; lumbar vertebrae are large-bodied with no costal facets, built for load-bearing. C1 (atlas) and C2 (axis) are atypical — C1 has no body and forms a ring around C2's dens, which is what allows head rotation.

The disc as a pain source. With repetitive loading, the annulus fibrosus develops microtears, the nucleus pulposus degenerates and loses height, and internal disc disruption can occur even with an intact outer annulus. "Discogenic" pain classically: axial-predominant (back more than leg), movement-related, worse with forward flexion, managed conservatively (activity, NSAIDs/acetaminophen, physio). Disc herniation terminology, by degree: bulge (>50% of the circumference displaced) vs. herniation (\<50%), which subdivides into protrusion (base wider than the extruded part) and extrusion (extruded part wider than its base — and "sequestered" if it's lost continuity with the parent disc entirely). Most lumbar disc herniations happen at L4–L5 and L5–S1, which is why L5 and S1 radiculopathies are the most common.

The facet joint as a pain source. Facet (zygapophyseal) joints are synovial and degenerate like any other joint, but degeneration on imaging correlates poorly with symptoms — so imaging findings here are often a red herring. Classic facetogenic pain: localized, sometimes unilateral axial pain, can cause cervicogenic headache, rarely radiates past the knee in the lumbar spine, and is classically worse with extension and relieved by flexion (the opposite aggravating pattern from disc pain).

Put together, spondylosis is the umbrella term for degenerative changes across disc and facet — "spondy" (vertebra) + "osis" (disease state) — encompassing degenerative disc disease plus facet arthropathy with loss of disc height and ligamentous hypertrophy. Taken further, this can progress to degenerative spondylolisthesis (one vertebra slipping relative to the next due to joint/ligament laxity, as opposed to isthmic spondylolisthesis from a pars defect), and the space-occupying changes can cause central canal stenosis or foraminal (lateral) stenosis with nerve root encroachment.

Muscular causes. Acute strain/spasm and repetitive strain injury (from repetitive tasks, forceful exertion, vibration, sustained awkward posture) and myofascial pain from trigger points — hyperirritable tissue spots tender to palpation that can refer pain elsewhere. There's no diagnostic test for muscular back pain; it's a clinical diagnosis made by excluding other causes, managed with education, reassurance about the generally excellent prognosis, relative rest, heat/ice, topical or short-course oral anti-inflammatories, and physiotherapy focused on postural support.

Whiplash-associated disorder deserves a specific mention: triggered by an acceleration-deceleration mechanism (classically MVC), most commonly injuring muscle (strain/partial tear), less commonly facets, ligaments, bone, or cord. Most people improve with conservative management — under 5% have severe ongoing symptoms at 2 years. Imaging is reserved for concerning features (significant exam findings beyond simple pain/stiffness, or neuro abnormalities). Management favours staying mobile and avoiding cervical collars; aggressive physio can actually worsen symptoms early on.

The practical bottom line for deciding who gets imaged: no red flags + symptoms under 6 weeks \= no imaging. Beyond that window, with red flags, or after a real trial of conservative treatment has failed, imaging becomes reasonable — and plain X-ray specifically is there to catch red-flag pathology (fracture, malignancy), not to characterize routine mechanical pain.
