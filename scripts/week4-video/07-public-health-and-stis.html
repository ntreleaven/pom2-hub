Public Health and STIs — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Alexander Summers | Duration on Elentra: 20 min

Duration target: ~7-8 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Identify common STIs in Canada, including relevant age groups
  • Describe patterns of global disease burdens with regards to STIs
  • Identify and list risk factors for STIs
  • Review the role of public health in reporting STIs
  • Outline population level interventions to reduce the burden of STIs

Who gets STIs in Canada

Chlamydia is consistently the most commonly reported bacterial STI in Canada, followed by gonorrhea, with both showing rising trends over the past decade. Infectious syphilis has also risen sharply in recent years, including a resurgence of congenital syphilis, which had become rare and is now a genuine public health concern again. The age distribution is heavily skewed toward youth and young adults — most chlamydia and gonorrhea cases occur in people aged 15–29 — which is exactly why opportunistic screening in this age group during any healthcare encounter (not just sexual health visits) is emphasized so strongly.

The global picture

The World Health Organization estimates over a million new curable STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, trichomoniasis) acquired daily worldwide. Burden isn't evenly distributed — it's shaped by access to testing and treatment, comprehensive sex education, healthcare infrastructure, and stigma, which means lower-resource settings carry disproportionate rates of complications like infertility and congenital syphilis, not necessarily higher rates of infection itself.

Risk factors

Individual-level: multiple or new sexual partners, inconsistent condom use, a prior STI diagnosis (a strong predictor of future risk), younger age, and substance use that affects risk-taking or reduces access to care. Structural/social-level factors matter just as much: unstable housing, incarceration history, limited healthcare access, and stigma affecting willingness to seek testing — these are the drivers public health interventions are actually designed to address, beyond individual counselling.

The role of public health reporting

Several STIs are legally designated reportable diseases in Ontario — chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and HIV among them — meaning laboratories and physicians are required to report confirmed cases to the local public health unit. This isn't just paperwork: it enables partner notification and contact tracing (public health can support this when a patient can't or won't notify partners directly), outbreak detection (a cluster of cases can trigger targeted public health response), and population-level surveillance that shapes resource allocation and screening guidelines. As a clinician, understanding what's reportable and how the reporting pathway works is part of comprehensive STI management, not a separate administrative task.

Population-level interventions

Individual treatment cures one infection; population interventions are what actually bend the epidemic curve. Key strategies: routine opportunistic screening programs targeting high-incidence age groups; free or low-barrier testing and treatment access (cost and stigma are major deterrents to testing); comprehensive sexuality education; HPV vaccination programs (now demonstrably reducing cervical dysplasia rates in vaccinated cohorts); harm reduction services for people who use substances, since injection and other substance use intersects meaningfully with STI and blood-borne infection risk; and partner notification programs, which multiply the impact of each individual diagnosis by identifying and treating additional cases who might otherwise remain undiagnosed and continue transmitting.

Bringing it together

The shift this module asks for is a zoom-out: everything else in this block is about diagnosing and treating the individual patient in front of you, but STIs are also a population phenomenon, concentrated in specific age groups and shaped by structural risk factors well beyond individual behaviour. Public health reporting exists to convert individual clinical encounters into population-level action — contact tracing, outbreak response, and resource allocation — which is why timely, accurate reporting is itself part of good clinical care, not a bureaucratic afterthought.
