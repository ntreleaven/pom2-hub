Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Propose a plan for management of chronic non-cancer pain
    • Distinguish between acute, subacute and chronic pain and also between pain types (nociceptive and non-nociceptive)
    • Outline the roles of various health care providers in the management of chronic pain (ie. physiotherapy, occupational therapy, kinesiology, psychology)
    • Formulate a management plan for chronic non-cancer pain, including non-pharmacologic and pharmacologic approaches

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: chronic pain is rarely cured. The focus is on active physical and cognitive management by the patient, with medication in a supporting role.

Time course

  • Acute: seconds, simultaneous with and proportional to the cause, nociceptive
  • Subacute: hours to days, with hyperalgesia and allodynia; mainly nociceptive, some neuropathic
  • Chronic: over 3 months, may outlast tissue damage, pronounced affective component, mainly neuropathic

Pain types

Nociceptive: well localized; clear duration, intensity and quality. Transduction, conduction, transmission, perception.

Neuropathic: from a lesion or dysfunction in the nervous system, with spontaneous pain or hypersensitivity; burning, electrical, pins and needles.

Chronic pain, two categories:

  • Chronic nociceptive (minority): chronically activated nociceptors (inflammatory disease, OA); prevent further activation
  • Chronic neuropathic (majority): "wind-up" and central sensitization, reduced descending 5-HT and NE activity, abnormal processing despite resolved injury, "fear of harm" avoidance

Fibres and modulation

Fibre Myelin Function Pain quality
Aβ Myelinated Touch, pressure, vibration Non-nociceptive
Aδ Thin myelin Pinprick, cold Sharp, well localized
C Unmyelinated Heat, cold, pressure Dull, poorly localized, persistent
  • Gate control: Aβ activates inhibitory interneurons, which is why rubbing helps
  • Endogenous opioids: enkephalins, endorphins, dynorphins
  • Descending inhibition: NE and 5-HT
  • Central sensitization: NMDA receptors

Effects on the patient

Depression, anxiety, irritability; poor sleep, fatigue, reduced mobility; strained relationships; disability, lost work, substance use.

Fear of harm: avoiding activity gives less pain now, then generalizes (operant and classical conditioning) into inactivity and isolation. Hurt does not equal harm: most original injuries have healed, and activity can normalize signal processing and release endorphins.

The team

Provider Role
Physicians (anesthesia, neurology, PM&R, internal medicine, psychiatry) Review, trial medications, interventions, long-term strategy for the GP
Nursing Support, documentation, triage
Psychology Group and individual therapy, CBT, pacing, family education
Physiotherapy Mobility, strength, flexibility; modalities only as an adjunct
Occupational therapy Activity participation, pacing, ergonomics for ADLs and work
Kinesiologist General exercise prescription and supervision
Chiropractor Spinal manipulation, more helpful acutely

Refer to a multidisciplinary clinic for failure of standard analgesics, a review of options, or worsening activity, mood or isolation.

Non-pharmacologic

Active therapy has the best evidence. Individualized, supervised programs work best; any regular activity beats avoidance. Yoga and tai chi outperform general exercise for back pain. "Work hardening" programs aren't effective.

Passive modalities have little evidence: TENS slightly better than placebo; ultrasound and low-level laser no better than placebo; acupuncture moderately better; massage works when combined with exercise and education. Spinal manual therapy: possible short-term benefit, no evidence for long-term use.

Acetaminophen Adult 325–1000 mg q4–6h, max 4 g/day
Tricyclics Amitriptyline, nortriptyline Pain and sleep 10–50 mg
SNRIs Duloxetine 60 mg, venlafaxine Pain and mood
SSRIs Help mood; no significant effect on pain alone
Anticonvulsants Gabapentin 900–2700 mg/day; pregabalin Neuropathic; α2δ on calcium channels; sedation, start low; 1–2 weeks for effect
Cannabinoids Nabilone, cannabis Growing evidence; higher CBD:THC; don't smoke or vape

Opioids: the START principle

S: Should I start? History, exam, Opioid Risk Tool (male, age 16–45, personal or family substance use, psychiatric history), urine screen, stop benzodiazepines, treatment agreement, informed consent.

T: Trial. Start low, go slow; avoid driving during titration. Optimal dose: improved function or at least 30% pain reduction, balanced against plateau and adverse effects. Watchful dose: 90 mg morphine equivalent per day.

A: A's of monitoring. Analgesia, ADLs, adverse effects, aberrant behaviour (escalating dose, altering route, illegal activity). Complications: neuroendocrine effects, sleep apnea, opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

R: Restrict use. History of addiction (strong recommendation against; under 20 MED/day if used); active psychiatric disorder (stabilize first); elderly (lower, slower); adolescents; pregnancy (taper).

T: Terminate. Addiction, misuse, poor effect. Taper from 10% of daily dose per day to 10% per month, slowing at a third of the original dose. Withdrawal: nausea, diarrhea, aches, chills, hyperalgesia, anxiety. Switching opioids: give 50% or less of the previous MED if high dose; 60–70% if moderate or low.

Take-home: always use adjuvants before opioids, take a multimodal approach, and educate that activity maintains function and controls pain.
