Part 1 covers knee anatomy, the ligament injuries and the meniscal injuries. PFPS and ITB syndrome are in Part 2.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: most acute knee injuries in sport are ligament or meniscus injuries. The mechanism, the timing of the swelling, and a handful of exam tests usually tell you which structure is torn before any imaging.

Objective 1: osteology and X-ray views

  • The knee has three articulations: the medial and lateral tibiofemoral joints and the patellofemoral joint.
  • Femur: medial and lateral condyles and epicondyles, the patellar surface, the intercondylar fossa (notch).
  • Tibia: medial and lateral condyles (the tibial plateau), the intercondylar eminence, the tibial tuberosity.
  • Fibula: the head. Patella.
  • Views:
    • AP and lateral
    • Axial ("skyline"): good for the patellofemoral joint and patellar fractures
    • Tunnel view: good for loose bodies in the notch

Objective 1: the joint, capsule and four cardinal ligaments

  • A hinge synovial joint with some gliding and rotation. It is the largest joint in the body, with the largest capsule and synovium.
  • Stability comes mainly from the muscles (especially the quadriceps), with ligaments adding to it.
  • Capsule reinforcements:
    • Anterior: quadriceps tendon, patellar ligament, medial and lateral patellar retinacula (from the vasti).
    • Medial: the tibial collateral ligament (MCL), plus the pes anserinus, semimembranosus and medial gastrocnemius. The deep MCL fibres are firmly attached to the medial meniscus.
    • Lateral: the cord-like fibular collateral ligament (LCL), plus biceps femoris and the IT band.
    • Posterior: the oblique popliteal ligament (from semimembranosus) and the arcuate popliteal ligament.

Cruciate ligaments (named for where they attach on the tibia; they sit inside the capsule but outside the synovial membrane):

ACL PCL
Course Lateral femoral condyle → anterior intercondylar tibia Medial femoral condyle → posterior intercondylar tibia
Strength Thinner, weaker Broader, stronger
Taut in Extension Flexion
Foot planted Stops the femur sliding posteriorly on the tibia Stops the femur sliding anteriorly on the tibia
Foot free Stops the tibia sliding anteriorly under the femur (and resists hyperextension and medial rotation) Stops the tibia sliding posteriorly under the femur

PCL "dashboard injury": a blow to the front of the tibia with the knee flexed drives the tibia backward and tears the PCL. The patellar ligament may avulse from the tibial tuberosity.

Objective 1: the menisci and the locking mechanism

Medial meniscus Lateral meniscus
C-shaped, broader posteriorly Nearly circular, smaller
Firmly attached to the MCL (tibial collateral ligament), so less mobile Freely movable
More often injured Less susceptible
  • Both deepen and stabilize the joint and act as shock absorbers. The transverse ligament of the knee joins them anteriorly.
  • Blood supply: the peripheral meniscus is richly vascular. The central meniscus is avascular, nourished by synovial fluid. This is why peripheral ("red") tears can be repaired and central ("white") tears are resected.
  • The screw-home mechanism: in the last 15–20° of extension, the femur medially rotates on the tibia, locking the knee with maximally tight ligaments for "quiet standing". To flex, popliteus laterally rotates the femur about 5°, "unlocking" the knee. It also pulls the lateral meniscus back.

Objective 2: ligament injuries

ACL injury (case: a football player pivots, the knee "moves" and gives way, and he can't bear weight because of pain and swelling).

  • Risk factors: female (4.5:1), pivoting sports, anatomic and biomechanical factors.
  • Mechanism: non-contact in 60–80%, cutting or pivoting while decelerating. Contact in 30%: a direct blow causing hyperextension or valgus.
  • Presentation: a "pop", immediate swelling (hemarthrosis), a feeling of instability or "giving out", and lateral knee pain from a bone contusion.
  • Evaluation:
    • Lachman (knee at ~30°; the gold standard), anterior drawer (hip 45°, knee 90°, hamstrings relaxed), pivot shift.
    • X-rays to rule out fracture (usually normal). MRI if the exam can't decide.
  • Treatment: RICE, weight bearing as tolerated, crutches as needed, physiotherapy, and orthopaedic referral for reconstruction.
  • Return to play 9–12 months after surgery, with a 3–6% reinjury rate.

PCL injury:

  • Mechanism: a direct blow to the anterior tibia with the knee flexed (dashboard), or hyperextension.
  • Presentation: poorly defined pain, sometimes posterior, with minimal swelling. Less common than ACL tears. Often comes with meniscal or chondral injury.
  • Evaluation: posterior drawer, sag sign, reverse Lachman. X-rays and stress X-rays for grading. MRI if a posterolateral corner (PLC) injury is suspected.
  • Treatment: physiotherapy is generally enough, plus bracing. Reconstruction only for rotatory instability or a posterolateral corner injury.
  • About 50% return to the same or a higher level.

MCL injury:

  • Very common, almost always from a collision with a valgus force.
  • Minimal to no swelling. Sometimes comes with a medial meniscal tear, and a complete tear often comes with an ACL tear.
  • Test with valgus stress on the flexed knee.
  • Ranges from sprain to complete tear. Treatment: early range of motion and bracing.

LCL injury:

  • Uncommon in isolation. A collision with a varus force (high energy). No swelling in isolation.
  • Associated with PCL injuries. Test with varus stress.
  • Treatment: bracing, with surgical repair if there are other injuries or instability.

Objective 2: meniscal injuries

(Case: a hockey player's knee twists with his skate caught. He has immediate medial pain and swelling that develops later that night.)

  • Tear types: longitudinal, degenerative, flap, radial, bucket handle, horizontal cleavage. Tears are also described by vascular zone: red-red, red-white, white-white.
  • Mechanism: depends on age and degeneration. Classically rotation or twisting with the foot planted.
  • Presentation:
    • medial or lateral joint line pain, after the injury or delayed
    • delayed or intermittent swelling and effusions
    • possibly mechanical symptoms: locking and catching
  • Evaluation: effusion and joint line tenderness, McMurray and Thessaly tests. X-rays (usually normal, but they show the degree of OA). MRI if conservative treatment fails or a large tear is suspected.

Degenerative meniscal tears (older, with OA):

  • Physio (keep ROM, strengthen the quads and hamstrings; active rather than passive therapy).
  • Oral OTC analgesics and intra-articular corticosteroid. Bracing and activity modification.
  • Surgery depends on the presence and severity of OA: arthroscopic repair vs debridement. Arthroplasty is the last resort.

Traumatic meniscal tears (young):

  • Physio first.
  • Repair red or red-white zone tears, larger tears, bucket-handle tears, root tears, and tears in younger patients.
  • Resect white-zone and chronic degenerative tears (partial meniscectomy). Meniscal transplantation is an option.
  • Return to play: 6 weeks to 3 months, depending on repair vs meniscectomy.

The "unhappy triad": a lateral blow forcing the knee into valgus:

  1. The MCL stretches and tears.
  2. A bucket-handle tear of the medial meniscus follows, because the meniscus is attached to the MCL.
  3. Forced abduction, external rotation and slight flexion tear the ACL.

Bottom line:

  • The ACL is taut in extension and stops the tibia sliding forward: test with Lachman. A non-contact pivot, pop and immediate hemarthrosis mean ACL. Reconstruct it and expect 9–12 months to return.
  • The PCL is the strongest ligament, taut in flexion. Dashboard mechanism, little swelling, posterior drawer and sag sign. Usually physio alone.
  • MCL: valgus, common, brace plus early ROM. LCL: varus, rare alone, goes with PCL injuries.
  • The medial meniscus is fixed to the MCL, so it tears more often. Twisting with a planted foot, joint line pain, delayed swelling, locking. McMurray and Thessaly tests. Repair red-zone tears and resect white-zone tears.
  • Unhappy triad = MCL + medial meniscus + ACL.
  • Screw-home: the femur medially rotates to lock the knee, and popliteus unlocks it.
