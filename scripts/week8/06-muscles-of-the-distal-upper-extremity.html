This module covers the muscle and tendon problems of the elbow: the epicondyloses and distal biceps.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: "tennis elbow" and "golfer's elbow" are among the most common things you'll see in primary care. They make sense once you know that the forearm flexors start on the medial epicondyle and the extensors on the lateral one. The other big lesson is that most of these are tendinosis (degeneration), not tendinitis, which changes how you treat them.

Objective 1: how the forearm is organized

The antebrachial fascia wraps the forearm. The radius (lateral) and ulna (medial) are joined by the interosseous membrane. These, plus the anterior intermuscular septum, make two compartments:

  • Anterior: flexes the wrist and hand and pronates the forearm. Common origin is the medial epicondyle (except the deep layer). Nerve: median, except 1½ muscles on the ulnar nerve. Three layers: 4 superficial, 1 intermediate, 3 deep.
  • Posterior: extends the wrist and hand and supinates the forearm. Common origin is the lateral epicondyle (except the deep layer). Nerve: all radial (the deep branch / posterior interosseous nerve). Two layers: 6 superficial, 5 deep.

At the wrist, the palmar carpal ligament (front) is continuous with the extensor retinaculum (back). Together they stop tendons bowstringing. The flexor retinaculum is a separate, deeper and more distal band. Don't confuse them.

Anterior compartment: the flexor-pronators

Superficial layer (4), all from the medial epicondyle:

  • Pronator teres: medial epicondyle + ulnar coronoid → lateral radius. Pronates and flexes the elbow. Median.
  • Flexor carpi radialis: → base of the 2nd (3rd) metacarpal. Flexes the wrist and abducts it (radial deviation). Median.
  • Palmaris longus: → palmar aponeurosis. Flexes the wrist and tenses the aponeurosis. Median.
  • Flexor carpi ulnaris: → pisiform, hook of hamate, 5th metacarpal. Flexes the wrist and adducts it (ulnar deviation). Ulnar (the first of the "1½").

Intermediate layer (1):

  • Flexor digitorum superficialis: medial epicondyle + radius → middle phalanges of digits 2–5. Flexes the wrist and the MCP (and PIP) joints. Median.

Deep layer (3), not from the epicondyle:

  • Flexor digitorum profundus: proximal ¾ of the ulna → distal phalanges of digits 2–5. Flexes the wrist and IP joints, and is the only muscle that flexes the DIP. Split nerve supply: the lateral half (digits 2–3) is median via the anterior interosseous nerve, and the medial half (digits 4–5) is ulnar (the other "½").
  • Flexor pollicis longus: anterior radius → distal phalanx of the thumb. Median (anterior interosseous).
  • Pronator quadratus: distal ulna → distal radius. Pronates. Median (anterior interosseous).

Posterior compartment: the extensor-supinators (all radial)

Superficial (6), from the lateral epicondyle / supracondylar ridge:

  • Brachioradialis: → distal lateral radius. Weakly flexes the elbow, strongest in mid-pronation: the "drinking muscle". A posterior muscle that flexes.
  • Extensor carpi radialis longus (→ base of the 2nd metacarpal) and brevis (→ base of the 3rd metacarpal): extend and abduct the wrist. ECRL is active in fist-clenching.
  • Extensor digitorum and extensor digiti minimi: → the extensor expansion. They extend the MCPs primarily and the IPs secondarily.
  • Extensor carpi ulnaris: → base of the 5th metacarpal. Extends and adducts the wrist, and is active in fist-clenching.
  • The notes add anconeus: weak elbow extension and elbow stabilization.

Deep (5), deep radial branch / posterior interosseous nerve:

  • Supinator: lateral epicondyle + ulna → proximal third of the radius. Supinates. The deep radial nerve pierces it.
  • Extensor indicis: → extensor expansion of the index finger, for independent index extension.
  • The "outcropping" thumb muscles: abductor pollicis longus, extensor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis. They border the anatomical snuffbox (floor: scaphoid and trapezium, with the radial artery running in it).

Wrist deviation needs a flexor and an extensor together: radial deviation = FCR + ECRL + ECRB · ulnar deviation = FCU + ECU.

Extensor features: all extensor tendons run in synovial sheaths through 6 osseofibrous tunnels under the extensor retinaculum. The extensor expansion is a triangular hood over each digit that extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi, extensor indicis, the lumbricals and the interossei all insert into.

Clinical anatomy:

  • Ganglion (synovial) cyst: clear, mucinous fluid, usually on the dorsum of the wrist, connected to the tendon sheaths. Treatment: nothing, or drainage or excision. (The old "Bible bump" smash is not recommended.)
  • Mallet (baseball) finger: the extensor tendon is partly torn from the base of the distal phalanx, usually by a sudden hyperflexion (a jammed finger), so the patient can't extend the DIP. Treatment: RICE and splinting. A complete avulsion fracture needs surgery to pin the fragment.

Objective 2: muscle and tendon problems at the elbow

Tendon problems affect elbow pain and strength. Unlike joint problems, they don't take away range of motion.

Tendinitis vs tendinosis ("does the undifferentiated doc really care? YES!"):

Tendinitis Tendinosis
How common less common than we think more common than we think
What true inflammation: rheumatologic disease, acute overload, infection collagen degeneration, not the inflammatory cascade. Immature, disorganized collagen and abnormal vascularity
Cause acute injury chronic overuse with insufficient recovery
Treatment reduce inflammation: RICE, anti-inflammatories, treat the cause no inflammation to reduce: therapies that drive collagen recovery, plus analgesia
Recovery faster slow: "100+ days" to make new collagen

Lateral epicondylitis/osis ("tennis elbow").

  • Common: 1–3% of the population a year, ages 30s–40s, occupational more than recreational.
  • It is a tendinopathy mostly of extensor carpi radialis brevis (or other muscles at the common extensor origin).
  • Pain is just distal to the lateral epicondyle. The classic complaint is pain and weakness, especially gripping and supination.
  • Exam: tender over the common extensor tendon, pain on resisted middle-finger extension ± wrist extension ± supination, and pain on passive stretch of the extensor origin.

Medial epicondylitis/osis ("golfer's elbow").

  • A tendinopathy at the common flexor origin, from the stress of the golf swing or repeated wrist flexion.
  • Exam: pain at the flexor origin, pain on resisted wrist flexion and pronation, and pain on passive stretch.

Biceps tendinopathy and rupture.

  • Tendinopathy: insidious onset, pain on resistance and on stretch, usual treatments.
  • Rupture: trauma or the end stage of overuse. A "pop" heard or felt, pain (sometimes none), bruising, weakness, and a change in muscle contour.
  • Proximal (long or short head) rupture: anterior shoulder pain, not uncommon (especially in the elderly), often degenerative. The arm usually still works, and it's typically managed non-operatively.
  • Distal rupture: anterior elbow pain. Rare but consequential, with poor supination and weak flexion. "Call 1-800-ORTHO, limited time offer": it needs prompt surgical repair.

Managing any tendinitis/osis:

  • Education: many improve quickly, some become chronic. Address the offending movements.
  • Non-pharmacologic: ice and RICE · modify the activity · rest by time or by task · fix ergonomics and biomechanics · orthoses and taping (a tennis elbow counterforce strap during the offending activity) · PT: eccentric strengthening twice a day for 12 weeks to stimulate collagen · massage, which may stimulate fibroblasts.
  • Pharmacologic: anti-inflammatories can be tried but may not help much in chronic tendinosis (topical preferred).
  • Interventional: regenerative options such as PRP are uncertain. Surgical referral if refractory.
  • Practice pointer: don't just ask "have you tried physio?" Ask them to "show me what you've been doing."

Bottom line: medial epicondyle = flexor-pronators on the median nerve (except FCU and FDP to digits 4–5, which are ulnar). Lateral epicondyle = extensor-supinators, all radial. FDP is the only DIP flexor. Brachioradialis is the posterior muscle that flexes. Tennis = ECRB, pain on resisted extension. Golfer = flexor origin, pain on resisted flexion and pronation. Treat tendinosis with eccentric loading for 12 weeks. A distal biceps rupture goes to orthopaedics fast.
