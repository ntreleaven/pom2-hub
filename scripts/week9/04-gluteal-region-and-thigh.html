Week 9 — Anatomy of the Gluteal Region & Thigh

Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the lower extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of leg pathology.
    • Compare and contrast the stability and mobility of the glenohumeral and hip joints, as determined by static (bony and ligamentous) and dynamic (muscular) stabilizers.
    • Identify the bony landmarks of the lower extremity and relate these to their appearances on plain radiographs.
    • Identify the lumbosacral plexus and its branches and describe what functional consequences would occur with lesions at different levels.
    • Trace the arterial blood supply and venous drainage of the lower extremity, and identify critical areas of collateral circulation.
    • Identify the femoral triangle, its anatomic borders and its contents.
    • Identify the major muscles of the buttock and their functions, and explain the anatomic basis of Trendelenburg sign/lurch/gait.
    • Identify the compartments of the thigh and leg and describe the function and innervation of the muscles in each compartment.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: this is the anatomy behind the limp after a hip replacement (Trendelenburg gait), the safe place to give an IM injection, the groin bulge below the inguinal ligament, and the hamstring "pop" in a hurdler. It's a big module, so the organizing rule is simple: each thigh compartment has one main job and one nerve.

The gluteal region: spaces and landmarks

  • The sacrotuberous (sacrum → ischial tuberosity) and sacrospinous (sacrum → ischial spine) ligaments turn the sciatic notches into the greater and lesser sciatic foramina.
  • Greater sciatic foramen: pelvis → gluteal region. It carries piriformis, the superior and inferior gluteal vessels and nerves, the sciatic nerve, the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh, and the nerve to quadratus femoris.
  • Lesser sciatic foramen: gluteal region → perineum. The pudendal nerve, internal pudendal vessels and the nerve to obturator internus pass through both foramina.
  • Piriformis is the key landmark. Only the superior gluteal nerve and artery exit above it. Everything else exits below: the inferior gluteal nerve and artery, the sciatic nerve, the pudendal nerve (S2–S4), and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh.
  • Surface anatomy: the iliac crests at L4, the intergluteal cleft, the greater trochanter, the ischial tuberosity, the gluteal fold.

Objective: gluteal muscles and the Trendelenburg sign

Muscle Nerve Main action
Gluteus maximus Inferior gluteal (myotome S1) Hip extension; standing up from a seat. Inserts on the gluteal tuberosity + IT band
Gluteus medius & minimus Superior gluteal Abduction & medial rotation; gait stabilization. Insert on the greater trochanter
Tensor fasciae latae Superior gluteal (myotome L5) Flexes hip, tenses the IT band (→ Gerdy's tubercle)
Piriformis Nerve to piriformis Lateral rotation, abduction, hip stabilization
Obturator internus + superior gemellus Nerve to obturator internus Lateral rotation; stabilize the femoral head
Quadratus femoris + inferior gemellus Nerve to quadratus femoris Lateral rotation

The Trendelenburg sign (the slide's case is a 72-year-old two weeks after a total hip replacement):

  • When you stand on one leg, the gluteus medius and minimus of the stance leg contract to keep the pelvis level.
  • If they are weak, usually from superior gluteal nerve injury (hip replacement surgery is a classic cause), the pelvis drops on the unsupported (swing) side.
  • So the weakness is on the side opposite the drop: the stance side.
  • To clear the foot, the patient leans the trunk toward the weak stance side. This is the gluteus medius (Trendelenburg) lurch/gait.

The sciatic nerve (L4–S3):

  • It is the largest nerve in the body and is really two nerves in one sheath: tibial and common peroneal (fibular).
  • Causes of sciatic injury: herniated disc, hip surgery, piriformis syndrome (the nerve can pass through the muscle), and misplaced intragluteal injection.
  • Give IM gluteal injections in the upper outer (superolateral) quadrant to avoid the sciatic nerve. To find it, put your index finger on the ASIS and spread your fingers back along the iliac crest.

Objective: compartments of the thigh

The fascia lata sends medial, lateral and posterior intermuscular septa to the femur, making three compartments:

Compartment Job Nerve
Anterior Flex hip, extend knee Femoral (L2–L4)
Medial Adduct hip Obturator (L2–L4), except pectineus (femoral) and the hamstring part of adductor magnus (tibial)
Posterior Extend hip, flex knee Sciatic (tibial and common peroneal divisions)

Posterior compartment: the hamstrings (myotome L5):

  • Biceps femoris: the long head starts on the ischial tuberosity and is supplied by the tibial division. The short head starts on the linea aspera and is supplied by the common peroneal division, the only hamstring part that is. It inserts on the head of the fibula.
  • Semitendinosus (→ pes anserinus) and semimembranosus (→ the medial tibial condyle): both start on the ischial tuberosity and are supplied by the tibial division.
  • Pes anserinus = Sartorius, Gracilis, semiTendinosus ("SGT"), on the anteromedial proximal tibia, superficial to the MCL.
  • Hamstring strain: active contraction against passive stretch. Severe pain, and bruising from torn vessels. Forcible hip flexion with the knee straight can cause an avulsion fracture of the ischial tuberosity (the hurdler case: a pop, then extensive posterior thigh bleeding days later).
  • Blood supply: the posterior compartment has no artery of its own. It gets perforating branches of the profunda femoris plus the inferior gluteal, medial circumflex and popliteal arteries.

Popliteal fossa:

  • Boundaries: biceps femoris (superolateral), semimembranosus and semitendinosus (superomedial), and the two heads of gastrocnemius (inferior).
  • Contents: the tibial and common peroneal nerves, the popliteal artery and vein (and genicular branches), the end of the small saphenous vein, lymph nodes and fat.
  • From deep to superficial: Artery, Vein, Nerve ("AVN").
  • The femoral vessels reach the fossa through the adductor hiatus, in adductor magnus.
  • Popliteal aneurysm: a pulsatile mass in the popliteal fossa. It is the commonest peripheral aneurysm, and 80% are asymptomatic. It risks rupture, thrombosis or embolism and can threaten the leg.

Anterior compartment:

  • Iliopsoas (psoas from T12–L5, iliacus from the ilium → lesser trochanter): the main hip flexor. Supplied by the femoral nerve plus anterior rami L1–L3. Myotome L1/2.
  • Pectineus (superior pubic ramus): adducts and flexes. Femoral nerve.
  • Sartorius (ASIS → pes anserinus): flexes, abducts and laterally rotates the hip, and flexes the knee (the "tailor's" position).
  • Quadriceps femoris: rectus femoris (AIIS, so it also flexes the hip), vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius. All insert on the tibial tuberosity via the quadriceps/patellar tendon. It is the powerful knee extensor.
  • The patellar reflex tests the femoral nerve and L3–L4. Myotome L3/4.

Nerves of the anterior thigh:

  • Femoral nerve (L2–L4): passes deep to the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral vessels. It gives muscular and cutaneous branches and ends as the saphenous nerve (anteromedial knee, leg and foot).
  • Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (L2–L3): passes under the inguinal ligament near the ASIS to the lateral thigh.

Medial compartment (myotome L3):

  • Adductor longus and brevis (pubis → linea aspera).
  • Adductor magnus: the adductor part goes to the linea aspera, is supplied by the obturator nerve, and adducts and flexes. The hamstring part goes to the adductor tubercle, is supplied by the tibial nerve, and adducts and extends. The adductor hiatus is the gap between the two parts.
  • Gracilis (pubis → pes anserinus): adducts, flexes the knee and helps medial rotation. It is a weak adductor, so it can be harvested as a graft with no deficit.
  • Obturator externus: lateral rotation.
  • Obturator nerve (L2–L4): runs through the obturator foramen. Its anterior and posterior divisions straddle adductor brevis.
  • Groin pull: a stretch or tear of the adductors near their origin (soccer, sprinting, gymnastics). It causes bruising and pain and is treated conservatively with RICE and NSAIDs.
  • Teen avulsions: the growth plate is weaker than the tendon, so forceful hip flexion can avulse the ASIS (sartorius and TFL origin).

Objective: arteries, collateral circulation, veins and lymph

  • The external iliac becomes the femoral artery under the inguinal ligament. The femoral pulse is at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament. Branches:
    • Profunda femoris (deep femoral), which gives the medial circumflex femoral (most of the femoral head's supply), the lateral circumflex femoral (ascending, transverse and descending branches) and 3–4 perforating branches to the posterior thigh.
  • The femoral artery runs through the adductor canal (sartorius, vastus medialis, adductor longus), then the adductor hiatus, and becomes the popliteal artery.
  • Collateral circulation:
    • Around the hip, the cruciate anastomosis: inferior gluteal + medial and lateral circumflex + first perforating artery. It keeps the limb alive after femoral artery ligation.
    • Around the knee, the genicular anastomosis: 4 genicular branches of the popliteal artery + the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery. It maintains flow in full flexion or with a popliteal occlusion.
  • Femoral vein: continues from the popliteal vein up the adductor canal and becomes the external iliac vein.
  • Great saphenous vein: runs from the dorsal venous arch, up the medial leg and thigh with the saphenous nerve, superficial to the fascia lata, through the saphenous opening into the femoral vein. It is harvested for coronary bypass, and the saphenous nerve is at risk when it is.
  • Lymph: the superficial inguinal nodes drain the thigh, medial leg, perineum and abdominal wall below the umbilicus. They drain on to the deep inguinal nodes and then the external iliac nodes.

Objective: the femoral triangle

  • Borders: inguinal ligament (superior), sartorius (lateral), adductor longus (medial).
  • Contents, lateral to medial: NAVEL: femoral Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space (femoral canal), Lymphatics.
  • The femoral sheath encloses the artery, vein and canal, but not the nerve. Its entrance is the femoral ring.
  • Femoral hernia: bowel through the femoral ring into the canal. The slide case is a groin bulge below the inguinal ligament that grows with crunches.

Bottom line:

  • Piriformis is the landmark: only the superior gluteal nerve and artery exit above it.
  • Superior gluteal nerve injury (classically after hip replacement) weakens gluteus medius and minimus. The pelvis drops on the swing side, the weakness is on the stance side, and the patient lurches toward it.
  • IM injections go in the upper outer quadrant.
  • Thigh compartments: anterior (femoral nerve) flexes the hip and extends the knee; medial (obturator) adducts; posterior (sciatic) extends the hip and flexes the knee. The short head of biceps femoris is the only common peroneal hamstring.
  • Femoral triangle: inguinal ligament, sartorius, adductor longus, with NAVEL inside. The nerve is outside the femoral sheath.
  • Popliteal fossa from deep to superficial: AVN.
  • Collateral circulation: the cruciate anastomosis around the hip and the genicular anastomosis around the knee.
