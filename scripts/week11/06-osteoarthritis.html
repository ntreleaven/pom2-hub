Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Create a plan to evaluate and treat a patient presenting with monoarthritis
    • Define and describe osteoarthritis, including its basic pathophysiology, epidemiology, risk factors and radiographic features.
    • Propose an approach to the diagnosis and treatment of osteoarthritis through non-pharmacologic, pharmacologic and surgical approaches.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: OA is the most common joint disorder worldwide, and a leading cause of disability. It is a clinical diagnosis, and most treatment is movement, not scans or arthroscopy.

Chronic monoarthritis differential

Osteoarthritis, osteoarthritis, osteoarthritis, then chronic crystal arthritis (gout, CPPD, basic calcium phosphate). First separate it from mechanical or sports causes of chronic knee pain.

Less commonly:

  • Autoimmune arthritis (RA, seronegative, JIA)
  • Neuropathic (Charcot) joint (diabetes, syphilis)
  • Chronic infection (atypical mycobacteria), usually with immunosuppression
  • Neoplasm (pigmented villonodular synovitis, synovial chondromatosis)
  • Avascular necrosis or spontaneous osteonecrosis

Typical presentation

  • One affected joint: knee >> hip; ankle; shoulder >> first MTP; CMC and hand usually occur with polyarthritis
  • Pain, aching or stiffness brought on or aggravated by activity
  • Widespread chronic pain suggests something else
  • Morning stiffness under 30 minutes, usually under 5
  • Exam: joint tenderness, cool to similar-temperature effusion (not warm or hot), joint thickening

Red flags to exclude: infection, autoimmune disease, malignancy, fracture, avascular necrosis.

Case: Mrs. Tran. A 52-year-old with diabetes and worsening knee pain limiting walking and stairs, 5 minutes of morning stiffness, a slight antalgic gait, a slightly swollen but cool knee, joint line tenderness and crepitus. Diagnosis: chronic monoarthritis due to knee OA.

Diagnosis and staging

OA is a clinical diagnosis.

  • Must have: joint pain, stiffness or aching on most days, chronic (over 6 weeks), worse with activity
  • Must not have: other causes (autoimmune, crystal, infection, malignancy, tendinopathy)
  • X-ray changes alone do NOT diagnose OA, although the findings are specific
  • X-ray is for staging: early, established, late or end-stage
  • MRI and bone scan: almost never. Only when the diagnosis is in doubt.

Radiographic features: osteophytes, joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis and cysts, on the Kellgren-Lawrence scale.

  • K/L 1–2: early OA
  • K/L 3–4: late to end-stage OA

Epidemiology

  • 1 in 7 Canadian adults over 20
  • Most common joint disorder worldwide
  • 12% of Canadian workers have symptomatic OA
  • $27.5B cost to Canadians in 2010
  • Symptomatic hip and knee OA is the 11th leading cause of disability worldwide, above TB and heart disease
  • Most symptomatic knee OA starts between ages 35 and 55

Risk factors

Primary OA has risk factors but no clear cause; it is the majority.

Secondary OA has an underlying cause:

  • Post-traumatic (ACL and meniscus tears)
  • Metabolic or endocrine (acromegaly, hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis)
  • Rare monogenic disease (Stickler, Marshall, dysplasias)
  • Joints damaged by inflammatory arthritis

Major factors raising risk of primary OA:

  • Obesity and metabolic syndrome
  • Ageing and senescence
  • Mechanical factors: joint trauma, alignment, muscle weakness, occupation
  • Genetics (polygenic)
  • Female sex (1.4×)

Case: Ms. Markus. A 36-year-old soccer player with an ACL tear at 23, pain with running for 5 years and now swelling. She has post-traumatic knee OA; she isn't too young. Her risk of knee replacement by age 55 is over 50%.

Pathophysiology

  • OA is a disease of the entire joint organ: the "sick joint"
  • OA is NOT caused by wear and tear or by age, although cartilage thins with age and risk rises
  • Risk factors make joint tissues more susceptible
  • Chronic metabolic and physiologic stress causes inflammation, pain and tissue breakdown
  • Joint failure (like heart failure) comes from tissues failing to heal
  • Treatment goals include removing what impairs healing (obesity, sedentary life)

Approach to management: "the only slide you really need"

  1. What stage? Early, late, end-stage.
  2. Which biomechanical factors can be treated? Alignment, overuse, weakness, gait.
  3. Is there a role for medical treatment? Improve pain, function, quality of life.
  4. Do they need joint replacement? End-stage disease with unacceptable symptoms and quality of life.

Goals: no approved treatment preserves or extends the life of the joint, but symptomatic treatment improves life with the joint. Limitations are cost, time and access.

Non-pharmacologic (hip and knee)

  • Weight: lose 5% of total body weight
  • Activity: 150 minutes a week (walk, swim, bike); neuromuscular control (Tai Chi, GLA:D)
  • Devices: walking aids, insoles, shoes, unloader brace
  • Joint protection: education, avoiding risky movements (OT)
  • Self-management: CBT
  • Topical heat

Physiotherapy means ACTIVE therapy with exercise, preferably neuromuscular. Passive therapies (ultrasound, TENS, laser, acupuncture) are not recommended.

Pharmacologic (hip and knee)

  • Topical NSAIDs: diclofenac BID–TID
  • Acetaminophen: clinically meaningful effect uncertain
  • Oral NSAIDs: if no contraindications
  • SNRI: duloxetine
  • Tramadol: generally avoid opioids
  • Intra-articular corticosteroid: short-term relief

Inconclusive: herbal remedies, glucosamine, topical NSAID in hip OA, intra-articular HA, PRP, stem cells.

Not recommended: opioids, neuropathic pain modulators, capsaicin, chondroitin.

Surgery

  • Recommended: joint replacement, a salvage procedure for end-stage disease with strong level 1 evidence
  • Not recommended: knee arthroscopy, with or without meniscectomy or repair; strong level 1 evidence of no meaningful benefit

Case: Mr. Liebowitz. A 68-year-old with chronic renal failure and varus knee OA. Plan: physio for at least 3 months, OT education, topical NSAID (avoid oral NSAIDs because of his renal failure), a steroid injection if refractory, then an SNRI trial for 2–3 months. Two years later he is using a cane with bone-on-bone X-rays despite full treatment, so refer to orthopedics for knee arthroplasty.

Take-home: diagnose clinically, stage with X-ray, treat with exercise and education first, and keep surgery for end-stage disease.
