High-Yield Content

Three big ideas: the pterion is thin bone over the middle meningeal artery; the meningeal spaces explain the three intracranial bleeds; the cavernous sinus collects face infections and holds CN III, IV, V1, V2, VI and the internal carotid.

1. Osteology

Skull = 22 bones (plus 3 pairs of ear ossicles):

  • Neurocranium (8): frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, occipital, 2 parietal, 2 temporal. Flat plates for protection.
  • Viscerocranium (14): maxillae, zygomatic, nasal, lacrimal, palatine, inferior nasal conchae (pairs), vomer, mandible. Frame the sensory organs.

Sutures and landmarks:

  • Coronal (frontal–parietal), sagittal (between parietals), lambdoid (parietal–occipital), squamous (parietal–temporal).
  • Bregma = coronal + sagittal. Lambda = sagittal + lambdoid.
  • Pterion = H-shaped junction of frontal, parietal, sphenoid and temporal bones. Clinical: a fracture here can tear the middle meningeal artery → epidural hematoma.

Newborn skull:

  • Fontanelles are membranous gaps that allow moulding in the birth canal and brain growth. Anterior (at bregma) and posterior (at lambda), plus sphenoid and mastoid. Most close by 8–12 months.
  • Palpating them assesses hydration, intracranial pressure and bone growth.
  • Craniosynostosis = premature closure of a suture → abnormal head shape. Sagittal → long narrow head (scaphocephaly); coronal and metopic can also fuse. May raise ICP, cause developmental delay or seizures.

Bones to know:

  • Temporal: squamous, mastoid, tympanic and petrous parts (petrous holds the inner ear).
  • Sphenoid: body with sella turcica, lesser wing, greater wing; optic canal, superior orbital fissure, foramen rotundum, ovale, spinosum.
  • Ethmoid: crista galli, cribriform plate (olfactory foramina), ethmoidal air cells.
  • Maxilla: infraorbital foramen (V2).
  • Paranasal sinuses: frontal, ethmoidal, sphenoidal, maxillary.

Calvarial fractures (notes): linear (most common), depressed (fragment pushes into brain), comminuted, and contrecoup (opposite side from the blow).

3. Meninges and spaces

  • Dura mater ("tough mother"): outer periosteal + inner meningeal layer. The meningeal layer folds in to form dural reflections.
  • Arachnoid ("spider-like"): thin, doesn't enter sulci; forms arachnoid granulations.
  • Pia ("faithful"): clings to the brain and enters the sulci.
Space Between Real or potential? Bleed

| Epidural | Skull and dura | Potential | Arterial (middle meningeal) | | Subdural | Dura and arachnoid | Potential | Venous (bridging veins) | | Subarachnoid | Arachnoid and pia | Real — CSF, cerebral arteries and veins | Arterial (aneurysm) |

Meningitis = inflammation of the meninges. Headache, fever, drowsiness; can progress to sepsis, coma and death. Signs: Kernig's (straight-leg raise hurts), Brudzinski's (neck flexion lifts the legs), nuchal rigidity. Confirm with lumbar puncture (CSF sample, below the end of the cord).

Dura blood supply and nerves: mostly the middle meningeal artery (from the maxillary artery of the external carotid, through foramen spinosum). Sensation: trigeminal above the tentorium, C2–C3 below. Pulling on dural vessels → referred headache.

4. Dural folds and herniation

  • Falx cerebri: between the cerebral hemispheres (crista galli to internal occipital protuberance).
  • Tentorium cerebelli: between cerebrum and cerebellum. Its tentorial notch lets the brainstem through and divides supra- and infratentorial compartments.
  • Falx cerebelli: partly between the cerebellar hemispheres.
  • Diaphragma sellae: roof over the pituitary, with an opening for the infundibulum.
  • Clinical (notes): space-occupying lesions are mostly infratentorial in children, supratentorial in adults. Raised ICP → uncal herniation through the tentorial notch: decreased consciousness, ipsilateral fixed dilated pupil (CN III), irregular breathing. Then tonsillar herniation into the foramen magnum compresses the medulla → death.

5. Venous sinuses (between the two dura layers)

  • Superior sagittal (with arachnoid granulations draining CSF), inferior sagittal, straight, confluence, transverse, sigmoid → internal jugular vein (via the jugular foramen); cavernous, superior and inferior petrosal.
  • Cavernous sinus: either side of the sella. CN III, IV, V1, V2 in the lateral wall; internal carotid and CN VI run through the middle. It receives the ophthalmic veins, which connect to the facial vein — so infection in the "danger area" of the face can spread in.
  • Cavernous sinus syndrome: orbital or facial pain, orbital swelling and chemosis, fever, oculomotor and trigeminal neuropathy. Most often from cavernous sinus thrombosis; also carotid aneurysm, carotid-cavernous fistula, meningioma, pituitary tumour. (CN VI is often affected first because it's free in the sinus.)
  • Emissary veins have no valves → scalp infection can spread inward.

6. Arterial supply

  • Internal carotids enter via the carotid canal, run through the cavernous sinus, give the ophthalmic artery, and end as the anterior and middle cerebral arteries.
  • Vertebrals (first branch of the subclavian) climb through the transverse foramina, enter via the foramen magnum, and join to form the basilar artery, which ends as the posterior cerebral arteries.
  • Circle of Willis (around the pituitary stalk and optic chiasm): anterior communicating, anterior cerebrals, internal carotids, posterior communicating, posterior cerebrals. The MCA is not part of the circle. It gives collateral flow that compensates for gradual occlusion; sudden occlusion or aneurysm rupture still causes deficits.
Artery Territory

| ACA | Medial frontal and parietal lobes (leg area), corpus callosum | | MCA | Lateral frontal, parietal and temporal lobes (face and arm, language) | | PCA | Occipital lobe and inferior temporal lobe |

  • Stroke: ischemic (thrombotic or embolic) or hemorrhagic. Symptoms are sudden and match the territory.
  • CN III lies between the superior cerebellar and posterior cerebral arteries — an aneurysm there (or of the PCOM) compresses it.
  • Local history: Dr. Charles Drake (London, Ontario) pioneered aneurysm surgery.

8. CSF pathway

Choroid plexus (lateral, third and fourth ventricles) makes CSF → lateral ventricles → interventricular foramina → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → median and lateral apertures → subarachnoid space → arachnoid granulations → superior sagittal sinus.

9. Disruption of CSF flow

⚠ Hydrocephalus categories are not spelled out on this year's slides. From the pathway above: a block inside the ventricles (e.g. aqueductal stenosis, posterior fossa tumour) gives obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus, with dilated ventricles upstream; failure of absorption at the arachnoid granulations (e.g. after meningitis or subarachnoid hemorrhage) gives communicating hydrocephalus. In infants the open fontanelles bulge and the head grows; in adults, signs are raised ICP (headache, vomiting, papilledema, reduced consciousness).
