Framing the approach

STIs present in a limited number of clinical patterns, and recognizing the pattern is more useful than memorizing a long list of organisms in isolation. Think in terms of: urethritis/cervicitis, genital ulcers, vaginal/urethral discharge, and pelvic/systemic spread — then map the common organisms onto each pattern.

Chlamydia and gonorrhea — the urethritis/cervicitis pattern

Both are common causes of urethritis in men and cervicitis in women, and both are frequently asymptomatic — which is exactly why opportunistic screening in sexually active young people matters so much. When symptomatic: dysuria, urethral or vaginal discharge, intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding, and pelvic discomfort. Gonorrhea discharge is classically more purulent; chlamydia discharge tends to be more mucoid, but this distinction isn't reliable enough to guide treatment — testing is required either way.

Diagnosis for both is by nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) on urine or a vaginal/cervical/urethral swab — highly sensitive and now the standard over culture for routine diagnosis.

Treatment: chlamydia is treated with doxycycline (now generally preferred over single-dose azithromycin due to better efficacy at extragenital sites); gonorrhea requires a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone, and because co-infection is common, empiric chlamydia coverage is usually added when gonorrhea is diagnosed unless chlamydia has already been excluded. Partners from the preceding 60 days need testing and treatment, and patients should abstain from sex until 7 days after treatment completion and symptom resolution.

Genital ulcer disease

The two organisms to distinguish are herpes simplex virus (HSV) and syphilis, with chancroid much rarer in Canada.

HSV classically causes multiple, painful, grouped vesicles that ulcerate — often with a prodrome of tingling or burning, and sometimes with systemic symptoms (fever, malaise, tender inguinal lymphadenopathy) on first presentation. Recurrences tend to be milder. Diagnosis is by PCR of a swab from an active lesion; serology has a role in specific scenarios (asymptomatic partner discordance, pregnancy counselling) but isn't a general screening test. Treatment is antiviral — acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir — for both primary episodes and recurrences, with suppressive daily therapy an option for patients with frequent outbreaks.

Syphilis (Treponema pallidum) causes a single, classically painless ulcer (the chancre) with a clean base and indurated edges at the primary stage — a critical distinguishing feature from the painful, multiple lesions of HSV. Left untreated, it progresses through secondary syphilis (diffuse rash including palms and soles, condyloma lata, lymphadenopathy) and, after a latent period, potentially to tertiary disease (cardiovascular and neurologic complications) years later. Diagnosis uses a two-step serologic approach — a non-treponemal test (RPR or VDRL, which also tracks treatment response by titre) confirmed by a treponemal-specific test. Treatment for early syphilis is a single intramuscular dose of benzathine penicillin G; later-stage or neurosyphilis requires extended or IV regimens. Syphilis in pregnancy requires urgent treatment to prevent congenital transmission.

Vaginal and vulvar presentations

Trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, and candidiasis all cause discharge but only trichomoniasis is a true STI (BV and candidiasis are dysbiosis, not sexually transmitted in the classic sense, though this course covers the differential together since they present similarly — see the separate Vaginal Discharge module for the full comparison). Trichomonas vaginalis causes a frothy, malodorous, often yellow-green discharge with vulvovaginal irritation; diagnosis by NAAT or wet mount (motile trichomonads); treatment is oral metronidazole or tinidazole, and — unlike most STIs — partners are treated presumptively without requiring their own testing, since the infection is often asymptomatic in men.

HPV

Human papillomavirus is the most common STI overall. Low-risk subtypes (6, 11) cause genital warts (condyloma acuminata) — painless, verrucous lesions treated with topical therapy, cryotherapy, or excision depending on extent. High-risk subtypes (particularly 16 and 18) drive cervical dysplasia and cancer, which is why cervical cancer screening exists as its own dedicated module — HPV vaccination is the primary prevention strategy, ideally given before sexual debut.

HIV

Presents anywhere from asymptomatic to an acute retroviral syndrome (fever, rash, lymphadenopathy, pharyngitis — mimicking mononucleosis) 2–4 weeks after exposure, through to years of clinical latency before AIDS-defining illness in untreated infection. Diagnosis is with a combined antigen/antibody screening test, confirmed by further testing if positive; a fourth-generation test can detect infection within about 2–4 weeks of exposure. Treatment is with combination antiretroviral therapy, ideally started promptly after diagnosis, which now allows most patients to achieve an undetectable viral load and a normal life expectancy — and undetectable \= untransmittable for sexual partners. Post-exposure prophylaxis (within 72 hours of a high-risk exposure) and pre-exposure prophylaxis for ongoing risk are both key prevention tools worth knowing exist, even outside this module's core scope.

Applying treatment regimens — the general rules

A few principles apply across almost every STI: test for other STIs whenever one is diagnosed (co-infection is common), treat presumptively when clinical suspicion is high and follow-up is uncertain, always address partner notification and treatment, counsel on abstinence until treatment is complete and symptoms resolved, and remember that public health reporting is mandatory for many of these infections in Ontario — which is exactly what the next module in this block covers in detail.

Closing

The clinical skill this module builds is pattern recognition: match the presentation (urethritis/cervicitis, ulcer, discharge, or systemic) to the short list of likely organisms, confirm with the right test, and apply a standard regimen — while remembering that asymptomatic infection is the rule, not the exception, for several of the most consequential STIs.
