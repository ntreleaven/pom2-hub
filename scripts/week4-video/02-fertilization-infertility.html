Fertilization & Infertility — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Source: Dr. Krista Cameron module (fertilization and infertility.pdf), Elentra Learning Objectives

Duration target: ~12-14 min narration

Learning Objectives

Fertility: Explain how fertilization occurs. Describe the optimal time for intercourse to achieve pregnancy. Infertility: Outline an approach to infertility for a family physician. Outline general causes and prevalence. Describe an approach to assessment. Describe populations needing early referral. Outline how pelvic infection or pain causes infertility.

Part 1: Fertilization

Let's start at the cellular level, with sperm transport. A sperm's total lifespan — from the start of spermatogenesis to arriving ready at the epididymis — is about 72 days. At ejaculation, active sperm move rapidly into the uterus while the seminal plasma itself stays behind in the vagina. That's clinically useful to know: patients often worry that fluid leaking out after standing up means the sperm are lost. Reassure them — that's just seminal fluid. The sperm themselves reach the fallopian tube within about five minutes of ejaculation, aided by contractions of the reproductive tract. There's no medical reason to lie down for 30 minutes after intercourse.

Before sperm can fertilize an egg, they undergo capacitation in the fallopian tube — a set of molecular changes that let them bind the zona pellucida, undergo the acrosome reaction, and develop hypermotility.

Meanwhile, the egg is transported by ciliary motion, muscular contractions, and tubular fluid flow, collected by the fimbriated end of the tube after ovulation. Fertilization itself happens in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. The oocyte is only viable for about 12–24 hours after ovulation; sperm can survive up to five days, though their fertilizing capacity is really only good for two to three days.

The fertilization sequence: sperm binds the zona pellucida (about one minute); the acrosome reaction is triggered, allowing penetration; the sperm fuses with the oocyte membrane, forming a zygote; a cortical reaction blocks polyspermy and activates the oocyte; meiosis completes around three hours later, releasing the second polar body; sperm and egg chromosomes combine to restore the diploid number; and DNA synthesis begins roughly 9–10 hours after insemination.

Why does timing of intercourse matter so much? Pregnancy rates are highest with intercourse around cycle days 13–14 in a standard 28-day cycle — but more precisely, the single best day is about two days before ovulation, with the odds dropping quickly after ovulation has already occurred.

Part 2: Infertility — definitions and prevalence

Infertility is defined as failure to conceive after one year of regular, unprotected intercourse — with the caveat that the definition assumes adequate frequency and timing; a couple who can't reliably time intercourse around ovulation doesn't neatly fit the one-year rule. Primary infertility means the woman has never been pregnant; secondary infertility means she has been pregnant before, regardless of the outcome.

Infertility affects 10–15% of reproductive-age couples. By one year of trying, 85% of couples conceive; by two years, 93%. But this is powerfully age-dependent: only about 7% of women aged 20–24 are infertile, rising to 15% by ages 30–34. Miscarriage risk follows the same curve — roughly 10–15% under 35, about one in four between 35–39, and close to 60% after 40, largely due to rising rates of aneuploid embryos.

Who needs an earlier workup? Standard guidance is one year of trying for women under 35, but only six months for women 35 and older. Anyone with a known fertility risk factor — irregular cycles from PCOS, for example — should be referred immediately rather than waiting, since they're unlikely to conceive spontaneously without ovulation.

The standard infertility workup

Walk through it as a sequence:

  1. Bloodwork on cycle days 2–4: FSH, LH, estradiol, prolactin, TSH — checking pituitary-ovarian signaling, ruling out hyperprolactinemia, and assessing thyroid function.
  2. Sonohysterogram, days 6–12: saline is used to distend the cavity and look for polyps or fibroids; foam (or dye) is then used to assess tubal patency, and the ovaries are scanned for follicle counts as a marker of reserve.
  3. Day 21 progesterone: about a week before expected menses, confirming that ovulation actually occurred — since even regular cycles don't guarantee ovulation.
  4. Semen analysis for the male partner — non-negotiable, since male factors account for roughly half of all infertility.
  5. Infectious disease screening for both partners — legally required in Ontario before any assisted reproductive technology.
  6. AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone): can be drawn any time in the cycle; it estimates ovarian reserve and guides IVF medication dosing, but does not predict natural fertility — a low AMH does not mean someone can't conceive naturally.

A key teaching point: always interpret day 2–4 hormones together, not in isolation. An FSH of 30 looks alarming — but if estradiol is already 500 on the same draw, the patient isn't actually at day 2 physiologically; a dominant follicle is already producing estrogen, which explains the "elevated" FSH. Order FSH, LH, and estradiol as a set.

Ways to confirm ovulation beyond day-21 progesterone: urinary LH monitoring (the surge predicts ovulation about 36 hours later — that's the best day for intercourse), serial ultrasound (accurate but impractical for routine use), cervical mucus changes, and basal body temperature (least reliable in practice).

PCOS and endometriosis as infertility drivers

PCOS is the most common ovulatory disorder and a diagnosis of exclusion. Beyond the reproductive symptoms — irregular cycles, infertility — counsel patients on long-term risks: cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and endometrial hyperplasia or cancer from unopposed estrogen exposure. For fertility, first-line treatment is ovulation induction; even a 5–10% weight loss can restart spontaneous ovulation.

Endometriosis affects 30–50% of infertile women by traditional estimates, though improved imaging suggests the true rate may be closer to 80% in some fertility clinic populations. Diagnosis is technically confirmed by laparoscopy with biopsy, but most cases are diagnosed clinically — symptoms plus response to hormonal therapy. It impairs fertility through multiple mechanisms simultaneously: embryotoxic peritoneal fluid, reduced implantation rates, impaired follicle development and diminished ovarian reserve, and tubal dysfunction from chronic pelvic inflammation. For fertility patients, hormonal suppression isn't an option while trying to conceive — early-stage disease may benefit from laparoscopic excision (improving spontaneous pregnancy rates for up to nine months post-op), while advanced disease usually goes straight to IVF, which bypasses the toxic pelvic environment.

Male factor infertility

Infertility is genuinely split 50/50 between female and male factors — 41% isolated female, 24% isolated male, 24% combined, 11% unexplained — which is why both partners always need investigation. The leading causes of male infertility: idiopathic (most common), varicocele, exogenous steroid use (anabolic steroids act as a male contraceptive by suppressing the brain-gonadal axis), erectile/ejaculatory dysfunction, prior genital infections causing obstruction, genetic conditions (Klinefelter syndrome, Y-chromosome microdeletions), radiation/chemotherapy, and lifestyle factors like marijuana, smoking, and hot tub use. An abnormal semen analysis isn't just a fertility finding — it's associated with increased lifetime cancer risk and earlier mortality, so it deserves real counselling, not just a fertility referral.

Recurrent pregnancy loss

Defined as two or more losses (consecutive or not, including biochemical pregnancies) — and any patient with two or more losses warrants a workup. Key causes: antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (5–20% of cases, treatable with heparin and aspirin), uterine anomalies (10–15%), endocrine disorders (thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinemia, poorly controlled diabetes), chronic endometritis (treatable with antibiotics), and balanced reciprocal translocations (about 5% of couples, requiring karyotyping of both partners). Despite a full workup, about half of recurrent pregnancy loss cases have no identifiable cause.

Closing

Fertilization is a tightly timed cellular process, and the best day for conception is about two days before ovulation. Infertility is common — 1 in 10 couples — and the workup is systematic: hormones, anatomy, ovulation confirmation, and semen analysis, always for both partners. PCOS and endometriosis are the two conditions you'll see most often driving female infertility, and each has a distinct mechanism and management pathway. And when a couple has experienced recurrent loss, a structured workup finds an explanation about half the time — which means the other half requires honest, supportive counselling about uncertainty.
