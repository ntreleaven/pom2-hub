Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students should be able to:

  • Predict symptoms that can occur with orbital abnormalities
  • Describe the orbit bone anatomy and eyelid anatomy
  • Describe the globe anatomy and function of its components
  • Describe the innervation and vascular supply of the orbit and its components
  • Describe the extraocular muscles, their innervation and their function

High-Yield Content

Four "main points" slides frame this module: the orbit is thin medially and inferiorly; seven extraocular muscles move the eye along an axis that differs from the orbit's; CN II sees, CN III/IV/VI move, V1 feels; and the ophthalmic veins connect the face to the cavernous sinus.

1. Bony orbit and fractures

  • Seven bones: frontal, sphenoid (greater and lesser wings), zygomatic, maxilla, ethmoid, lacrimal, palatine.
  • The eye is held mostly by orbital fat, with help from the muscles.
  • Medial and inferior walls are very thin.
  • Blowout fracture: indirect trauma (e.g. a ball or elbow to the eye) raises orbital pressure and breaks a weak wall. In an inferior (floor) blowout, orbital fat herniates into the maxillary sinus — the "teardrop sign" on coronal CT.

2. The eyeball

  • Outer: sclera (white) and clear cornea in front.
  • Middle: choroid (vascular), ciliary body, iris.
  • Inner: retina — light-sensitive; sends signals to the brain via the optic nerve.
  • Lens hangs from suspensory ligaments. The ciliary body changes lens shape for accommodation.
  • Anterior and posterior chambers hold aqueous humour, which drains via Schlemm's canal. Behind the lens is the vitreous body.

Iris muscles:

Muscle Arrangement Nerve Action

| Dilator pupillae | Radial | Sympathetic (superior cervical ganglion) | Dilates in dim light | | Sphincter pupillae | Circular | Parasympathetic via CN III | Constricts in bright light |

Conditions:

  • Pink eye — conjunctival infection
  • Cataract — loss of lens transparency
  • Glaucoma — reduced aqueous drainage → high intraocular pressure → compressed retina → blindness

3. Eyelid, conjunctiva and lacrimal apparatus

  • Conjunctiva: transparent mucous membrane, palpebral (lines the lid) and bulbar (covers the eye); vessels show as "bloodshot eyes". Secretes mucus.
  • Tarsal plate stiffens the lid; tarsal glands make an oily layer that keeps tears on the cornea.
  • Lacrimal gland (upper lateral orbit) makes tears; blinking spreads them across the cornea. Drainage: puncta → canaliculi → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → nasal cavity.
  • Lacrimal gland control: parasympathetic from CN VII, carried by V1 branches (lacrimal nerve).
  • Stye = infected or blocked tarsal gland. Corneal ulcer = corneal irritation, can come from too little tear fluid.

4. Extraocular muscles

Seven muscles: levator palpebrae superioris (lifts the lid), four recti (superior, inferior, medial, lateral) and two obliques (superior, inferior).

Movements: elevation (up), depression (down), adduction (toward the nose), abduction (away), plus internal and external rotation.

The tricky part: the orbit's axis points laterally (~23°), but the eye looks straight ahead. So the superior and inferior recti pull slightly medially, and the obliques pull from behind and medially.

Clinical testing ("H" pattern): test each muscle in the position where it acts alone — e.g. look down and in to test the superior oblique.

5. Nerves of the orbit

  • Sight: optic nerve (CN II) in a dural sheath, through the optic canal.
  • Motor: III, IV, VI through the superior orbital fissure. (LR6 SO4, the rest III.)
  • Sensory: ophthalmic division of trigeminal (V1) — frontal, lacrimal and nasociliary branches.

Nerve palsies — the key "predict the symptom" slides:

Palsy Eye position Other signs

| CN VI (abducens) | Can't abduct; eye turns in | Injured by raised intracranial pressure (long course); head turns to avoid diplopia | | CN III (oculomotor) | "Down and out" | Ptosis; may have a dilated pupil | | CN IV (trochlear) | "Up and in" (eye drifts up) | Head tilt away from the lesion; commonly from trauma |

Horner's syndrome — interruption of the sympathetic supply (e.g. superior cervical ganglion):

  • Miosis (unopposed parasympathetic)
  • Mild ptosis (loss of the sympathetic part of the levator — superior tarsal muscle)
  • Red skin (vasodilation) and anhidrosis (no sweating) on that side

6. Vessels of the orbit

  • Ophthalmic artery (first branch of the internal carotid) supplies the eye and orbit. Its central retinal artery runs in the optic nerve to the retina — visible at the optic disc on fundoscopy, with the fovea and macula temporal to it.
  • Occlusion of the central retinal artery → blindness in that eye.
  • Superior and inferior ophthalmic veins drain to the cavernous sinus, and connect with the facial vein and pterygoid plexus.
  • So bacteria from the face can reach the cavernous sinus, and cavernous sinus infection can affect the orbit's motor and sensory nerves (III, IV, V1, V2, VI, plus the internal carotid).

7. Predicting symptoms from orbital abnormalities

  • Floor fracture → fat (and sometimes inferior rectus) trapped in the maxillary sinus → trouble looking up, diplopia, numb cheek (infraorbital nerve, V2).
  • Ptosis + down-and-out eye + big pupil → CN III (think PCOM aneurysm if the pupil is involved).
  • Ptosis + small pupil → Horner's.
  • Painful red eye with high pressure → glaucoma; cloudy lens → cataract.
  • Sudden painless monocular blindness → central retinal artery occlusion.
  • Facial infection then fever, swollen eye and ophthalmoplegia → cavernous sinus thrombosis.
