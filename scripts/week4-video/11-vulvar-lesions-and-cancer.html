Vulvar Lesions and Vulvar Cancer — Video Script

Week 4 | Reproduction Block | Teacher: Laura Sovran | Duration on Elentra: 30 min

Duration target: ~9 min narration

Learning Objectives

  • Describe different lesions of the vulva
  • List the various differential diagnoses for the various lesions
  • Compare and contrast vulvar conditions with other skin lesions
  • Describe the presentation of vulvar malignancy
  • List benign masses of the vulva with clinical presentations

Approaching vulvar lesions like skin, because that's what the vulva is

The vulva is skin — covered by squamous epithelium — which means the same dermatologic framework you'd use anywhere else on the body applies: describe morphology first (macule, papule, plaque, ulcer, vesicle), then ask about color, distribution, symmetry, and associated symptoms (itch versus pain versus asymptomatic), before generating a differential. The difference is context — vulvar skin is under different hormonal influence, chronic moisture, and mechanical friction, which shifts the differential toward a specific set of conditions you see far less often elsewhere on the body.

Benign vulvar masses

Bartholin gland cyst/abscess: the most common vulvar mass in reproductive-age women — a unilateral, fluctuant swelling at the posterior introitus (4 or 8 o'clock position) from a blocked Bartholin duct. A simple cyst is often asymptomatic; an abscess is acutely painful, erythematous, and may need incision and drainage or Word catheter placement. Recurrent Bartholin cysts in a postmenopausal patient warrant biopsy to exclude Bartholin gland carcinoma, which is rare but disproportionately affects that age group.

Epidermal inclusion cysts: small, mobile, non-tender subcutaneous nodules, often related to prior trauma or episiotomy scarring — benign and only treated if symptomatic.

Vulvar varicosities: more common in pregnancy due to increased pelvic venous pressure, usually resolving postpartum.

Hidradenitis suppurativa: a chronic inflammatory condition of apocrine gland-bearing skin causing recurrent, painful nodules and abscesses, often with sinus tract formation and scarring over time — a chronic disease requiring dermatologic co-management, not a simple infection.

Skin conditions with characteristic vulvar patterns

Lichen sclerosus: thin, white, "cigarette-paper" or porcelain-white plaques, classically in a figure-of-eight distribution around the vulva and perianal skin, causing intense pruritus and, over time, architectural change — labial fusion, clitoral hood scarring, and introital narrowing. It carries a small but real increased risk of vulvar squamous cell carcinoma, so biopsy is warranted for any area that doesn't respond typically to treatment or that develops a new nodule or ulcer. First-line treatment is high-potency topical corticosteroids.

Lichen planus: violaceous, flat-topped papules, which on the vulva often presents as erosive disease — painful erosions, particularly at the vaginal introitus, sometimes with vaginal involvement causing scarring and adhesions. Distinguishing it from lichen sclerosus matters because management (topical or systemic immunosuppression) and monitoring differ.

Contact dermatitis / lichen simplex chronicus: erythema, edema, and eventually thickened, lichenified skin from chronic scratching in response to an irritant (soaps, panty liners, incontinence) — resolves with irritant avoidance and, in the chronic thickened phase, breaking the itch-scratch cycle with topical steroids.

Psoriasis: on the vulva often lacks the classic silvery scale seen elsewhere (moisture keeps it macerated) and presents instead as well-demarcated, smooth erythematous plaques — worth considering in a patient with known psoriasis elsewhere on the body.

Vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) — the precancerous step

Analogous to CIN on the cervix, VIN represents dysplastic squamous change. Two distinct pathways exist: HPV-associated VIN (usual type), linked to high-risk HPV like cervical dysplasia and often multifocal, typically in younger patients; and differentiated VIN, HPV-independent, usually arising in a background of lichen sclerosus in older patients, with a higher and faster risk of progression to invasive cancer despite looking less dramatic clinically. Any persistent, unusual, or non-healing vulvar lesion — particularly in a patient with known lichen sclerosus — needs a low threshold for biopsy.

Vulvar malignancy

Vulvar cancer is uncommon overall but presents in two settings you should recognize: an older patient, often with a background of lichen sclerosus or differentiated VIN, presenting with a persistent lump, ulcer, or area of thickened/discolored skin, frequently associated with longstanding pruritus that has recently changed character (increasing pain, bleeding, or a new mass within a previously itchy area); or a younger patient with HPV-associated disease, often with a history of cervical or other lower genital tract dysplasia. Squamous cell carcinoma accounts for the large majority of cases; melanoma, Paget's disease, and Bartholin gland carcinoma are rarer but important to keep on the list, particularly for pigmented or unusual lesions. The single most important clinical principle: any vulvar lesion that doesn't resolve with appropriate treatment, that changes in appearance, or that occurs in a patient with known lichen sclerosus/VIN, needs a biopsy rather than continued empiric treatment.

Bringing it together

Approach the vulva the way you'd approach skin anywhere else — morphology, distribution, and symptom pattern first — while keeping in mind the specific conditions that concentrate here: Bartholin cysts as the classic benign mass, lichen sclerosus and lichen planus as the dermatoses with malignant potential worth monitoring closely, and a low threshold to biopsy anything persistent, atypical, or arising against a background of chronic vulvar dermatosis, since that's precisely the setting in which vulvar cancer tends to be missed or diagnosed late.
