Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the upper extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of arm pathology.
    • Determine the sensory and motor loss anticipated with injuries to specific sites of the plexus (the roots and the terminal branches).
    • Identify the major arteries and veins of the upper extremity and their relationships to surrounding structures.
  2. Create a differential diagnosis and appropriate management plan for patients presenting with upper extremity complaints (numbness, weakness, pain, limitations of range of motion).

This is an anatomy module. Its clinical payoff is which nerve or vessel a fracture at each level of the humerus puts at risk, and where you take a blood pressure and draw blood.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: in trauma, the first question after "is it broken?" is "is the limb still neurovascularly intact?" Answering it means knowing which artery and nerve lie against which part of the humerus. This module traces the main artery from the neck to the elbow and the nerves that travel with it.

Objective 1: the arterial pathway

The main artery changes name as it crosses landmarks: subclavian → axillary → brachial → radial + ulnar.

Subclavian artery. It comes off the brachiocephalic trunk on the right and straight off the aortic arch on the left. Two branches matter for the limb because they feed the anastomosis around the scapula:

  • Dorsal scapular artery: runs back to the upper superficial back muscles.
  • Suprascapular artery (often from the thyrocervical trunk): runs with the suprascapular nerve. The artery passes above the superior transverse scapular ligament to the back of the shoulder.

Axillary artery: from the lateral border of the 1st rib to the inferior border of teres major. Pectoralis minor crosses in front of it and divides it into three parts:

  • 1st part (rib 1 to the top of pectoralis minor): the superior thoracic artery, to the upper chest wall.
  • 2nd part (behind pectoralis minor): the thoracoacromial artery, with acromial, clavicular, deltoid and pectoral branches, and the lateral thoracic artery, which runs with the long thoracic nerve.
  • 3rd part (pectoralis minor to teres major):
    • The subscapular artery splits into the thoracodorsal (with the thoracodorsal nerve) and the circumflex scapular (around the lateral border of the scapula).
    • The anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries encircle the surgical neck. The posterior one travels with the axillary nerve.

Scapular anastomosis: the circumflex scapular, suprascapular, dorsal scapular, posterior circumflex humeral and thoracoacromial branches all connect. That is why the limb can survive an axillary artery blockage.

Brachial artery: starts at the lower border of teres major. It runs medial to biceps, in front of coracobrachialis and brachialis, and ends in the cubital fossa as the radial (lateral) and ulnar (medial) arteries. It has three branches in the arm:

  • Profunda brachii (deep brachial) artery: goes to the posterior compartment, running in the radial groove with the radial nerve. Near the elbow it becomes the radial collateral artery.
  • Superior and inferior ulnar collateral arteries: join the network around the elbow.

Clinical notes:

  • Blood pressure: the cuff compresses the brachial artery, and you listen over it in the cubital fossa.
  • The brachial artery hugs the medial humeral shaft, so it is at risk in mid-shaft and supracondylar fractures. The profunda brachii is stuck to the bone in the radial groove, so it is at risk in mid-shaft fractures.

Veins

Deep veins are paired and travel with the major arteries. The superficial veins:

  • Cephalic: ascends the anterolateral forearm and arm, through the deltopectoral triangle, into the end of the axillary vein.
  • Basilic: ascends the medial forearm and lower arm, then joins the brachial veins to form the axillary vein.
  • Median cubital: crosses the cubital fossa obliquely, joining cephalic to basilic. This is the usual vein for blood draws and IV access.

Objective 1: nerves of the arm and their relationships

Pectoral girdle nerves travel with arteries: dorsal scapular nerve + artery, long thoracic nerve + lateral thoracic artery, thoracodorsal nerve + artery, suprascapular nerve + artery, and axillary nerve + posterior circumflex humeral artery.

The terminal branches in the arm:

  • Musculocutaneous (C5, 6, 7): pierces coracobrachialis and runs between biceps and brachialis. It supplies all of the anterior compartment, then becomes the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm. The biceps (bicipital myotatic) reflex tests C5 and C6.
  • Median (C5–T1): lateral to the brachial artery, crossing to its medial side mid-arm, then deep to the bicipital aponeurosis in the cubital fossa. It supplies nothing in the arm.
  • Ulnar (C7, 8, T1 in these notes): medial in the anterior compartment, then it pierces the medial intermuscular septum at mid-shaft into the posterior compartment. It supplies nothing in the arm.
  • Medial cutaneous nerves of the arm and forearm: both come from the medial cord and run beside the ulnar nerve. They are smaller and branch higher up, which is how you tell them apart in the lab.
  • Radial (C5–T1): enters behind the brachial artery, then runs in the radial groove with the profunda brachii. It supplies triceps, then pierces the lateral septum to reach the forearm between brachialis and brachioradialis.

Objective 1 → 2: predicting loss from a fracture

Injury Structure at risk What you'd find
Surgical neck fracture (low-energy fall, elderly, osteoporosis) Axillary nerve (+ circumflex humerals) weak deltoid abduction, numb skin over the deltoid
Mid-shaft fracture (direct blow) Radial nerve + profunda brachii (radial groove) ± brachial artery wrist drop, numb back of the hand and forearm
Supracondylar / distal humerus Brachial artery + median nerve pulse deficit, weak forearm flexors
Medial epicondyle Ulnar nerve weak intrinsic hand muscles, numb ring and little fingers

Cutaneous supply and dermatomes

  • Over the deltoid: axillary nerve (posterior cord)
  • Medial arm: medial cutaneous nerve of the arm (medial cord, C8–T2)
  • Medial forearm: medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (medial cord, C8, T1)
  • Lateral forearm: lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm (musculocutaneous)
  • Posterior arm and forearm: posterior cutaneous nerves (radial)

The dermatome test areas are covered in the distal neurovascular script (C5 lateral elbow, C6 thumb, C7 middle finger, C8 little finger, T1 medial elbow).

Bottom line: subclavian → axillary (rib 1 to teres major, three parts around pectoralis minor) → brachial → radial and ulnar in the cubital fossa. Take the BP over the brachial artery and draw blood from the median cubital vein. Fracture-nerve pairs: surgical neck = axillary, mid-shaft = radial + profunda brachii, supracondylar = brachial artery + median, medial epicondyle = ulnar.
