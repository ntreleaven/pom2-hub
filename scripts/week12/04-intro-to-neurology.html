High-Yield Content

Neurology is a logic puzzle: describe the problem → localize the lesion → build a differential from the time course → plan investigations and treatment. About 10% of family-medicine visits are for a neurologic symptom.

2. Neuro glossary

Term Meaning

| Plegia | Paralysis — essentially no voluntary movement | | Paresis | Weakness | | Dysarthria | Trouble with articulation | | Aphasia | Trouble producing or understanding language | | Dysphagia | Trouble swallowing | | Ataxia | Trouble with motor coordination | | Dysmetria | Trouble reaching a specific target | | Obtunded | Reduced awareness; can't be fully roused | | Dominant hemisphere | Where language lives — left in almost all right-handers; still mostly left in left-handers (~27% right) | | MCA stroke | Face and arm > leg contralateral weakness (because of the homunculus) |

Also know the planes: sagittal, axial, coronal.

3. Organization of the nervous system

Microscopic: a neuron has a cell body (nucleus), dendrites and an axon. Action potentials travel down the axon to the synapse. Myelin insulates the axon.

Macroscopic:

  • CNS = brain and spinal cord.
  • PNS = cranial nerves, roots, plexus, nerves, neuromuscular junction, muscle, sensory receptors.
  • Grey matter = cell bodies (cortex, folded into gyri; basal ganglia and thalamus; brainstem nuclei). White matter = myelinated axon tracts.

Lobes:

  • Frontal: motor, speech production, executive function
  • Parietal: sensation, attention in time and space
  • Occipital: vision
  • Temporal: auditory processing, language understanding, memory

Deep structures:

  • Basal ganglia: integrate cortical activity into one movement; speed of movement
  • Thalamus: relay centre; most sensory input passes through it
  • Limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate): emotion and memory
  • Cerebellum: coordination — limbs, gait, speech, swallowing
  • Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla): all cranial nerve nuclei plus the sensory and motor tracts

Spinal cord: cervical C1–C8, thoracic T1–T12, lumbosacral. The cord ends at about L1; below that is the cauda equina. So lesions at or above L1 give a UMN pattern; below L1, an LMN pattern. White matter holds ascending sensory and descending motor tracts; grey matter holds cell bodies.

4. Pathways

Motor: motor cortex (contralateral to the movement) → white matter → brainstem → decussates in the medulla → spinal cord → anterior horn cell → root → nerve → NMJ → muscle.

UMN vs LMN — know this table:

Upper motor neuron (brain, cord) Lower motor neuron (AHC, root, plexus, nerve, NMJ, muscle)

| Spasticity | Atrophy | | Hyperreflexia | Reflexes reduced or absent | | Babinski (extensor plantar) | Plantar flexor or absent | | No significant atrophy | Normal tone (or low) | | No fasciculations | Fasciculations may be present |

  • Cerebellum and basal ganglia feed back to the motor cortex. Lesions there do not cause weakness.
    • Cerebellum → incoordination
    • Basal ganglia → parkinsonism (slow, hard to start, rigid) or excess movement (chorea, dystonia, athetosis, hemiballismus)

Sensory: nerve → root → cord → brainstem → thalamus → white matter → primary sensory cortex. (Nice to know: the dorsal columns carry vibration and proprioception and cross in the medulla; the spinothalamic tract carries pain and temperature and crosses at entry to the cord.)

Visual: the image is inverted on the retina → optic nerves → optic chiasm, where each hemifield goes to the opposite optic tract → eventually the occipital pole.

5. Localizing the lesion

  1. CNS or PNS? UMN signs → CNS. LMN signs → PNS.
  2. Refine: cortical signs (language, neglect)? Sensation affected or spared? Painful or painless? Distribution? Bladder?
  3. Why bother? To know what to image. Don't order a CT head for a spinal cord problem.

Patterns of weakness:

Pattern Likely site

| Hemibody with face or cranial nerves | Brain | | Hemibody, no face or CN | Spinal cord | | Quadriparesis | Cervical cord | | Paraparesis | Thoracic cord or lumbar roots | | One myotome | Root | | Eyes and swallowing | NMJ | | Generalized proximal weakness | Muscle |

Language: dominant hemisphere. Expressive = inferior frontal lobe; receptive = posterior temporal lobe.

6. Differential by time course

Onset Think

| Hyperacute (seconds–minutes) | Stroke, migraine aura (resolves in minutes), trauma | | Acute (hours–days) | Inflammation, infection | | Subacute (days–weeks) | Inflammation, infection, tumour, toxic | | Chronic (months–years) | Degenerative, hereditary |

Full checklist: VITAMINS C D — vascular, infectious, trauma, autoimmune, metabolic/medication, inflammatory/iatrogenic, neoplastic, seizure/psychogenic, congenital, degenerative.

DDx of acute aphasia: ischemic stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, migraine, post-ictal Todd's paresis, metabolic upset (e.g. hypoglycemia) on an old lesion, psychogenic.

7. Practice cases

  • Sudden flashes in the right visual field of both eyes in a 44-year-old → left occipital cortex / visual pathway behind the chiasm (a right homonymous field problem). Hyperacute: migraine aura vs stroke.
  • 75-year-old with 2 months of worsening word-finding, wrong words, then nonsense; slight right facial droop → left (dominant) hemisphere language areas. Subacute progression: tumour high on the list.
  • 68-year-old with 3 weeks of falls, clumsy hands, urinary urgency; brisk reflexes in arms and legs, upgoing toes, poor vibration at the toes → cervical spinal cord (UMN in all four limbs plus dorsal columns and bladder). Subacute: compression, inflammation, B12.
  • 59-year-old with 6 months of slowness and stiffness; reduced blinking, soft speech, rigidity, slow tapping, reduced arm swing, normal power and reflexes → basal ganglia. Chronic: degenerative — Parkinson's disease.
