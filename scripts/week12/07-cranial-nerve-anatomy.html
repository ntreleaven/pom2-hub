Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Describe cranial nerve anatomy and function.
  2. Predict symptoms of cranial nerve pathology.

High-Yield Content

The cranial nerves are 12 pairs of nerve bundles that leave the cranial cavity, numbered rostral to caudal. Some are sensory, some motor, some both. Mnemonic for type: "Some Say Marry Money But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More" (S = sensory, M = motor, B = both).

Trigeminal sensory map:

  • V1 ophthalmic: forehead, scalp, upper eyelid, nose, cornea, upper nasal mucosa and sinuses. Also carries sympathetics to the pupil dilator.
  • V2 maxillary: lower eyelid to upper lip, maxillary teeth, maxillary sinus, palate.
  • V3 mandibular: lower lip and chin, cheek, part of ear and temple, mandibular teeth, TMJ, anterior 2/3 of tongue (touch and temperature). Motor to the muscles of mastication, mylohyoid, anterior digastric, tensor veli palatini, tensor tympani.

Eye movement summary: LR6 SO4, the rest III. Lateral rectus looks out; superior oblique looks down and in.

Taste: anterior 2/3 = VII (chorda tympani, riding with the lingual nerve of V3); posterior 1/3 = IX; root of tongue = X.

2. Central vs peripheral cranial nerve problems

The voluntary pathway: cortex → subcortical white matter → brainstem → CN nucleus → cranial nerve. Everything up to the nucleus is CNS (UMN); the nucleus and nerve are PNS (LMN). Sorting this out tells you where to look.

3. Symptoms of cranial nerve pathology

CN I (rarely tested):

  • Symptom: reduced smell.
  • Causes: Kallmann syndrome (congenital), trauma, inferior frontal tumour, post-viral (e.g. COVID-19).
  • Use a familiar smell (cloves, coffee); avoid noxious smells like ammonia, which stimulate trigeminal fibres instead.

CN II:

  • Symptom: reduced vision or trouble focusing.
  • Causes: optic neuritis, ischemic optic neuropathy, compression by tumour.
  • Tests: acuity, fields, fundoscopy (papilledema), pupils, RAPD by the swinging flashlight test.

CN III, IV, VI:

  • Symptom: diplopia from misaligned eyes.
  • Nerve causes: diabetic third nerve palsy, orbital inflammation, cavernous sinus infection, third nerve compression by a PCOM aneurysm, trauma → fourth nerve palsy.
  • Brainstem causes: midbrain or pontine stroke.
  • Key rule: diplopia means brainstem, cranial nerve, eye muscle or NMJ — never a lesion rostral to the brainstem.
  • Tests: eye movements in an "H", ask about diplopia, check the pupil, look for ptosis.

CN V:

  • Symptom: reduced sensation over the face or one division; may have neuropathic pain.
  • Peripheral causes: compression or inflammation (cavernous sinus, schwannoma); trigeminal neuralgia (a vessel pulsing against the nerve).
  • Central causes: brainstem stroke or MS; thalamic or cortical lesions.
  • Tests: touch or pinprick in V1–V3, corneal reflex, jaw strength. Weak mastication only happens with peripheral lesions.

CN VII — know UMN vs LMN facial weakness:

UMN (stroke, brainstem; above the VII nucleus) LMN (VII nucleus or nerve)

| Forehead spared | Forehead involved (lost wrinkles) | | Can close the eye (weakly) | Can't close the eye — globe rolls up (Bell's phenomenon) | | Causes: cortical/subcortical stroke, brainstem lesion | Causes: Bell's palsy (idiopathic), parotid tumour |

The forehead is spared in UMN lesions because the upper face gets input from both hemispheres. Test the 5 branches and taste on the anterior 2/3.

CN VIII:

  • Symptoms: hearing loss and/or vertigo.
  • Causes: end-organ disease (middle ear, semicircular canals), vestibular neuritis, brainstem stroke.
  • Tests: hearing, nystagmus, Romberg.

CN IX and X:

  • Symptoms: dysarthria, hoarseness, dysphagia, autonomic dysfunction.
  • LMN: nucleus, nerve, brainstem or skull base. UMN: cortical control of speech or swallowing.
  • Tests: voice, palate elevation, gag reflex.

CN XI: rarely important. Trapezius → shrug. SCM turns the head away from its own side.

CN XII:

  • Symptoms: lisp, trouble moving food in the mouth.
  • LMN causes: skull base tumour, ALS (look for fasciculations and atrophy). UMN causes: stroke, brain tumour.
  • The tongue deviates toward the weak side on protrusion.

4. Branch details from the schematics (nice to know)

  • Vagus: superior laryngeal → external (cricothyroid) and internal (sensation above the vocal cords); recurrent laryngeal → other laryngeal muscles and sensation below the cords.
  • Hypoglossal: all tongue muscles except palatoglossus (vagus).
  • Facial nerve parasympathetics: greater petrosal → pterygopalatine ganglion → lacrimal gland; chorda tympani → submandibular ganglion → submandibular and sublingual glands.
  • Glossopharyngeal parasympathetics: lesser petrosal → otic ganglion → parotid.
