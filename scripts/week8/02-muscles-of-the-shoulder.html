Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the upper extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of arm pathology.
    • Identify the major muscles of the upper extremity (shoulder girdle, brachium, forearm muscles from the medial and lateral epicondyles, hand muscles).
  2. Create a differential diagnosis and appropriate management plan for patients presenting with upper extremity complaints (numbness, weakness, pain, limitations of range of motion).
    • Describe the anatomic basis of common shoulder pathologies including AC joint separation, rotator cuff disease, biceps tendinopathy, arthritis, instability and adhesive capsulitis.
    • Outline an approach to diagnosis and management (non-pharmacologic, pharmacologic and surgical) for those shoulder conditions.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: shoulder pain is one of the most common MSK complaints, and the commonest cause, rotator cuff disease, is mostly a slow intrinsic degeneration of tendon, not an injury. If you know which muscle does what and which nerve supplies it, you can examine a shoulder and predict which tendon is failing.

Objective 1: muscles of the shoulder girdle

Start with a review of the superficial back from Week 7: trapezius, latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, levator scapulae.

Pectoral region:

  • Pectoralis major: medial clavicle, sternum, upper 6 ribs and costal cartilages → lateral lip of the intertubercular groove. Adducts, flexes and medially rotates the humerus. Nerves: medial and lateral pectoral.
  • Pectoralis minor: ribs 3–5 → coracoid process. Stabilizes the scapula by pulling it down and forward against the chest wall. Nerves: medial and lateral pectoral.
  • Serratus anterior: ribs 1–8 → medial border of the scapula. Protracts the scapula. Nerve: long thoracic (C5, 6, 7). A long thoracic lesion gives a winged scapula: when the patient pushes against a wall, the medial border lifts off the chest wall.

Shoulder muscles:

  • Deltoid: lateral third of the clavicle, acromion, spine of the scapula → deltoid tuberosity. Anterior fibres flex and medially rotate, middle fibres abduct, posterior fibres extend and laterally rotate. Nerve: axillary (C5, C6).
  • Teres major: inferior angle of the scapula → medial lip of the intertubercular groove. Adducts and medially rotates the arm. Nerve: lower subscapular (C5, 6). It is not part of the rotator cuff. (One row of the notes table says "abducts", but the slide says adducts.)

The rotator cuff (SITS): the majority of the cuff is myotome C5–C6.

Muscle Origin → insertion Nerve Action
Supraspinatus supraspinous fossa → greater tubercle suprascapular (C5, 6) first 15° of abduction
Infraspinatus infraspinous fossa → greater tubercle suprascapular (C5, 6) lateral rotation
Teres minor lateral border of scapula → greater tubercle axillary (C5, 6) lateral rotation
Subscapularis subscapular fossa → lesser tubercle upper + lower subscapular (C5, 6, 7) medial rotation

All four stabilize the glenohumeral joint, holding the humeral head in a shallow socket. That is the cuff's first job, and arm motion is its second. Supraspinatus runs over the humeral head beneath the subacromial (subdeltoid) bursa and the acromion, which is why it's the tendon that wears out first.

Objective 2: rotator cuff disease (Dr. Faber)

What it is: the most common shoulder disorder. Prevalence rises with age, onset is in the 40s, and it is considered progressive.

Pathophysiology: intrinsic, extrinsic, or a combination.

  • Intrinsic (the main driver): reduced vascularity of the tendon, tension overload, and tendon degeneration.
  • Extrinsic: acromion morphology reducing the subacromial space volume.

Symptoms: restricted motion, pain, weakness.

Examination: the key sign is "active motion << passive motion." The patient can't lift the arm themselves because of pain or weakness, but you can move it through the range. Impingement signs: Neer, Hawkins, painful arc. Strength tests isolate each tendon: supraspinatus (empty can), infraspinatus (resisted external rotation), subscapularis (lift-off / belly press).

Classification:

  • Stage I: inflammation (impingement), tendon intact.
  • Stage II: partial-thickness defect.
  • Stage III: full-thickness defect.
  • Rotator cuff arthropathy: a massive defect, so the humerus migrates superiorly, abuts the acromion, and arthritis results.

Treatment: most patients are managed non-surgically:

  • Education and activity modification
  • Pain management: NSAIDs
  • Physiotherapy, which means muscle reconditioning
  • Injections
  • Surgery, but when? For pain that persists, weakness, or after an acute injury (an acute traumatic tear). Dr. Faber's line: good surgeons know how to operate, better surgeons know when, the best know when not to.

By stage: I → physiotherapy ± subacromial decompression · II → consider rotator cuff repair · III → rotator cuff repair · arthropathy → shoulder replacement.

Summary: very common in middle age, usually intrinsic tendon change, treated non-surgically in the majority, and surgery depends on severity.

⚠ Objective 2: the other shoulder conditions (not on the PDF slides)

The organizing question for a painful shoulder is: is passive motion lost too? In cuff disease, passive motion is preserved. In the stiff shoulders (degenerative arthritis, adhesive capsulitis), passive motion is lost as well.

  • AC joint separation: a fall or blow to the point of the shoulder. Graded by displacement: types 1–3 (sprain, subluxation, dislocation) are treated conservatively with a sling, ice, NSAIDs and exercise. Types 4–6 (displaced posteriorly, superiorly or inferiorly) need surgical fixation. AC arthritis is a gradual, localized ache when reaching across the chest, with a tender bump and normal range.
  • Biceps tendinopathy: deep anterior shoulder pain with tenderness in the intertubercular groove (see Muscles of the Arm).
  • Glenohumeral arthritis: degenerative OA loses passive motion too (osteophytes block it) but keeps cuff strength, with asymmetric joint-space loss on X-ray. Inflammatory arthritis has a weak cuff and symmetric joint-space loss with erosions and osteopenia.
  • Instability: dislocation is usually anterior-inferior, which puts the axillary nerve at risk, so check deltoid power and the skin over the deltoid before and after reduction. Traumatic instability is unidirectional ("torn loose") and often needs a labral repair. Atraumatic instability is multidirectional ("born loose") and gets physiotherapy first.
  • Adhesive capsulitis ("frozen shoulder"): spontaneous global loss of active AND passive motion with a normal X-ray. Strongly associated with diabetes. It is a diagnosis of exclusion. Stages: freezing → frozen → thawing. Managed with education, analgesia, physiotherapy, and an intra-articular steroid early on.

Bottom line: know SITS (insertions, nerves, which one rotates medially) and that teres major is not in the cuff. Active < passive motion means cuff. Lost passive motion means arthritis or a frozen shoulder. Most cuff disease is treated with physiotherapy, and surgery is staged to severity.
