High-Yield Content

Why this matters: a fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH) is one of the most common injuries you'll see. It produces two fractures you must not mishandle: the distal radius, judged on X-ray by three questions, and the scaphoid, which can hide on the first X-ray and lose its blood supply if missed. The soft-tissue anatomy of the hand sets up carpal tunnel and hand nerve injuries.

Objective 1: joints of the hand

  • CMC joints: digits 2–5 are plane joints. The thumb CMC is a saddle joint, which allows opposition. Supported by the capsule and extensive ligaments.
  • MCP joints: condyloid. Supported by the capsule and medial and lateral collateral ligaments.
  • IP joints: hinge. Same support.

Objective 2: distal radius fractures (Dr. Faber)

What and why: a very common injury, usually from a FOOSH, with localized pain. If the anatomy isn't restored, function is poor.

Reading the X-ray: three parameters.

  1. Is it crooked (bent)? Look at the articular angle on the lateral view. Normal is about 12° of palmar (volar) tilt. A fracture often tips it into dorsal tilt (e.g. 20° dorsiflexed). If left crooked: the distal radioulnar joint motion is altered and forearm rotation becomes restricted. CT shows the DRUJ asymmetry.
  2. Is it short? Look at ulnar variance. Greater than neutral variance means the radius has collapsed. Compare with the opposite side, because some people are naturally different. If left short: ulnar impaction syndrome, where the ulna bumps into the carpus and it hurts.
  3. Is it smooth? Look at the joint surface. A step of more than 1 mm is associated with post-traumatic arthrosis. If left uneven: radiocarpal arthritis with pain and stiffness, treated by fusion.

Treatment principles:

  1. Age-dependent: treatment is forgiving at the extremes of age. Children remodel, and older patients load the wrist less. Active middle-aged patients need the most exact restoration.
  2. Closed reduction
  3. Surgical reduction if closed reduction isn't adequate
  4. Rehabilitate

Closed reduction, step by step:

  • Adequate analgesia: hematoma block, regional anaesthesia, or conscious sedation.
  • Traction to relax the muscles and disimpact.
  • Reduction: increase the deformity first to unlock the fragments, then correct it.
  • Padding: cotton (cheap, used under plaster or fiberglass) or synthetic (waterproof).
  • Casting: plaster of Paris is cheaper, easily moulded, heavy and not water-resistant. Fiberglass is light and strong, can be waterproof, is more radiolucent, but is expensive and harder to mould.
  • A three-point mould holds the reduction. "Well moulded doesn't mean TIGHT!"
  • Repeat X-ray. If the joint surface still isn't smooth, go to ORIF or external fixation.

Summary: common · treatment is set by age and activity level and fracture displacement · closed reduction and immobilization, surgical reduction if inadequate, then rehabilitation.

Objective 2: scaphoid fractures (Dr. Faber)

  • The most common carpal fracture, in young people. It usually heals uneventfully but is sometimes difficult to diagnose, and delayed diagnosis is associated with complications.
  • Unique, retrograde blood supply: blood enters distally and flows back toward the proximal pole. A fracture can cut off the proximal fragment, leading to non-union and osteonecrosis.
  • Clinical exam: swelling and localized tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox (floor: scaphoid + trapezium).
  • Imaging: X-ray is usually sufficient, but be SUSPICIOUS. If the X-rays are normal, treat it as fractured and get additional imaging (CT, MRI).
  • Treatment:
    • Undisplaced or distal-pole fractures: cast until union. A healed waist fracture was shown on CT at 8 weeks in a cast.
    • Displaced or proximal-pole fractures: surgery (screw fixation, volar approach).
  • Complications: non-union, osteonecrosis, late arthrosis.

Objective 1: soft tissues of the hand

Fascia:

  • Palmar aponeurosis: where palmaris longus inserts. Thick longitudinal fibres run from the retinaculum to the medial four digits and blend with their fibrous sheaths. Its septa divide the palm into compartments. Dupuytren's contracture: progressive thickening and fibrosis of the aponeurosis draws the fingers into permanent flexion and needs surgical excision.
  • Palmar carpal ligament: a thickening of the antebrachial fascia, continuous with the extensor retinaculum. It is superficial and proximal to the flexor retinaculum.
  • Flexor retinaculum: deep to the palmar carpal ligament. It forms the roof of the carpal tunnel and runs from the scaphoid and trapezium to the pisiform and hook of hamate.

The carpal tunnel (floor = carpal bones, roof = flexor retinaculum):

  • Inside: 4 FDS tendons + 4 FDP tendons + flexor pollicis longus + the median nerve.
  • Outside, superficial to the retinaculum: the ulnar nerve and artery, the palmar branch of the median nerve, and the palmaris longus tendon.

Hand compartments and intrinsic muscles:

  • Thenar compartment (acts on the thumb): abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis. Nerve: the recurrent branch of the median nerve, except the deep head of FPB, which is the deep branch of the ulnar.
  • Adductor pollicis (transverse and oblique heads): adducts the thumb. Deep ulnar. Not part of the thenar compartment. Test it by holding paper between the thumb and index finger.
  • Hypothenar compartment (acts on the little finger): abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi brevis, opponens digiti minimi. All deep branch of the ulnar.
  • Central compartment: the long flexor tendons and the lumbricals. Lumbricals arise from the FDP tendons, insert into the lateral extensor expansion, and flex the MCP while extending the IP joints. The lateral two are median, the medial two are deep ulnar (matching the FDP nerve split).
  • Interosseous compartment: palmar interossei (3) ADduct (PAD) toward the middle finger, and dorsal interossei (4) ABduct (DAB). All deep ulnar. Test dorsal by spreading the fingers against resistance, and palmar by gripping paper between the fingers.

Digital sheaths: fibrous digital sheaths are osseofibrous canals that bind the tendons to the phalanges and act as pulleys, preventing bowstringing. Synovial sheaths lubricate the tendons. An infected sheath (tenosynovitis) gives a swollen finger that hurts to move. It is usually confined to that digit but can spread to the palm and forearm.

Extrinsic recap: FDS splits and inserts on the middle phalanges, and FDP passes through it to the distal phalanges. FDP is the only DIP flexor.

Objective 1: arteries of the hand

  • Superficial palmar arch: mainly the ulnar artery (entering through Guyon's canal, superficial to the flexor retinaculum), completed by the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery. It lies just deep to the palmar aponeurosis and gives the common → proper palmar digital arteries.
  • Deep palmar arch: mainly the radial artery, which crosses the snuffbox and dives between the heads of the 1st dorsal interosseous, completed by the deep palmar branch of the ulnar. It lies across the metacarpal bases and gives the palmar metacarpal arteries.
  • The radial artery also gives the princeps pollicis (thumb) and radialis indicis (lateral index finger) arteries.

⚠ Objective 2: carpal tunnel syndrome (not on the Part 1 slides; check against Part 2)

  • Mechanism: compression of the median nerve inside the crowded carpal tunnel (nine tendons plus the nerve under the flexor retinaculum). Acutely, a displaced distal radius fracture or a dislocated lunate can do it.
  • Features: numbness and tingling in the lateral 3½ digits, classically worse at night. The central palm is spared, because the palmar branch of the median nerve passes superficial to the retinaculum. This distinguishes it from pronator syndrome, where the palm is numb too. Late signs are thenar weakness and wasting (recurrent branch). Tinel's and Phalen's signs are positive, and nerve conduction studies confirm it.
  • Management: education, night splinting in neutral, activity modification, steroid injection, and surgical carpal tunnel release for persistent symptoms or weakness. Acute carpal tunnel syndrome after a wrist fracture needs urgent decompression.

Bottom line: distal radius X-ray = crooked (volar tilt about 12°), short (ulnar variance vs the other side), smooth (step over 1 mm = arthrosis). Get analgesia, traction, exaggerate then correct, and use a three-point mould. Treatment is forgiving at the age extremes. Snuffbox tenderness with a normal X-ray = treat as a scaphoid fracture and re-image, because the blood supply is retrograde. Cast undisplaced or distal fractures and fix displaced or proximal ones. The carpal tunnel holds nine tendons plus the median nerve. Thenar = recurrent median, almost everything else intrinsic = deep ulnar. PAD/DAB.
