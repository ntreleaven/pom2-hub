Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module the learner will be able to:

  1. Identify the important functional anatomy of the lower extremity, as it pertains to clinical presentations of leg pathology.
    • Identify the bony landmarks of the lower extremity and relate these to their appearances on plain radiographs.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: naming the landmark precisely is how you tell orthopedics where a fracture is. Use the same systematic read on every film.

A systematic read

  • Skeletal maturity: is the physis open (a lucent line) or fused?
  • Bone: cortex and medullary cavity, density, generalized bone disease
  • Alignment: malalignment suggests fracture, dislocation or other injury
  • Joints: inflammation, narrowing, degenerative change
  • Soft tissues: abnormalities can be extrinsic to bone

Parts of a long bone: epiphysis, physis, metaphysis, diaphysis; cortex and medullary cavity.

TIMS-C for pathology: Trauma and tumours; Infection, inflammatory, idiopathic; Metabolic and malignancy; Systemic (leukemia, lymphoma) and senile (osteoarthritis); Congenital (mostly immature skeletons).

Osteoarthritis at any joint has three features: joint space narrowing, marginal osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis. Look for all three; one may dominate.

Hip

Standard views: AP hip (one joint), cross-table lateral, AP pelvis (both hips and the pelvis). The frog-leg view is used in children to assess the femoral head epiphysis.

AP hip landmarks: femoral head and fovea, femoral neck, greater and lesser trochanters, intertrochanteric region. Acetabular roof, quadrilateral plate, iliopectineal line, teardrop, ilioischial line. Superior and inferior pubic rami, pubic symphysis, ischial tuberosity.

AP pelvis: iliac wings and crest, ASIS, AIIS, sacroiliac joints, pubic symphysis.

Three alignment lines on AP pelvis:

  • Pelvic ring: should be continuous
  • Obturator ring: complete outline on each side
  • Shenton's line: smooth curve from the inferior femoral neck to the inferior superior pubic ramus

Example: a step-off in Shenton's line supports a femoral neck fracture. Total hip arthroplasty shows an acetabular cup, femoral head, neck and stem.

Knee

Routine series: AP (with or without weight bearing), lateral, skyline for the patella. Weight-bearing AP often includes both knees for comparison.

AP: femoral condyles, intercondylar fossa, adductor tubercle, popliteal groove; tibial plateaus (the preferred radiology term), tibial spines, intercondylar eminence; fibular head, neck and shaft. The joint spaces are radiolucent cartilage, which isn't directly visible. A line along the lateral distal femur should pass within 5 mm of the lateral proximal tibia.

Lateral:

  • Blumensaat's line is the roof of the intercondylar notch; the ACL runs parallel to this region
  • Medial plateau is flat; lateral plateau is slightly convex and extends more posteriorly
  • Tibial tuberosity, where the patellar tendon attaches
  • Soft tissues: quadriceps and patellar tendons, suprapatellar fat pads, Hoffa's fat pad

Effusion: fluid distends the normally collapsed suprapatellar recess and displaces the fat pads.

Lipohemarthrosis: a fat-fluid level. In trauma, it points to a subtle intra-articular fracture, because marrow fat escapes into the joint.

Skyline: medial and lateral patellar facets, median ridge, trochlear groove. Soft tissue is thicker medially, which helps with orientation.

Immature knee: look for uniform growth plates; displacement, fragmentation or irregularity may be pathology.

Don't call it a fracture: a bipartite patella is a well-defined fragment, most often superolateral. Patellar fractures more often involve the poles or mid-body.

Post-op: total knee arthroplasty; ACL reconstruction shows bone tunnels, which shouldn't be widened, and the tibial tunnel shouldn't lie in the anterior third.

Ankle

Standard views: AP, mortise (ankle rotated slightly inward, showing the joint space and distal tibiofibular joint), lateral.

The mortise is formed by: medial malleolus, tibial plafond and lateral malleolus, with the talar dome inside. The fibular notch forms the inferior tibiofibular syndesmosis.

Lateral: talus (head, neck, body), calcaneus (tuberosity, anterior process), subtalar joint, navicular, cuboid, Achilles tendon and Kager's fat pad (a triangular lucency anterior to it).

Immature skeleton: an 11-year-old has open lucent physes and a visible calcaneal apophysis; a 17-year-old has fused plates with faint sclerotic lines.

Pathology examples:

  • Transverse medial and oblique lateral malleolar fractures; weight-bearing views may test the mortise under stress
  • Calcaneal spur: a projection from the plantar calcaneus
  • Enthesophyte: new bone where a tendon attaches, for example the Achilles. An osteophyte forms at a joint margin.
  • Achilles tendinopathy: a thick tendon encroaching on Kager's fat pad

Take-home: read systematically, check the alignment lines, and look at the soft tissues for the clue to a hidden fracture.
