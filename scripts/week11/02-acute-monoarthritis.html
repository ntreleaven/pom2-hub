Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

  1. Identify MSK infections and develop an approach to management of MSK infections
    • Determine a differential diagnosis for an acutely swollen joint/soft tissue
    • List the common pathogens for septic arthritis and outline an appropriate management plan (including investigations and treatment) for suspected septic arthritis
  2. Create a plan to evaluate and treat a patient presenting with monoarthritis
    • Define and distinguish between mono, oligo and poly arthritis
    • Determine a differential diagnosis for acute and chronic monoarthritis
    • Distinguish between the two most common forms of non-infectious acute monoarthritis (gout and pseudogout) in terms of presentation, risk factors, investigations/diagnosis and treatment plans.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: a hot red joint looks the same whether it is infected or full of crystals. Infection destroys a joint quickly, so the whole approach is built around not missing it.

Definitions

  • Acute: under 6 weeks. Arthritis: an inflamed joint. Acute monoarthritis is one inflamed joint for under 6 weeks.
  • ⚠ From the Polyarthritis slides: mono = 1 joint, oligo = 2–3, poly = 4 or more.

The differential

Acute hot red monoarthritis: infection, infection, infection, gout, pseudogout, and "oh, did I mention infection?" Rarer: reactive and psoriatic arthritis.

⚠ From the Osteoarthritis slides, chronic monoarthritis: osteoarthritis first, then chronic crystal arthritis. Less often: autoimmune arthritis, Charcot joint, chronic infection (atypical mycobacteria), neoplasm, avascular necrosis.

The one rule: if the joint is red, aspirate it

Send the fluid for the 3 C's:

  • Cell count (a couple of hours)
  • Culture and sensitivity (24–48 hours), plus Gram stain
  • Crystals (a couple of hours)

"To inject is human, to aspirate divine."

Septic arthritis: Mr. Williams

A 62-year-old man with diabetes and a right knee replacement has a hot, red, very painful left ankle, temperature 38.6 °C, after a recent pedicure. Fluid: 59 × 10⁹ cells/L, no crystals, Gram-positive cocci in clusters.

A medical emergency: without rapid treatment it causes fulminant joint damage.

Systemic risk factors: immunosuppression, diabetes, cancer, certain drugs (methotrexate, leflunomide, prednisone, anti-TNF, other biologics), very young or old age, alcohol excess, IV drugs, recent infection.

Local risk factors: prosthetic joint, recent joint surgery, underlying joint abnormality, penetrating trauma, skin infection.

Spread: hematogenous (#1), direct inoculation, or from a contiguous site such as infected bone.

Organisms:

  • Gram-positive cocci, 80%: staphylococcus (60+%), streptococcus (20%)
  • Gram-negative, 15%: immune compromise, elderly, neonates, IV drug users
  • Anaerobes

Treatment: admit, urgent ortho consult, I&D in the OR (a closed-space infection usually needs drainage), empiric IV antibiotics (cefotaxime in this case), analgesia, rest. Repeat I&D until the joint is clean, and IV antibiotics for 4–6 weeks.

Gout: Mike's toe

A 48-year-old brewery worker on hydrochlorothiazide, two days after cholecystectomy, has severe pain, swelling and redness of the first MTP. Fluid: 15 × 10⁹ cells/L, no growth, needle-shaped, negatively birefringent crystals.

The sink analogy: uric acid production equals excretion at steady state. Under-excretion is the most common cause of hyperuricemia (the clogged drain). An acute attack is the sink overflowing.

Who: first attack in men aged 35–50; in women after menopause, since estrogen protects uric acid excretion.

Attack: rapid and intense over hours; can't stand the bedsheets; swollen, warm, red; may have fever. It can look just like an infected joint. Usually a single lower-limb joint; first MTP in 50%. Untreated, it resolves in 7–10 days.

Risk factors: impaired renal function, diuretics (furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide), excess alcohol, family history, male sex. Associations ("a disease of plenty"): obesity, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia.

Triggers, SMARTS: Surgery, Mechanical injury, Alcohol, Recent illness, Travel or dehydration, Start or stop allopurinol.

Diagnosis: must aspirate and find needle-shaped, negatively birefringent urate crystals.

Bloods: CBC (raised WBC, reactive thrombocytosis), creatinine, uric acid (may be normal during an attack), fasting glucose and lipids.

Hyperuricemia can't diagnose gout, and isolated hyperuricemia is not treated.

Acute treatment ("mop up the water first"):

  • Rest, ice, elevation; hydration; reduce alcohol; less meat and seafood, more vegetables and low-fat dairy
  • Intra-articular corticosteroid
  • Oral NSAID or COXIB (indomethacin 50 mg PO TID)
  • Oral colchicine 0.6 mg PO q8h
  • Oral prednisone 30 mg daily for 2 days, then reduce by 5 mg every 2 days

Recurrence: 60% at 1 year, 80% at 2 years, 90% at 5 years.

When to consider allopurinol:

  • Recurrent attacks affecting lifestyle
  • Patients at risk from acute treatments
  • Patient accepts lifelong medication
  • Tophi

Allopurinol rules: it inhibits xanthine oxidase ("turns off the tap"). Do not start during an acute attack. Use NSAID or colchicine prophylaxis. Aim for the lower limit of the lab reference range.

Chronic gout: years of hyperuricemia deposit urate in skin and bone as tophi; urate-lowering therapy is indicated.

Pseudogout: Mary's knee

A 78-year-old woman with mild chronic knee pain develops a very swollen, warm, mildly red right knee after a brisk walk. Fluid: 18 × 10⁹ cells/L, no growth, rhomboid intracellular positively birefringent crystals.

Who: older people, often with osteoarthritis. Where: usually one lower-limb joint, most often the knee. Attack: less intense and slower to peak than gout. Untreated, it lasts several days to a few weeks.

Pseudogout vs chondrocalcinosis: pseudogout is the acute arthritis caused by calcium pyrophosphate crystals in the joint. Chondrocalcinosis is the X-ray finding of calcium pyrophosphate in cartilage.

Treatment: rest, ice, elevation, hydration; intra-articular steroid, NSAID or COXIB, or the same prednisone taper.

Typical joint First MTP Knee

Take-home: must rule out infection, must aspirate, and send the 3 C's.
