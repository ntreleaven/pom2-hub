  2. Create a differential diagnosis and appropriate management plan for patients presenting with upper extremity complaints (numbness, weakness, pain, limitations of range of motion).

This module covers the nerve problems at the elbow, with ulnar neuropathy at the elbow as the main condition.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: the forearm is where the three big nerves get pinched, each at predictable spots. It's also where you take a pulse, draw blood and test root levels. If you know the course of each nerve, you can localize a lesion from the pattern of weakness and numbness.

Objective 1: arteries and veins of the forearm

The brachial artery divides in the cubital fossa:

  • Radial artery: runs down the lateral forearm deep to brachioradialis, then wraps laterally around the wrist. Its proximal branch, the recurrent radial artery, joins the elbow network. Radial pulse: between the flexor carpi radialis and abductor pollicis longus tendons, and in the anatomical snuffbox.
  • Ulnar artery: runs deep to pronator teres and enters the wrist beside the pisiform. Branches: the anterior and posterior ulnar recurrent arteries (elbow network) and the common interosseous, which splits into the anterior interosseous (with the anterior interosseous nerve) and the posterior interosseous (into the posterior compartment).

Collateral circulation around the elbow: the recurrent arteries join the collateral arteries from above. If the brachial artery is clamped or occluded below the profunda brachii, the forearm still gets enough blood.

Veins: the dorsal venous network of the hand drains into the cephalic (lateral) and basilic (medial) veins, joined across the cubital fossa by the median cubital. Deep veins are paired with the arteries, and perforating veins connect the superficial and deep systems.

The cubital fossa:

  • Borders: brachioradialis (lateral), pronator teres (medial), and a line through the epicondyles (top).
  • Contents, lateral to medial: biceps tendon → brachial artery → median nerve. The notes add the radial nerve at the lateral edge.
  • Superficial: median cubital, cephalic and basilic veins, and the medial and lateral cutaneous nerves of the forearm.
  • Clinical significance: the median cubital vein for blood draws and IVs, and the brachial artery for blood pressure.

Objective 1: the three nerves and where they get hurt

Median nerve (C5–T1).

  • Course: through the cubital fossa (superficial here, deep to the bicipital aponeurosis), between the two heads of pronator teres, between FDS and FDP, then under the flexor retinaculum.
  • It gives the anterior interosseous nerve just below the cubital fossa. Together they supply all anterior forearm muscles except FCU and the medial half of FDP.
  • Sites at risk:
    • cut in the cubital fossa
    • pronator syndrome: compression between the two heads of pronator teres, usually from muscle bulk built by elbow overuse. Proximal anterior forearm pain and tenderness, and altered sensation over the palmar lateral 3½ digits AND the adjacent palm.
    • carpal tunnel syndrome, under the flexor retinaculum

Ulnar nerve (C7, 8, T1 on these slides).

  • Course: wraps behind the medial epicondyle (the "not-so-funny bone"), passes between the two heads of FCU, runs between FCU and FDP, and enters the hand through the ulnar (Guyon's) canal.
  • In the forearm it supplies only FCU + the medial half of FDP: the "1½ muscles".
  • Sites at risk: behind the medial epicondyle · between the heads of FCU · Guyon's canal.
  • Guyon's canal is formed by the pisiform, the hook of hamate and the overlying ligament, and carries the ulnar nerve and artery. Compression ("ulnar canal syndrome", "handlebar neuropathy" in cyclists leaning on the hook of hamate) gives hypoesthesia of the medial 1½ digits and weak intrinsic hand muscles.

Radial nerve (C5–T1).

  • In the cubital fossa, between brachialis and brachioradialis, it splits into two branches:
    • Superficial (dorsal sensory) branch: sensory only, to the dorsum of the hand lateral to the 4th digit. It crosses the roof of the snuffbox.
    • Deep branch: pierces supinator and becomes the posterior interosseous nerve, supplying the posterior compartment (brachioradialis, ECRL, ECRB, supinator, extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi, ECU, APL, EPB, EPL, extensor indicis).
  • Sites at risk: where it pierces supinator, and in the snuffbox.
  • The posterior cutaneous nerve of the forearm (from the radial nerve) supplies the skin of the back of the forearm.

Sensory supply of the hand: superficial radial (dorsolateral) · dorsal branch of the ulnar (dorsomedial) · median (palmar lateral 3½ digits).

Objective 1: testing root levels (myotomes and dermatomes)

The level of a cord injury or root impingement can be found from which movements are weak (myotomes) and which key skin patch is numb (dermatomes):

Root Myotome Dermatome
C5 arm abduction, forearm flexion lateral elbow
C6 forearm flexion, wrist extension tip of the thumb
C7 forearm extension, wrist flexion, finger extension tip of the middle finger
C8 finger flexion tip of the little finger
T1 finger abduction and adduction medial elbow

Objective 2: approach to nerve problems at the elbow

Use the same framework as the rest of the week (OLDCARS, look, feel, move, specific tests, don't forget the nerves). Ulnar neuritis sits in the medial elbow differential alongside medial epicondylosis and UCL injury.

Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow (UNE), also called cubital tunnel syndrome. The ulnar nerve can also be compressed at the wrist, but less commonly.

Causes of irritation:

  • Direct trauma (hitting the funny bone)
  • Repeated pressure (leaning on a desk)
  • Elbow flexion tensions the nerve (sleeping with the elbow bent)
  • Subluxation of the nerve with flexion
  • Ligament hypertrophy, or the bulk of medial epicondylosis

Clinical features:

  • Medial elbow pain
  • Numbness and tingling into the ring and small finger: the fingers "fall asleep"
  • Positive Tinel's sign at the entrapment site: tapping over the nerve sends pins and needles down its distribution
  • Check for sensory loss and motor deficits

Motor and sensory pattern:

  • Sensory loss should be in the ulnar distribution. That is not the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm territory and not a C8 dermatome: medial forearm numbness points to the medial cord or the C8 root, not the ulnar nerve.
  • The patient may or may not be weak. If they are, the small muscles of the hand are usually most affected.
  • Remember that tingling can come from nerve, muscle, tendon, ligament or joint, or be referred (think cardiac). True sensory loss is neurogenic.

Management:

  • Rule out red flags. Significant or progressive weakness needs more aggressive, earlier treatment.
  • EMG and nerve conduction studies confirm the diagnosis, show severity, and separate neuritis (irritation) from neuropathy (damage).
  • Education is critical: neuritis is totally reversible, and so is mild neuropathy.
  • Non-pharmacologic: avoid external pressure (don't lean on it, fix the ergonomics) and avoid elbow hyperflexion (a towel splint or elbow pad overnight).
  • Pharmacologic: not much!
  • Surgery: ulnar neurolysis or ulnar nerve transposition.

Bottom line: cubital fossa, lateral to medial = biceps tendon, brachial artery, median nerve (TAN). Median nerve: pronator teres and carpal tunnel. Ulnar nerve: behind the medial epicondyle, FCU, Guyon's canal. Radial nerve: supinator and the snuffbox. For myotomes and dermatomes, C6 = thumb, C7 = middle finger, C8 = little finger, T1 = finger spreading. UNE is medial elbow pain, numb ring and little fingers and a positive Tinel's sign. Stop the pressure and night-time flexion, get an EMG if weak, and operate if it's progressive.
