Week 7 — Clinical Approach to Osteology and Joints of the Proximal Upper Extremity

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the important functional anatomy of the upper extremity as it relates to arm pathology: the distinguishing bony and joint features of the proximal upper extremity, and how these appear on plain radiographs.
  • Build a differential diagnosis and management plan for a patient presenting with upper extremity complaints — numbness, weakness, pain, or reduced range of motion — focused on the shoulder region.
  • Describe the anatomic basis of the common shoulder pathologies: AC joint separation, rotator cuff disease, biceps tendinopathy, glenohumeral arthritis, instability, and adhesive capsulitis.
  • Outline an approach to diagnosis and management (non-pharmacologic, pharmacologic, and surgical) for each of those conditions.

(This module runs in two parts — osteology of the proximal upper extremity, then the joints of the pectoral girdle — and feeds directly into the Clinical Anatomy of the Proximal Upper Extremity lab.)

High-Yield Content

The pectoral girdle is built from four joints, not one. It's easy to think of "the shoulder" as a single joint, but the functional unit is actually four: the sternoclavicular (SC) joint, the acromioclavicular (AC) joint, the scapulothoracic "joint" (not a true synovial joint — just the gliding of the scapula against the thoracic wall), and the glenohumeral (GH) joint itself. Full shoulder range of motion is the coordinated motion of all four together — this coordinated motion is called scapulohumeral rhythm, and it matters clinically because for the muscles crossing the GH joint to work efficiently, their scapular origin has to stay stable, which depends on the other three joints doing their job.

Osteology — the landmarks worth knowing cold:

Clavicle — S-shaped (medial half convex anteriorly, lateral half concave anteriorly), with an enlarged triangular medial end at the SC joint and a flatter lateral end at the AC joint.

Scapula — flat and triangular, essential for overhead motion. The acromion process articulates with the clavicle; the coracoid process is a large anterior projection for muscle attachment (biceps short head, coracobrachialis, pec minor). The glenoid fossa is a shallow socket, with a supraglenoid tubercle above it (origin of the long head of biceps) and an infraglenoid tubercle below (origin of the long head of triceps). The spine of the scapula splits the posterior surface into the supraspinous fossa (holds supraspinatus) and infraspinous fossa (holds infraspinatus). The suprascapular notch, bridged by the transverse scapular ligament into a foramen, is the passage the suprascapular nerve and vessels use to reach the posterior scapula — a notch worth remembering because it's a recognized entrapment site for the suprascapular nerve. The superior and inferior angles give sharp ridges for muscle attachment and landmarking, and the subscapular fossa on the anterior surface houses subscapularis.

Humerus — the head articulates with the glenoid; the greater tubercle (superolateral) and lesser tubercle (superoanterior) are rotator cuff insertion sites, separated by the intertubercular (bicipital) groove, which carries the long head of biceps tendon. The anatomical neck separates the head from the tubercles; the surgical neck is the narrowing just distal to that — the common fracture site, especially in elderly patients after a low-energy fall onto an outstretched hand, and a fracture here risks the axillary nerve. Further down the shaft, the radial (spiral) groove carries the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery posteriorly — a shaft fracture from a direct blow can injure both. At the distal humerus, the medial epicondyle (origin of the common flexor tendon, with the ulnar nerve running directly posterior to it — "funny bone" territory) and lateral epicondyle (origin of the common extensor tendon) flank the capitulum (articulates with the radial head) and trochlea (articulates with the ulna's trochlear notch), with the coronoid fossa and olecranon fossa accommodating the ulna's coronoid and olecranon processes in full flexion and extension respectively.

Radius and ulna, briefly — the radius is the lateral, shorter forearm bone: its head articulates with the humeral capitulum and the ulna's radial notch and pivots within the annular ligament, its neck sits just distal to the head, and the radial tuberosity (medial projection) is the insertion site for biceps brachii. The ulna is the medial, longer stabilizing bone: the olecranon process (posterior, triceps insertion) and coronoid process (anterior, brachialis insertion) flank the trochlear notch that articulates with the humeral trochlea, and the radial notch on the lateral coronoid process articulates with the radial head.

Fracture-to-nerve pairings worth memorizing as a set, since they come up constantly: surgical neck fracture → axillary nerve; midshaft humerus fracture → radial nerve (plus the deep/profunda brachial artery); distal humerus fracture → median nerve; medial epicondyle fracture → ulnar nerve.

The joints, each with a distinct clinical personality:

Sternoclavicular joint — the only true articulation connecting the upper limb to the axial skeleton. Its surrounding ligaments are strong enough that true dislocation is rare; the clavicle itself is more likely to fracture first.

Acromioclavicular joint — a plane (gliding), uniaxial synovial joint between the clavicle's acromial end and the scapula's acromion, with a wedge-shaped intra-articular disc. Stability comes from two ligament groups: the AC ligament (superior reinforcement) and the coracoclavicular ligaments — conoid and trapezoid — which anchor the clavicle to the coracoid process and are what actually let the scapula and free limb hang suspended from the clavicle like a strut. This two-tier ligament anatomy is exactly why AC injuries are graded: an upward force along the humerus (think falling onto the point of the shoulder) first sprains/ruptures the AC ligament alone (a subluxation), and only a more severe blunt force additionally ruptures the coracoclavicular ligaments (a true dislocation) — which presents with the clavicle displaced superiorly and the acromion unusually prominent to palpation, the classic "step-off" deformity.

Glenohumeral joint — ball-and-socket, multiaxial, the most mobile joint in the body, and that mobility is a direct trade-off against stability baked into its anatomy. Four features maximize mobility: poor congruency between the large humeral head and shallow glenoid, multiple surrounding bursae, extensive articular cartilage, and a deliberately lax joint capsule. Counterbalancing that, three things provide stability: the fibrocartilaginous glenoid labrum (deepens the socket and improves fit), the musculotendinous rotator cuff, and the ligamentous support — the glenohumeral ligaments (superior/middle/inferior, thickenings of the anterior capsule), the coracohumeral ligament (coracoid to greater tubercle, reinforcing the capsule superiorly), the coracoacromial ligament (spanning acromion to coracoid, forming the coracoacromial arch that blocks superior humeral head displacement — it's strong enough that a forceful superior thrust would fracture the humerus or clavicle before rupturing the arch), and the transverse humeral ligament bridging the two tubercles over the bicipital groove.

Why dislocation goes anteroinferior, specifically. The coracoacromial arch blocks superior escape, and the joint capsule is comparatively weak anteroinferiorly — so when the glenohumeral joint dislocates, it is overwhelmingly anterior and inferior, and the axillary nerve (which wraps around the surgical neck right at that location) is the structure most at risk during the dislocation itself. This is the direct anatomic explanation for why every shoulder-dislocation workup includes an axillary nerve check (sensation over the "regimental badge" area of the lateral deltoid, plus deltoid contraction) before and after reduction. Management is immobilization followed by rotator-cuff strengthening physiotherapy, since recurrent instability is largely a function of how well the dynamic stabilizers — the cuff muscles — compensate for the joint's inherent laxity.

Impingement and instability as a connected cycle, not two separate diagnoses. Shoulder impingement syndrome happens when the supraspinatus tendon gets compressed in the subacromial space, producing tendinitis. But chronic overuse can also stretch the static stabilizers (capsule, ligaments, labrum), producing mild instability — and that instability forces the rotator cuff to work harder to keep the humeral head centred in the glenoid. Over time the cuff fatigues and weakens, which allows subtle subluxation of the humeral head, which in turn causes secondary impingement of the supraspinatus against the acromion. So impingement and instability aren't always two separate problems to pick between — in a lot of patients, one is actively driving the other, which is exactly why rotator cuff strengthening is the first-line treatment for both.

Innervation of the glenohumeral joint, via Hilton's Law. Recall from the intro-anatomy module: the nerves that supply a joint are the same nerves that supply the muscles acting on it. For the shoulder, that's the suprascapular, axillary, lateral/medial pectoral, musculocutaneous, upper/lower subscapular, thoracodorsal, and radial nerves — the same nerves driving the rotator cuff and the other shoulder-crossing muscles. Blood supply comes mainly from the anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries and the suprascapular artery.

Tying it back to the clinical picture: this bony and ligamentous framework is the "why" behind the exam findings in the companion clinical-approach content — why a fall onto the point of the shoulder gives you a graded AC injury rather than a dislocation, why axillary nerve function gets checked with every shoulder dislocation, and why "impingement" and "instability" so often turn out to be the same patient at two different points along the same vicious cycle.
