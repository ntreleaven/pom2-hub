By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  2. Develop a plan for appropriate investigations of a patient with a possible rheumatologic condition

  3. Propose a plan for treatment of inflammatory/rheumatologic conditions
    • Outline an approach to treating rheumatologic conditions with disease modifying drugs (DMARDs) and important side effects/toxicities of commonly used DMARDs
    • Distinguish biologic drugs from disease modifying drugs and explain the role biologics play in management of rheumatologic disease.
    • Compare and contrast the treatment strategies used to manage inflammatory arthropathies in adults vs in children.

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: JIA is the most common chronic rheumatic disease in children, and its most dangerous feature, uveitis, is usually silent.

Diagnostic criteria

JIA affects about 1 in 1000 children, against about 1% of adults with RA. Most categories are more common in girls, and it is rare before 6 months.

All four are needed:

  • Age of onset under 16
  • Arthritis in one or more joints
  • Duration over 6 weeks
  • Exclusion of other causes

JIA is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion. Bloodwork and imaging are adjuncts.

Inflammatory presentation: morning stiffness, improves with mobility, responds to NSAIDs, subacute or chronic course, altered weight-bearing.

Red flags for another diagnosis:

  • Constitutional symptoms
  • Acute onset
  • Severe pain
  • Inability to walk
  • Night pain disturbing sleep
  • Severe tenderness or redness of joints

Oligoarticular:

  • Under 6, usually 1–3 years; girls 4:1
  • A "painless limp"; a warm, swollen joint that isn't very tender
  • Knees, ankles, wrists, elbows; the hip is extremely uncommon
  • Thirds: stays limited and may remit; 1–2 more joints; or extends to many joints

Polyarticular:

  • Peaks at 2–5 and 10–14; girls 3:1
  • Insidious, 5 or more joints in the first 6 months, stiffness of 60 minutes or more, fatigue
  • RF-negative: younger, fewer joints, asymmetric
  • RF-positive: older, more joints, like adult RA (3–5% of JIA)

Systemic (Still's disease): the most serious and potentially life-threatening subtype.

  • Fever: 39 °C or higher, once or twice daily (quotidian), peaks late afternoon to evening, normal between spikes, child looks unwell only during the fever
  • Rash: evanescent, salmon-coloured, may appear only with fever
  • Arthritis: arthralgia early, effusions later
  • Other: organomegaly, lymphadenopathy, serositis, lethargy, weight loss, growth disturbance
  • Often first mistaken for infection or leukemia
  • Serious complication: macrophage activation syndrome

Enthesitis-related: boys, often over 10; sacroiliitis, dactylitis, enthesopathy; HLA-B27 in 50%; 30% go on to AS; family history of AS, IBD, psoriasis, uveitis or reactive arthritis. Its uveitis is symptomatic (pain, photophobia, decreased vision).

Psoriatic: biphasic (young and preteen), family history of psoriasis, onycholysis, nail pitting, dactylitis.

Undifferentiated: meets none, or two or more, of the categories.

Complications and extra-articular features

MSK: muscle atrophy, contracture, joint damage, leg length discrepancy. Growth: slowed during active disease; limb shortening or lengthening; micrognathia.

Anterior uveitis is the most common extra-articular manifestation.

  • Typically asymptomatic, except in ERA
  • Risk factors: ANA positivity, female sex, younger age, shorter disease duration
  • Usually develops in the first 4 years and doesn't parallel joint disease
  • Regular slit lamp exams; interval depends on subtype, age, duration and ANA status
  • Complications: synechiae, glaucoma, cataract, band keratopathy, cystoid macular edema, blindness

Examination and investigations

pGALS (gait, arms, legs, spine) is a screening exam for: a child with muscle, joint or bone pain; an unwell child with fever; a limp; motor delay or regression; a chronic disease with MSK involvement.

Test Role in JIA
CBC Leukocytosis, thrombocytosis (sJIA); normocytic or microcytic anemia
ESR, CRP May be normal or elevated
ANA Prognosticates uveitis risk; does NOT diagnose JIA
RF Classifies and prognosticates polyarticular JIA
HLA-B27 Associated with ERA (and reactive and IBD arthritis)

Radiographs: soft tissue swelling, effusion, periarticular osteopenia; erosions are uncommon; advanced maturation then growth arrest. Early epiphyseal closure gives a short limb or small mandible; inflammation in adolescence can delay closure and give a long limb. Use X-ray to rule out other causes such as tumour, especially in a single joint.

Treatment

Short-term aims: relieve discomfort, preserve function, prevent deformity, control inflammation. Long-term aims: limit side effects, promote growth, rehabilitate, psychosocial and educational support. Care is multidisciplinary. Non-drug care: education, physio, OT, nutrition.

  • Oligoarticular: NSAIDs and/or steroid joint injections. If not improving or extended, treat as polyarticular.
  • Polyarticular: NSAIDs, selected injections, oral steroids as a bridge, then methotrexate or leflunomide, then biologics (shared decision with family).
  • Systemic: NSAIDs, systemic steroids, IL-1 blocker (anakinra), IL-6 blocker (tocilizumab).
  • Uveitis: steroid drops, then methotrexate, then infliximab or adalimumab; oral steroids.
  • ERA: exercise, NSAIDs, DMARDs for peripheral disease, biologics (anti-TNF especially for axial disease), topical steroids for uveitis.

Initial primary care management: order preliminary tests, start an NSAID, and refer to rheumatology and ophthalmology/optometry.

Adults vs children

Both use stepwise NSAIDs, steroids, DMARDs and biologics. Children differ in: eye screening, attention to growth and development, a family and school team, and local injections early in oligoarticular disease.

⚠ DMARD toxicity (not on these slides)

Standard teaching to check against the course: methotrexate (liver toxicity, cytopenias, teratogenicity; given with folic acid); leflunomide (liver toxicity, teratogenicity). Biologics target a specific cytokine or cell and carry an infection risk.

The two cases: the 5-year-old girl with many active joints fits polyarticular JIA. The 3-year-old boy with quotidian fever, rash and splenomegaly fits systemic JIA, after excluding infection and leukemia; watch for macrophage activation syndrome.
