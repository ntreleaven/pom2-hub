Week 7 — Axial Spine Radiology

Video script (NotebookLM source)

Learning Objective

Determine a differential diagnosis and appropriate management plan for patients presenting with axial pain: specifically, recognize normal spinal anatomy on plain X-rays and identify common spinal anomalies (fracture, spondylolysis, scoliosis).

High-Yield Content

The systematic approach — ABCS. Every spine film gets read the same way, every time, so nothing gets missed:
- A — Alignment. Check the overall curvature (cervical and lumbar should show mild lordosis; thoracic should show kyphosis) and check that each vertebral segment lines up with its neighbours using the standard spinal alignment lines.
- B — Bones. Trace the cortex of every visible bone. For the spine specifically: vertebral body height (watch for normal regional variation), the pedicles, and the transverse processes.
- C — Cartilage and joints. Cartilage itself isn't radiopaque, so what you're actually looking at is the disc space between vertebrae — its width, and whether there's any unexpected calcification or bony bridging across it. Also check the facet joints.
- S — Soft tissues. Specifically the prevertebral and paraspinal soft tissue stripes — abnormal widening here is a clue to things you can't see directly, like an abscess or a hematoma. Then zoom out and look at everything else on the film (lung fields on a C-spine film, mediastinum on a T-spine film, bowel gas pattern on an L-spine film) — don't let tunnel vision on the spine itself cause you to miss an incidental finding elsewhere.

Regional view sets, and why each exists:
- Cervical spine: AP, lateral, obliques, and an open-mouth (odontoid) view for C1/C2. In trauma, obliques are skipped to avoid moving the neck, and most trauma patients go straight to CT anyway since plain film can miss subtle fractures under overlapping soft tissue. On the AP view, count vertebrae by finding the first rib (marks T1), then counting upward: T1 → C7 → C6, and so on.
- Thoracic spine: same basic principle, more challenging to see individual vertebrae clearly because of overlapping ribs and mediastinal structures.
- Lumbar spine: AP and lateral are standard; dedicated sacral/SI joint views are requested separately if needed. Count vertebrae using the 12th rib as a landmark (the vertebra just below it is L1), remembering that some patients have an extra or a missing lumbar-type vertebra (transitional anatomy). On the lateral view, a key structure is the pars interarticularis — the bone bridge between the superior and inferior articular processes — because a defect here is the anatomic basis of isthmic spondylolisthesis. Oblique views exist specifically to inspect it, using the "Scottie dog" pattern: the transverse process is the nose, the superior articular facet the ear, the inferior articular facet the front leg, and the pars interarticularis the neck. A defect shows up as a lucent line across the dog's neck — the "Scottie dog collar sign."

Bone pathology, organized with the mnemonic TIMSC (a general radiology framework, not spine-specific, but it structures the differential nicely): Trauma/Tumour, Infection/Inflammatory/Idiopathic, Metabolic/Malignancy, Systemic/Senile, Congenital.

Trauma — the highest-yield section for this module. Cervical spine trauma classically follows a few recognizable patterns:
- A Jefferson fracture is a C1 burst-type fracture through both the anterior and posterior arches.
- A hangman's fracture is bilateral pars interarticularis fractures of C2, producing traumatic spondylolisthesis of C2 on C3 — unstable.
- Teardrop fractures come in two flavours with opposite mechanisms: an extension teardrop avulses the anteroinferior corner of a vertebral body from a hyperextension force; a flexion teardrop fracture is a more serious hyperflexion injury of the anterior vertebral body, often with posterior ligament and cord injury.
- Hyperflexion injuries can also be purely ligamentous — look for abnormal widening between adjacent spinous processes (interspinous ligament disruption) even with no visible fracture.
- Facet dislocation shows up as "perched" or incongruent facets on the lateral view, with associated spondylolisthesis — unilateral dislocation produces less slippage than bilateral. On the AP view, the spinous processes should be evenly spaced and midline; asymmetric spacing or lateral displacement is a clue to facet injury even without a clear lateral view.
- Stability, in one sentence: fractures confined to the anterior vertebral body tend to be stable; once a fracture involves the posterior elements, it's unstable — that distinction is what radiology reports are actually flagging when they comment on posterior element involvement.
- Dens fractures are classified by location: Type I (tip), Type II (base/waist — the most common and most often requiring fixation), Type III (extends into the C2 body).

Non-traumatic pathology, the other half of the module:
- Cervical spondylosis (osteoarthritis) — the most common non-traumatic finding: straightened cervical lordosis (often from muscle spasm), disc-space narrowing, anterior marginal osteophytes, and endplate sclerosis. Same pattern of findings you'd expect from osteoarthritis anywhere else in the body.
- Retropharyngeal abscess — a soft-tissue read, not a bone one: the prevertebral soft tissue at C5/6 shouldn't be wider than the vertebral body itself: widening (sometimes with small gas locules suggesting anaerobic infection) is the tell.
- Isthmic spondylolisthesis — anterior slippage of one vertebra on the one below it, due to a bilateral pars interarticularis defect; graded by the amount of slippage in millimetres, and the pars defect itself is the "collar" on the Scottie dog view described above. (Degenerative spondylolisthesis, by contrast, happens without a pars defect and is more an older-adult, facet/ligament-laxity phenomenon.)
- Ankylosing spondylitis (an inflammatory, seronegative spondyloarthropathy) — late-stage findings include ossification of the anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments and facet joint ankylosis, producing a rigid spine that's then at much higher fracture risk from even minor trauma because the normal shock-absorbing mobility is gone.

The practical thread tying this module to the rest of the week: these are exactly the findings that turn "non-specific back pain" into one of the red-flag diagnoses from the clinical modules — a pars defect explains mechanical-pattern pain in a young athlete, retropharyngeal widening explains a febrile patient with neck pain who looks sicker than expected, and recognizing an unstable fracture pattern is what tells you a patient needs urgent immobilization and neurosurgical involvement rather than reassurance.
