Learning Objectives (from Elentra)

By the end of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Develop an approach to pediatric musculoskeletal pathology
    • Define scoliosis and describe an approach to management (including assessment, appropriate investigations and treatment)
    • Outline an approach to a child with a limp, and specifically explain the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, management and complications of transient synovitis, slipped capital femoral epiphysis and Legg-Calve-Perthes disease
    • Define club foot and outline an appropriate timeline for management
    • Define developmental dysplasia of the hip, list risk factors for this condition, explain physical exam maneuvers to identify it and outline an approach to management
    • Explain the Salter Harris classification of pediatric fractures involving the epiphysis and the potential complications associated with these fractures.
    • Describe the unique fracture types and overuse injuries that may occur in pediatrics

High-Yield Content

Why this matters: children aren't small adults. Their bones bend, their growth plates fail before ligaments do, and a missed injury can affect growth for years.

Children's bones

More porous and pliable, with a thick, active periosteum and a physis that's a site of relative weakness. So there's less ligament injury and dislocation.

Unique fractures: buckle, greenstick, plastic deformation, avulsion.

Healing is seldom a problem. Remodelling is better with at least 2 years of growth remaining, metaphyseal fractures, and deformity in the plane of the joint. It's worse with displaced intra-articular fractures, midshaft angulation or rotation, and out-of-plane deformity.

Treatment: closed reduction (hematoma block, conscious sedation), splint or cast, post-reduction films, timely follow-up.

Apophysis injuries

Avulsion fractures: a burst of contraction pulls off the apophysis in fast-growing bones. Pelvis, 5th metatarsal, medial epicondyle. A 14-year-old sprinter's anterior hip pain fits. Often non-surgical.

Overuse apophysitis:

  • Osgood-Schlatter: tibial tuberosity; ages 8–14 (F), 10–15 (M); clinical diagnosis
  • Sinding-Larsen: distal patella
  • Sever's: calcaneus

Management: benign and self-limiting, usually gone within 2 years; RICE, activity as tolerated, NSAIDs. Other overuse injuries: stress fractures, osteochondritis dissecans.

Salter-Harris fractures

Complications: growth arrest, leg length discrepancy, angular deformity. About 10% develop growth disturbance.

Type Features Treatment and prognosis
I Through the physis; misdiagnosed as a sprain (sprains are rare in kids) Excellent; stable with closed reduction
II Most common; most often distal radius Usually good; closed reduction
III Intra-articular Usually good; anatomic reduction
IV Intra-articular; bony bridge risk; lateral humeral condyle, distal tibia Anatomic reduction
V Compression of the physis; often missed, diagnosed late by growth arrest Refer to ortho

Prognosis factors: age, severity, type (IV and V worse), reduction method, growth plate vascularity, open vs closed.

Three messages: any growth plate injury is serious; follow for at least 1 year, preferably 2; warn the family.

The pediatric elbow

CRITOE, order and approximate age of ossification: Capitellum 1, Radial head 3, Internal (medial) epicondyle 5, Trochlea 7, Olecranon 9, External (lateral) epicondyle 11.

Swelling means a high suspicion of bony injury. Call peds ortho for a very swollen elbow, instability, displaced fracture, or incongruently reduced dislocation.

Developmental dysplasia of the hip

Definition: a spectrum from a shallow acetabulum to perinatal subluxation or dislocation. 1–6 per 1000 births; left more than right.

Risk factors: firstborn, female, breech, family history, oligohydramnios, race (more common in Caucasian and North American Indigenous populations), other MSK problems (torticollis, calcaneovalgus foot, club foot, scoliosis).

Exam:

  • Limited abduction (key finding)
  • Leg length discrepancy, Galeazzi test
  • Barlow: "sign of exit." Adduct with a posterior force; the femoral head slips out.
  • Ortolani: "sign of entry." Lift and abduct; the head slips back in.
  • Both are unreliable after 3–6 months
  • Older child: painless limp, waddling gait

Management, by age: diagnose early. Pavlik harness up to 6 months, then closed reduction with spica cast, then open reduction with or without osteotomy.

Club foot

Congenital talipes equinovarus: ankle in plantarflexion, heel and forefoot in varus. Genetic component; linked with spina bifida and syndromes. Untreated: progressive stiffness and reduced walking.

Ponseti timeline:

  • Weekly manipulation and casting for 4–6 weeks, starting around 3 weeks to 3 months of age
  • Percutaneous tendo-Achilles lengthening
  • Post-op cast for 3 weeks
  • Abduction brace (boots and bars): 3 months full time, then at night for 2–3 years
  • Resistant or recurrent: posteromedial release

Rotation and angulation

Avoid overtreating normal variants. Genu varum is normal to age 2; genu valgum is normal from 3 to 7. In-toeing and out-toeing are extremely common normal variations.

Warning signs: excessive variation, short stature, progressive varum, asymmetry, persistence beyond 7–8 years, associated conditions.

The child with a limp

Causes differ by age: missed DDH, club foot, hip pathology, overuse, accidental and non-accidental trauma, neuromuscular and inflammatory disease.

Warning signs: asymmetry, progression, leg length discrepancy, pain, deformity.

⚠ Not on the slides, standard teaching to check against the course:

  • Transient synovitis: self-limited hip inflammation in a well child, often after a viral illness, ages about 3–8. Treat with rest and NSAIDs. Exclude septic arthritis.
  • Legg-Calvé-Perthes: idiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral head, ages about 4–8, more often boys; painless or mild limp. Observation, or containment and surgery. Complication: early OA.
  • SCFE: the femoral head slips off the neck through the physis, in obese adolescents; hip or knee pain with external rotation on flexion. Non-weight-bearing and urgent surgical pinning. Complications: AVN, OA.

Scoliosis

Definition: a structural lateral curve with rotational deformity. Idiopathic is most common, adolescent onset typical, F>M. Others: neuromuscular (CP, spina bifida, neurofibromatosis), congenital, post-traumatic.

Assessment: rarely painful; the curve or rib hump is noticed incidentally. Do a neurological review and thorough exam; look for dimple, hairy patch, lipoma, hemangioma. Adams forward bend test shows a rib prominence on the convex side and rules out postural scoliosis.

Investigation: radiographs defining location, levels, direction and magnitude ("right thoracic T7–T12, 45 degrees"), and ruling out anomalies, trauma and tumours.

Progression risk: girls, younger physiologic age, pre-menarchal, larger curves, double over single, thoracic over lumbar.

Management: education and monitoring; orthosis (TLSO); surgical fusion with instrumentation depending on curve and progression risk. No drug role.
